IDS: Q-175
FIRMS: Two Sigma
SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Conditional probability & Bayes
DIFFICULTY: easy
TITLE: Sum of 5 before sum of 2
QUESTION:
A pair of fair six-sided dice is rolled over and over. What is the probability that a total of $5$ appears before a total of $2$?
HINT1:
Most rolls give neither $5$ nor $2$ and simply don't matter. What happens if you only look at the rolls that do matter?
HINT2:
Count how many of the $36$ outcomes give a total of $5$ and how many give a total of $2$. Condition on the first roll whose total is either $5$ or $2$.
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{4}{5}$
REFRESH:
- **Conditioning on a relevant event**: if $A$ and $B$ are disjoint events of an i.i.d. trial, the first trial landing in $A\cup B$ lands in $A$ with probability $\dfrac{P(A)}{P(A)+P(B)}$.
EXPLANATION:
The race is decided by the first roll that is either a $5$ or a $2$; all other rolls are wasted time and carry no information. So the answer is just the relative weight of the two totals on a single roll. A total of $5$ comes from $(1,4),(2,3),(3,2),(4,1)$: $4$ outcomes. A total of $2$ only from $(1,1)$: $1$ outcome. The $5$ is four times as likely on every roll, so it wins the race with probability $4/(4+1)$.
SOLUTION: Condition on the deciding roll
On one roll, $P(\text{sum}=5)=\frac{4}{36}$ and $P(\text{sum}=2)=\frac{1}{36}$. Rolls are independent, so the first roll that lands in $\{5,2\}$ is, conditionally on landing there, a $5$ with probability
$$\frac{4/36}{4/36+1/36}=\frac{4}{5}.$$
SOLUTION: First-step recursion
Let $p$ be the required probability. On the first roll: with probability $\frac{4}{36}$ we win, with probability $\frac{1}{36}$ we lose, and with probability $\frac{31}{36}$ nothing happens and the situation restarts. So
$$p=\frac{4}{36}+\frac{31}{36}p\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{5}{36}p=\frac{4}{36}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p=\frac{4}{5}.$$
===END===

SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Counting & combinatorics
DIFFICULTY: medium
TITLE: Range of three dice equals 4
QUESTION:
Three fair six-sided dice are rolled. What is the probability that the difference between the largest and the smallest value shown is exactly $4$?
HINT1:
There are only two possible (smallest, largest) pairs. Count the triples for one of them.
HINT2:
For the pair $(\min,\max)=(1,5)$: all three dice lie in $\{1,\dots,5\}$, at least one shows $1$ and at least one shows $5$. Use inclusion–exclusion on "no $1$" and "no $5$".
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{2}{9}$
REFRESH:
- **Inclusion–exclusion**: $|A\cup B|=|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|$, so the number of outcomes avoiding neither restriction is total $-|A|-|B|+|A\cap B|$.
EXPLANATION:
A range of $4$ on a die with faces $1$ to $6$ means (min, max) is $(1,5)$ or $(2,6)$, and by symmetry the two cases have the same count. For $(1,5)$ the three dice live in a window of $5$ values and must touch both ends of the window. Counting "all in the window" and subtracting "misses the bottom end" and "misses the top end" (adding back "misses both") gives the count for one window; double it and divide by $6^3$.
SOLUTION: Inclusion–exclusion
Fix the window $\{1,\dots,5\}$. Ordered triples inside it: $5^3=125$. Those with no $1$: $4^3=64$. Those with no $5$: $4^3=64$. Those with neither: $3^3=27$. Triples in the window using both $1$ and $5$:
$$125-64-64+27=24.$$
The window $\{2,\dots,6\}$ gives another $24$, and the two sets are disjoint (different minima). So
$$P=\frac{48}{216}=\frac{2}{9}.$$
SOLUTION: Direct case count
With min $1$ and max $5$ the multiset of values is $\{1,5,x\}$.
- $x\in\{2,3,4\}$: three distinct values, $3!=6$ orders each: $18$.
- $x=1$ or $x=5$: multisets $\{1,1,5\}$ and $\{1,5,5\}$, $3$ orders each: $6$.
Total $24$ per window, $48$ overall, $48/216=2/9$.
===END===

TITLE: A die with one face lowered
QUESTION:
A fair die has faces $1,2,3,4,5,6$. One face is chosen uniformly at random and the number written on it is decreased by $1$ (so a $1$ becomes $0$, a $4$ becomes $3$, and so on). The altered die is then rolled. What is the probability that it shows an even number?
HINT1:
Whether the altered face was odd or even changes how many even faces the new die has.
HINT2:
Condition on the parity of the chosen face: lowering an odd number makes it even, lowering an even number makes it odd. ($0$ counts as even.)
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{1}{2}$
REFRESH:
- **Law of total probability**: $P(E)=\sum_i P(E\mid H_i)P(H_i)$ for a partition $(H_i)$.
- $0$ is even.
EXPLANATION:
The original die has $3$ even faces. If an odd face is lowered it turns even, so the die now has $4$ even faces; if an even face is lowered it turns odd, leaving $2$ even faces. The two cases are equally likely, so on average the die still has $3$ even faces out of $6$: the alteration moves the count up or down by one symmetrically.
SOLUTION: Total probability
Let $O$ = "the chosen face was odd", $P(O)=\frac12$.
$$P(\text{even})=P(O)\cdot\frac{4}{6}+P(O^c)\cdot\frac{2}{6}=\frac12\cdot\frac46+\frac12\cdot\frac26=\frac{1}{2}.$$
SOLUTION: Expected number of even faces
Let $K$ be the number of even faces after the change: $K=4$ or $K=2$ with probability $\frac12$ each, so $\mathbb E[K]=3$. Given the die, a roll is even with probability $K/6$, hence $P(\text{even})=\mathbb E[K/6]=3/6=\frac12$.

TITLE: Roll until you match the first roll
QUESTION:
Francisco rolls a fair die and records the result. He then keeps rolling until he gets a number that is greater than or equal to that first result. Let $N$ be the number of rolls he makes after the first one (the last, successful roll included). Find $\mathbb E[N]$.
HINT1:
Once the first roll is known, what kind of random variable is $N$?
HINT2:
If the first roll is $k$, each later roll succeeds independently with probability $\frac{7-k}{6}$, so $N$ is geometric. Average its mean over $k$.
ANSWER:
$\mathbb E[N]=\dfrac{49}{20}=2.45$
REFRESH:
- **Geometric distribution**: the number of independent trials up to and including the first success, with success probability $p$, has mean $1/p$.
- **Tower rule**: $\mathbb E[N]=\mathbb E\big[\mathbb E[N\mid K]\big]$.
EXPLANATION:
Given the first roll $k$, the number of faces $\ge k$ is $7-k$, so every further roll is a success with probability $\frac{7-k}{6}$ and the waiting time has mean $\frac{6}{7-k}$. As $k$ runs through $1,\dots,6$, $7-k$ runs through $6,\dots,1$, so the average of $\frac{6}{7-k}$ is $\frac16\cdot 6\left(1+\frac12+\dots+\frac16\right)$: the harmonic number $H_6$. A bad first roll ($6$) costs on average $6$ extra rolls, which is why the answer is much more than $1$.
SOLUTION: Condition on the first roll
Let $K$ be the first roll. Given $K=k$, $N\sim\text{Geometric}\left(\frac{7-k}{6}\right)$, so $\mathbb E[N\mid K=k]=\frac{6}{7-k}$. Then
$$\mathbb E[N]=\frac16\sum_{k=1}^{6}\frac{6}{7-k}=\sum_{j=1}^{6}\frac1j=1+\frac12+\frac13+\frac14+\frac15+\frac16=\frac{60+30+20+15+12+10}{60}=\frac{147}{60}=\frac{49}{20}.$$
===END===

TITLE: 60-sided die against 40-sided die

A fair $40$-sided die (faces $1$ to $40$) and a fair $60$-sided die (faces $1$ to $60$) are rolled. What is the probability that the $60$-sided die shows a strictly larger number than the $40$-sided die?
HINT1:
Split according to whether the big die lands in the range the small die can reach.
HINT2:
If the $60$-sided die shows $41$ to $60$ it wins for sure. Otherwise both dice are uniform on $\{1,\dots,40\}$: use symmetry and the probability of a tie.
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{79}{120}$
REFRESH:
- **Symmetry**: for two i.i.d. discrete variables, $P(X>Y)=P(Y>X)=\frac{1-P(X=Y)}{2}$.
EXPLANATION:
One third of the time the $60$-sided die lands above $40$, where it cannot lose. The other two thirds of the time both dice are uniform on the same set $\{1,\dots,40\}$, so they are exchangeable: each wins equally often, apart from ties, which happen with probability $\frac1{40}$. So in that case the big die wins with probability $\frac{39}{80}$, a hair under one half.
SOLUTION: Split on the big die
Let $X$ be the $40$-sided roll and $Y$ the $60$-sided roll.
- $P(Y>40)=\frac{20}{60}=\frac13$, and then $Y>X$ surely.
- Given $Y\le 40$, $Y$ is uniform on $\{1,\dots,40\}$, independent of $X$, so $P(Y>X\mid Y\le40)=\frac{1-1/40}{2}=\frac{39}{80}$.
$$P(Y>X)=\frac13+\frac23\cdot\frac{39}{80}=\frac13+\frac{13}{40}=\frac{40+39}{120}=\frac{79}{120}.$$
SOLUTION: Condition on the small die
Given $X=x$, $P(Y>x)=\frac{60-x}{60}$. Averaging over $x$:
$$P(Y>X)=\frac{1}{40}\sum_{x=1}^{40}\frac{60-x}{60}=\frac{60-\bar x}{60},\qquad \bar x=\frac{41}{2},$$
so $P=\frac{60-20.5}{60}=\frac{39.5}{60}=\frac{79}{120}$.

IDS: Q-182
FIRMS: Optiver
SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Games & optimal stopping
DIFFICULTY: hard
TITLE: Roll or take with a d20, 100 moves
QUESTION:
A fair $20$-sided die starts with $1$ on top. You have $100$ moves. Each move is one of:
1. **Roll**: the top face is replaced by a uniform random number from $1$ to $20$.
2. **Take**: add the current top face to your score. The die is not changed, so you can take again on the next move.

Consider threshold strategies: fix $n\in\{1,\dots,20\}$ in advance and roll until the die shows a value $\ge n$. Assume the move on which the threshold is first reached counts both as the roll and as the first take; so if this happens on move $R$, you collect that value on each of the $101-R$ moves $R,R+1,\dots,100$.

Which threshold $n$ maximises the expected total score, and what is that maximal expected score?
HINT1:
Two things depend on $n$: how long you wait before collecting, and how big the value is once you collect. Write each as a function of $n$.
HINT2:
With threshold $n$, each roll succeeds with probability $\frac{21-n}{20}$, so $R$ is geometric with mean $\frac{20}{21-n}$. The value you lock in is uniform on $\{n,\dots,20\}$ with mean $\frac{n+20}{2}$, and it is independent of $R$.
ANSWER:
$n=18$, expected score $\dfrac{5377}{3}\approx 1792.33$
REFRESH:
- **Geometric waiting time**: trials until the first success with probability $p$ has mean $1/p$.
- **Conditioning on success**: given a uniform roll on $\{1,\dots,20\}$ is $\ge n$, it is uniform on $\{n,\dots,20\}$, and this does not depend on how many attempts it took.
EXPLANATION:
The game is really a single decision: once you lock onto a good face you should never roll again (the problem fixes this), so everything is decided by how picky you are. A high threshold locks in a bigger number but costs more moves waiting. The expected score is roughly (value locked in) $\times$ (moves left), and since the locked value and the waiting time are independent, you can multiply their expectations. Waiting is cheap with $100$ moves: even requiring $18$ or more costs only $\frac{20}{3}\approx 6.7$ moves on average, while it raises the per-move value to $19$. Going to $19$ or $20$ doubles or triples the waiting time for a small gain, and that no longer pays.
SOLUTION: Maximise value × remaining moves
Fix $n$ and let $p=\frac{21-n}{20}$. The number of moves $R$ until the first success is geometric with $\mathbb E[R]=\frac1p=\frac{20}{21-n}$, and the locked value $V$ is uniform on $\{n,\dots,20\}$, independent of $R$, with $\mathbb E[V]=\frac{n+20}{2}$. The score is $V\,(101-R)$, so
$$f(n)=\mathbb E[V]\,\big(101-\mathbb E[R]\big)=\frac{n+20}{2}\left(101-\frac{20}{21-n}\right).$$
Values near the top:
$$\begin{aligned}
f(16)&=18\,(101-4)=1746,\\
f(17)&=18.5\,(101-5)=1776,\\
f(18)&=19\left(101-\tfrac{20}{3}\right)=19\cdot\tfrac{283}{3}=\tfrac{5377}{3}\approx1792.33,\\
f(19)&=19.5\,(101-10)=1774.5,\\
f(20)&=20\,(101-20)=1620.
\end{aligned}$$
$f$ increases up to $n=18$ and decreases after (for small $n$ it is far lower, e.g. $f(1)=10.5\cdot100=1050$). So the optimal threshold is $n=18$, giving $\frac{5377}{3}\approx 1792.33$.

Strictly, if the threshold is never reached within $100$ moves the score is $0$ rather than the negative number the formula assigns; with $n=18$ this correction is of order $19\cdot(0.85)^{100}\cdot 5.7\approx 10^{-5}$, so the exact value is $1792.333\ldots+0.00001$, which is $\frac{5377}{3}$ for all practical purposes.
===END===

IDS: Q-183
FIRMS: Squarepoint Capital
SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Markov chains & random walks
DIFFICULTY: hard
TITLE: First total above 100 with a d4
QUESTION:
A fair four-sided die (faces $1,2,3,4$) is rolled repeatedly and the results are added to a running total that starts at $0$. What is the expected value of the first running total that is strictly greater than $100$?

Follow-up in the same question: what is this expected value if the running total starts at $96$ instead?
HINT1:
The first total above $100$ is one of $101,102,103,104$. They are not equally likely: think about how the walk can land on each of them.
HINT2:
Far from the start, the walk lands on any given integer with probability $1/\mathbb E[\text{roll}]=\frac{2}{5}$. The first total above $100$ equals $100+j$ when the walk visits $100+j-k$ and then rolls $k$, for some $k\ge j$. For the start at $96$, just do backward recursion from $100$ down to $96$.
ANSWER:
From $0$: $102$ (to within $10^{-19}$). From $96$: $\dfrac{52277}{512}\approx 102.10$.
REFRESH:
- **Renewal theorem (lattice case)**: for a random walk with i.i.d. positive integer steps of mean $\mu$ (and gcd of possible steps $1$), the probability that the walk visits integer $m$ tends to $1/\mu$ as $m\to\infty$ (here exponentially fast).
- **First-step analysis**: $f(s)=\frac14\sum_{k=1}^4 f(s+k)$ for $s\le 100$, with $f(s)=s$ for $s>100$.
EXPLANATION:
The naive answer "uniform on $101..104$, so $102.5$" is wrong: a big roll is more likely to be the one that jumps over a line (the inspection paradox), so the overshoot is biased towards small values. Landing on $101$ happens from $100$, $99$, $98$ or $97$ (four ways), while landing on $104$ only happens from $100$ with a $4$ (one way). Starting at $0$, the walk has long forgotten its start when it reaches $100$, so each of $97,\dots,100$ is visited with probability essentially $\frac{1}{2.5}=\frac25$. That makes the overshoot probabilities proportional to $4:3:2:1$, whose mean position is $102$.

Starting at $96$ the walk has not had time to "mix", so the exact answer must be computed by a short backward recursion, and it comes out slightly above $102$.
SOLUTION: Renewal / overshoot distribution (start at 0)
Let $u_m=P(\text{walk visits } m)$. With mean step $\mu=\frac{1+2+3+4}{4}=\frac52$, $u_m\to\frac25$ geometrically fast; at $m\approx 100$ the error is below $10^{-19}$.

The first total above $100$ equals $100+j$ ($j=1,\dots,4$) iff for some $k\in\{j,\dots,4\}$ the walk visits $100+j-k\le100$ and the next roll is $k$. These events are disjoint, so
$$P(100+j)=\sum_{k=j}^{4}u_{100+j-k}\cdot\frac14\approx\frac25\cdot\frac{5-j}{4}=\frac{5-j}{10}.$$
So $P(101)=0.4$, $P(102)=0.3$, $P(103)=0.2$, $P(104)=0.1$, and
$$\mathbb E=101(0.4)+102(0.3)+103(0.2)+104(0.1)=40.4+30.6+20.6+10.4=102.$$
(Exact computation by recursion gives $102-1.9\times10^{-20}$.)
SOLUTION: Backward recursion (start at 96)
Let $f(s)$ be the expected first total above $100$ from total $s$. For $s>100$, $f(s)=s$; for $s\le100$, $f(s)=\frac14\sum_{k=1}^4 f(s+k)$.
$$\begin{aligned}
f(100)&=\tfrac14(101+102+103+104)=102.5,\\
f(99)&=\tfrac14(f(100)+101+102+103)=\tfrac{408.5}{4}=102.125,\\
f(98)&=\tfrac14(f(99)+f(100)+101+102)=\tfrac{407.625}{4}=101.90625,\\
f(97)&=\tfrac14(f(98)+f(99)+f(100)+101)=\tfrac{407.53125}{4}=101.8828125,\\
f(96)&=\tfrac14(f(97)+f(98)+f(99)+f(100))=\tfrac{408.4140625}{4}=102.103515625.
\end{aligned}$$
So from $96$ the answer is $\frac{52277}{512}\approx102.10$. The same recursion run all the way down to $0$ gives $102$ to $19$ decimal places, confirming the first method.

Note: the source states $102.5$; that is the value from a start of $100$ (or the naive uniform-overshoot guess). The correct value from $0$ is $102$, and from $96$ it is $\approx102.10$, because larger rolls are more likely to be the ones that cross $100$.

TITLE: 100 dice against 400 coin flips
QUESTION:
Roll a fair six-sided die $100$ times and let $X$ be the sum of the faces. Flip a fair coin $400$ times and let $Y$ be the number of heads. What is $P(X>Y)$?
HINT1:
Compare the means and the standard deviations of $X$ and $Y$.
HINT2:
Look at $D=X-Y$: find its mean and variance, and ask how many standard deviations $0$ is away from the mean. Is the probability exactly $1$?

$P(X>Y)\approx1-7.5\times10^{-15}$: essentially $1$, but not exactly $1$.
REFRESH:
- **Sum of i.i.d. variables**: mean and variance add. One die roll has mean $\frac72$ and variance $\frac{35}{12}$; one fair coin has mean $\frac12$ and variance $\frac14$.
- **Central limit theorem**: a sum of many i.i.d. terms is approximately normal with that mean and variance.
- **Normal tail**: $P(Z>z)\approx\frac{\varphi(z)}{z}$ for large $z$.
EXPLANATION:
$X$ sits around $350$ with standard deviation about $17$; $Y$ sits around $200$ with standard deviation $10$. The gap of $150$ is about $7.6$ standard deviations of $X-Y$, which is astronomically safe: $X>Y$ essentially always. But it is not a certain event: $X=100$ (all ones) and $Y=400$ (all heads) is possible, so the probability is $1$ minus something tiny and positive. The interview point is to see the gap in standard deviations quickly and to not say "exactly $1$".
SOLUTION: Normal approximation
$\mathbb E[X]=100\cdot3.5=350$, $\operatorname{Var}X=100\cdot\frac{35}{12}\approx291.7$; $\mathbb E[Y]=200$, $\operatorname{Var}Y=400\cdot\frac14=100$. With $D=X-Y$ (independent parts):
$$\mathbb E[D]=150,\qquad \operatorname{Var}D\approx391.7,\qquad \sigma_D\approx19.8.$$
$P(X\le Y)=P(D\le0)\approx P\!\left(Z\le-\frac{150}{19.8}\right)=P(Z\le-7.58)\approx\frac{\varphi(7.58)}{7.58}\approx2\times10^{-14}.$
So $P(X>Y)\approx1-10^{-14}$.

An exact convolution of the two distributions gives $P(X\le Y)\approx7.5\times10^{-15}$ (the normal approximation is only rough that far in the tail, but the order of magnitude is right). Since $P(X=100,Y=400)=6^{-100}2^{-400}>0$, the probability is strictly less than $1$.

SLUG: two-fives-before-four-and-six
IDS: Q-209
FIRMS: SIG
SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Markov chains & random walks
DIFFICULTY: hard
TITLE: Two fives, or a four and a six
QUESTION:
Jenny rolls a fair die repeatedly and writes down the results. She stops as soon as either she has seen two $5$s in total (not necessarily in a row), or she has seen both a $4$ and a $6$ (in any order). What is the probability that she stops because of the two $5$s?
HINT1:
Faces $1$, $2$, $3$ never change anything. What happens if you only record $4$, $5$ and $6$?
HINT2:
Restricted to the relevant rolls, each of $4,5,6$ has probability $\frac13$. Track the state (number of $5$s seen, whether one of $\{4,6\}$ has been seen) and condition on the first relevant roll.
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{7}{18}$
REFRESH:
- **First-step analysis**: the probability of absorption in a target set satisfies $h(s)=\sum_{s'}P(s,s')\,h(s')$, with $h=1$ on the target and $0$ on the other absorbing states.
- **Self-loops**: if from state $s$ you stay in $s$ with probability $r$, you can divide the other transition probabilities by $1-r$.
EXPLANATION:
Ignoring $1,2,3$, each relevant roll is $4$, $5$ or $6$ with probability $\frac13$ each. The two stopping conditions race: two $5$s needs two specific hits, while the $4$-and-$6$ condition needs two different hits, but a repeat of the same one ($4$ then $4$) wastes a roll. After one $5$ and one of $\{4,6\}$, the next useful roll is a coin flip between "another $5$" and "the missing one". Working the few states out by hand gives $\frac{7}{18}$, a bit under one half.
SOLUTION: First-step analysis on the relevant rolls
Record only $4,5,6$; each has probability $\frac13$. Let $w(f,s)$ be the probability of stopping with two $5$s, where $f$ = number of $5$s seen, $s$ = whether one of $\{4,6\}$ has been seen.

State $(1,\text{yes})$: next roll is $5$ (win), the missing one of $\{4,6\}$ (lose), or the same one again (stay). So $w(1,\text{yes})=\frac{1/3}{2/3}=\frac12$.

State $(0,\text{yes})$: $5$ goes to $(1,\text{yes})$; the missing one loses; the same one stays. So $w(0,\text{yes})=\frac{\frac13\cdot\frac12}{\frac23}=\frac14$.

State $(1,\text{no})$: $5$ wins; $4$ or $6$ goes to $(1,\text{yes})$. So $w(1,\text{no})=\frac13+\frac23\cdot\frac12=\frac23$.

Start $(0,\text{no})$: $5$ goes to $(1,\text{no})$; $4$ or $6$ goes to $(0,\text{yes})$:
$$w(0,\text{no})=\frac13\cdot\frac23+\frac23\cdot\frac14=\frac29+\frac16=\frac{4+3}{18}=\frac{7}{18}.$$
===END===

TITLE: Race to two tails, alternating turns
QUESTION:
Two players take turns flipping a fair coin, one flip per turn, player A first. Each player's own flips form their own sequence (the other player's flips in between do not count for them). The game ends as soon as one of the players gets two tails in a row in their own sequence. What is the expected total number of flips (both players counted)?
HINT1:
What does the game need to remember? For each player, only whether their last flip was a tail.
HINT2:
Let $x_{ab}$ be the expected number of remaining flips when it is A's turn, with $a=1$ if A's last flip was a tail and $b$ likewise for B. By symmetry, the value when it is B's turn with states $(a,b)$ equals $x_{ba}$. Write four linear equations.
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{34}{5}=6.8$
REFRESH:
- **First-step analysis for expected hitting times**: $m(s)=1+\sum_{s'}P(s,s')\,m(s')$ with $m=0$ on the absorbing states.
- **Waiting time for TT** with one fair coin has mean $6$.
EXPLANATION:
Alone, a player needs on average $6$ of their own flips to see TT. With two players racing, the game stops at whichever clock rings first, but each player flips only every other turn, so the total is not obvious and needs an actual computation. The state is tiny: whose turn it is, and whether each player's last flip was a tail. Using the symmetry "B's turn in state $(a,b)$ looks like A's turn in state $(b,a)$" cuts it to four unknowns, and solving the linear system gives $6.8$.
SOLUTION: Four-state first-step analysis
Let $x_{ab}$ = expected remaining flips with A to move, A's last flip tail iff $a=1$, B's iff $b=1$. When A flips: heads (prob $\frac12$) sets $a=0$; tails either ends the game (if $a=1$) or sets $a=1$. Then it is B's turn, which by symmetry is worth $x$ with the roles swapped. Hence
$$x_{ab}=1+\tfrac12x_{b0}+\tfrac12\,[a=0]\,x_{b1}.$$
$$\begin{aligned}
x_{10}&=1+\tfrac12x_{00},\\
x_{11}&=1+\tfrac12x_{10}=\tfrac32+\tfrac14x_{00},\\
x_{01}&=1+\tfrac12x_{10}+\tfrac12x_{11}=1+\tfrac12+\tfrac14x_{00}+\tfrac34+\tfrac18x_{00}=\tfrac94+\tfrac38x_{00},\\
x_{00}&=1+\tfrac12x_{00}+\tfrac12x_{01}=1+\tfrac12x_{00}+\tfrac98+\tfrac3{16}x_{00}.
\end{aligned}$$
The last line gives $\left(1-\frac12-\frac3{16}\right)x_{00}=\frac{17}{8}$, i.e. $\frac{5}{16}x_{00}=\frac{17}{8}$, so $x_{00}=\frac{34}{5}=6.8$.
SOLUTION: Independent clocks
Let $T_A,T_B$ be i.i.d. copies of the TT waiting time of one player (in own flips). A's $k$-th flip is global flip $2k-1$ and B's is $2k$, so the game length is $G=\min(2T_A-1,\,2T_B)$ and $\mathbb E[G]=\sum_{g\ge0}P(T_A>\tfrac{g+1}{2})\,P(T_B>\tfrac g2)$. With $P(T>n)=F_{n+2}/2^n$ (Fibonacci, $F_1=F_2=1$: sequences of length $n$ without TT), the series sums numerically to $6.8$, confirming the first method.

- $\binom{n}{2}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ counts unordered pairs.

SOLUTION: Count matching pairs
Matching pairs among the $38$ cards: $9\binom42+\binom22=9\cdot6+1=55$. All pairs: $\binom{38}{2}=\frac{38\cdot37}{2}=703$.

SOLUTION: Sequential drawing
Draw one card, then another. The first card is from a full number with probability $\frac{36}{38}$ (then $3$ matches remain among $37$), or from the depleted number with probability $\frac{2}{38}$ (then $1$ match remains):
$$P=\frac{36}{38}\cdot\frac{3}{37}+\frac{2}{38}\cdot\frac{1}{37}=\frac{108+2}{1406}=\frac{110}{1406}=\frac{55}{703}.$$

$a+b=-1$, $ab=-6$, so $a^2+b^2=(a+b)^2-2ab=1+12=13$.

$A^2=\begin{pmatrix}25-14&-10+8\\35-28&-14+16\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}11&-2\\7&2\end{pmatrix}$, and $\operatorname{tr}(A^2)=11+2=13$.
