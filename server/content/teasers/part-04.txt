SLUG: broken-stick-triangle
IDS: G4B-02, H4B-16, R3B-26, R3B-27, R3B-28, R3B-29
SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Continuous & geometric probability
DIFFICULTY: medium
TITLE: Broken stick triangle
QUESTION:
A stick of length 1 is cut at two points $x$ and $y$, chosen independently and uniformly on $[0,1]$. This splits it into three pieces. What is the probability that the three pieces can be assembled into a triangle?
HINT1:
Three lengths form a triangle exactly when each one is shorter than the sum of the other two. Since the lengths add up to 1, what does that say about each piece individually?
HINT2:
The condition is "every piece is shorter than $\tfrac12$". Draw the unit square of $(x,y)$, treat the cases $x<y$ and $x>y$ separately, and write the three pieces in terms of $x$ and $y$. The answer is an area.
ANSWER:
$\tfrac14$
REFRESH:
- **Triangle inequality**: positive lengths $a,b,c$ form a (non-degenerate) triangle iff $a<b+c$, $b<a+c$ and $c<a+b$.
- **Uniform on a square**: if $x,y$ are independent $U[0,1]$, then $(x,y)$ is uniform on the unit square, so $P((x,y)\in A)=\text{area}(A)$.
EXPLANATION:
With total length $1$, the condition $a<b+c$ is the same as $a<1-a$, i.e. $a<\tfrac12$. So the three pieces form a triangle exactly when **no piece is at least half the stick**. That turns the question into a pure geometry question: the pair $(x,y)$ is a uniform point in the unit square, and we just need the area of the set of points for which all three pieces are shorter than $\tfrac12$.

Intuitively, both cuts must fall in different halves of the stick (otherwise the piece on the other side contains a whole half), and moreover they must be less than $\tfrac12$ apart (otherwise the middle piece is too long). Each of these two requirements "costs" a factor $\tfrac12$, giving $\tfrac14$.
SOLUTION: Area in the unit square
Suppose first $x<y$. The pieces are $x$, $y-x$ and $1-y$. Each must be $<\tfrac12$:
$$x<\tfrac12,\qquad y-x<\tfrac12,\qquad 1-y<\tfrac12\iff y>\tfrac12.$$
In the square these three constraints cut out the triangle with vertices $(0,\tfrac12)$, $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, $(\tfrac12,1)$: it is half of the small square $[0,\tfrac12]\times[\tfrac12,1]$, so its area is $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac14=\tfrac18$.

By symmetry (swap the roles of $x$ and $y$), the case $x>y$ gives the triangle with vertices $(\tfrac12,0)$, $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, $(1,\tfrac12)$, also of area $\tfrac18$. The event $x=y$ has probability $0$.

Total favourable area: $\tfrac18+\tfrac18=\tfrac14$. Since the square has area $1$,
$$P(\text{triangle})=\tfrac14.$$
SOLUTION: Complement: one piece too long (circle trick)
The triangle fails iff some piece is $\ge\tfrac12$, and at most one piece can be that long (two such pieces would already use up the whole stick, which has probability $0$). So
$$P(\text{no triangle})=\sum_{i=1}^{3}P(\text{piece } i\ge\tfrac12).$$
Place 3 points uniformly and independently on a circle of circumference 1, and cut the circle at the third point: the other two points are then independent uniform points on a stick of length 1, so the three arcs have exactly the law of our three pieces. By rotational symmetry the three arcs are exchangeable, so each has the same probability of being $\ge\tfrac12$. For the first piece (from $0$ to the first cut) to be $\ge\tfrac12$, both cuts must lie in $[\tfrac12,1]$: probability $(\tfrac12)^2=\tfrac14$. Hence
$$P(\text{no triangle})=3\cdot\tfrac14=\tfrac34,\qquad P(\text{triangle})=\tfrac14.$$
The same argument with $n$ pieces gives $1-\dfrac{n}{2^{n-1}}$ (see the follow-up).
SOLUTION: Barycentric (simplex) picture
Write the pieces as $(a,b,1-a-b)$. The point $(a,b)$ is uniform on the triangle $\{a\ge0,b\ge0,a+b\le1\}$ of area $\tfrac12$ (this is the joint law of the spacings of two uniform points). The triangle condition is $a<\tfrac12$, $b<\tfrac12$, $a+b>\tfrac12$: the "middle" triangle with vertices $(0,\tfrac12)$, $(\tfrac12,0)$, $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, obtained by removing three corner triangles of area $\tfrac18$ each. Its area is $\tfrac12-3\cdot\tfrac18=\tfrac18$, and the probability is $\dfrac{1/8}{1/2}=\tfrac14$.
FOLLOWUP: Break the longer piece
A unit stick is broken at a uniform point. Then the **longer** of the two pieces is broken at a point chosen uniformly along it. What is the probability that the three pieces form a triangle?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$2\ln 2-1\approx 0.386$.

Let $L=\max(X,1-X)$ be the longer piece; since $X\sim U[0,1]$, $L$ is uniform on $[\tfrac12,1]$ (density $2$). The short piece $1-L\le\tfrac12$ is automatically fine. Break the long piece at $U\sim U[0,L]$ into $U$ and $L-U$; we need $U<\tfrac12$ and $L-U<\tfrac12$, i.e. $U\in(L-\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, an interval of length $1-L$. So
$$P(\text{triangle}\mid L)=\frac{1-L}{L},$$
$$P(\text{triangle})=\int_{1/2}^{1}2\,\frac{1-L}{L}\,dL=2\big[\ln L-L\big]_{1/2}^{1}=2\left(-1+\ln 2+\tfrac12\right)=2\ln2-1\approx0.386.$$
Breaking the longer piece helps a lot compared with $\tfrac14$, since it never wastes the second cut on the short piece.
FOLLOWUP: Right-angled triangle
In the broken-stick problem, what is the probability that the three pieces form a right-angled triangle?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$0$. A right angle requires an exact equation $a^2+b^2=c^2$ between the pieces (with $c$ the longest). In the $(x,y)$ square this equation defines a curve, a set of zero area, and $(x,y)$ has a density, so the probability is $0$. (Right triangles exist, but you hit one with probability zero, just as you hit any specific point.)
FOLLOWUP: Broken stick n-gon
Now $n-1$ independent uniform points cut the unit stick into $n$ pieces ($n>3$). What is the probability that the pieces form an $n$-gon?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$1-\dfrac{n}{2^{n-1}}$.

A polygon can be built from lengths summing to $1$ iff every piece is $<\tfrac12$ (the generalised triangle inequality: each side shorter than the sum of the others). At most one piece can be $\ge\tfrac12$, so the events "piece $i$ is too long" are disjoint and
$$P(\text{no polygon})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P(\text{piece } i\ge\tfrac12).$$
Using the circle trick ($n$ uniform points on a circle, cut at one of them), the $n$ pieces are exchangeable, and the first piece is $\ge\tfrac12$ iff all $n-1$ cuts are in $[\tfrac12,1]$, probability $2^{-(n-1)}$. Therefore $P(n\text{-gon})=1-n/2^{n-1}$: $\tfrac14$ for $n=3$, $\tfrac12$ for $n=4$, $\tfrac{11}{16}$ for $n=5$, tending to $1$. (If one reads "$n$ points", there are $n+1$ pieces and the answer is $1-(n+1)/2^{n}$.)

TITLE: Waiting for a Poisson bus
QUESTION:
Buses arrive at a station according to a Poisson process with rate $\lambda=0.1$ per minute (on average one bus every 10 minutes). The buses have been running for a long time, and you arrive at the station at a random time. What is your expected waiting time for the next bus? On average, how many minutes ago did the last bus leave?
HINT1:
What is special about the exponential distribution when you have already waited some time? Can the same property be used looking backwards in time?
HINT2:
Use memorylessness: $P(\tau>s+t\mid\tau>s)=P(\tau>t)$. Also, a Poisson process run backwards in time is again a Poisson process with the same rate. Don't be surprised if the two answers add up to more than 10.
ANSWER:
Expected wait 10 minutes; the last bus left on average 10 minutes ago (so the gap you land in averages 20 minutes).
REFRESH:
- **Exponential law** with rate $\lambda$: density $\lambda e^{-\lambda t}$ on $t\ge0$, $P(\tau>t)=e^{-\lambda t}$, mean $1/\lambda$, variance $1/\lambda^2$.
- **Memorylessness**: $P(\tau>s+t\mid\tau>s)=\dfrac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}}=e^{-\lambda t}=P(\tau>t)$.
- **Poisson process**: inter-arrival times are i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$; the number of arrivals in an interval of length $t$ is $\text{Poisson}(\lambda t)$, independent across disjoint intervals.
EXPLANATION:
The inter-arrival times are exponential with mean $1/\lambda=10$ minutes, and the exponential law has no memory: however long it has been since the last bus, the remaining wait is still exponential with mean 10. So when you arrive at a random time the expected wait is 10 minutes, not 5.

The same is true backwards: arrivals in disjoint time intervals are independent and the process looks the same when time is reversed, so the time since the last bus is also exponential with mean 10.

This looks paradoxical: the interval containing your arrival then has expected length $10+10=20$ minutes, while the average gap between buses is 10. The resolution is the **inspection paradox**: a random arrival time is more likely to fall into a long gap than into a short one (a 30-minute gap "catches" you six times more often than a 5-minute gap). The gaps you observe are length-biased.
SOLUTION: Memorylessness forward and backward
Let $\tau$ be the time from your arrival at $t_0$ until the next bus. Since you arrive independently of the process, the event "no bus in $(t_0,t_0+t]$" has probability $P(N(t)=0)=e^{-\lambda t}$ by the Poisson counting law, whatever happened before $t_0$. So $P(\tau>t)=e^{-\lambda t}$, i.e. $\tau\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ and
$$E[\tau]=\frac1\lambda=10\text{ min}.$$
Similarly, let $\sigma$ be the time since the last bus. The event $\{\sigma>t\}$ is "no arrival in $(t_0-t,t_0]$", which has probability $e^{-\lambda t}$ as long as the process has run for more than $t$ (it has run "for a long time"). So $\sigma\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ and
$$E[\sigma]=10\text{ min}.$$
The two are independent (disjoint intervals), and the gap containing you has length $\sigma+\tau$, a Gamma$(2,\lambda)$ variable with mean 20 minutes.
SOLUTION: Length-biased sampling (renewal argument)
For a general renewal process with i.i.d. gaps $X$, a random arrival falls into a gap with probability proportional to its length, so the gap you land in has the size-biased density $\dfrac{x f(x)}{E[X]}$. Given that gap, your position in it is uniform, so the expected residual wait is half its length:
$$E[\text{wait}]=\frac12\cdot\frac{E[X^2]}{E[X]}=\frac{E[X^2]}{2E[X]}.$$
For $X\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$: $E[X]=\tfrac1\lambda$, $E[X^2]=\tfrac{2}{\lambda^2}$, so $E[\text{wait}]=\dfrac{2/\lambda^2}{2/\lambda}=\dfrac1\lambda=10$ min. By symmetry the expected time since the last bus is the same, 10 min. (For buses exactly every 10 minutes, $E[X^2]=100$ and the formula gives the familiar 5 minutes.)
===END===

SLUG: moments-of-normal-and-mgf
IDS: G4B-04, R3B-67, R3B-68, R3B-69, R3B-70, H4B-07

TITLE: Moments of the normal distribution
QUESTION:
(a) If $X\sim N(0,1)$, what is $E[X^n]$ for $n=1,2,3,4$?

(b) If $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ (mean $\mu$, standard deviation $\sigma$) and $\lambda>0$, compute $E[X^2]$ and $E[e^{\lambda X}]$.

(c) In particular, if $X\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$, what is $E[e^X]$?
HINT1:
Odd moments of a symmetric distribution are easy. For the rest, think of the moment generating function $M(t)=E[e^{tX}]$ and how its derivatives at $0$ relate to moments.
HINT2:
Compute $E[e^{tX}]$ by writing $tx-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$ as a single square plus a constant (complete the square); the remaining integral is that of another normal density. Then differentiate $M$ four times at $t=0$.
ANSWER:
$E[X],E[X^2],E[X^3],E[X^4]=0,1,0,3$; for $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$: $E[X^2]=\mu^2+\sigma^2$ and $E[e^{\lambda X}]=e^{\mu\lambda+\sigma^2\lambda^2/2}$, so $E[e^X]=e^{\sigma^2/2}$ when $\mu=0$.
REFRESH:
- **Normal density**: $f(x)=\dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}$; it integrates to 1 for every $\mu$ and $\sigma>0$.
- **Variance identity**: $\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2$.
- **MGF**: $M(t)=E[e^{tX}]$; if it is finite near $0$, then $M^{(n)}(0)=E[X^n]$.
- **Completing the square**: $x^2-2bx=(x-b)^2-b^2$.
EXPLANATION:
Two ideas do all the work. First, the standard normal density is symmetric about $0$, so every odd moment vanishes: $x^n f(x)$ is an odd function. Second, the exponential kernel of the normal density combines nicely with $e^{tx}$: multiplying $e^{-x^2/2}$ by $e^{tx}$ just shifts the centre of the bell curve to $t$ and leaves an extra factor $e^{t^2/2}$ outside. That gives the MGF in closed form, $M(t)=e^{t^2/2}$, and its Taylor expansion
$$e^{t^2/2}=1+\frac{t^2}{2}+\frac{t^4}{8}+\cdots=\sum_n E[X^n]\frac{t^n}{n!}$$
reads off all the moments at once: $E[X^2]=2!\cdot\tfrac12=1$, $E[X^4]=4!\cdot\tfrac18=3$. The general normal is $\mu+\sigma Z$, which turns $E[e^{\lambda X}]$ into $e^{\lambda\mu}M_Z(\sigma\lambda)$. That quantity, the mean of a lognormal, appears constantly in Black–Scholes.
SOLUTION: Moment generating function
**Standard normal MGF.**
$$M(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{tx}\frac{e^{-x^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dx=e^{t^2/2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-(x-t)^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dx=e^{t^2/2},$$
since $tx-\tfrac{x^2}{2}=-\tfrac{(x-t)^2}{2}+\tfrac{t^2}{2}$ and the last integrand is the $N(t,1)$ density.

**Derivatives.** With $M(t)=e^{t^2/2}$:
$$\begin{aligned}
M'(t)&=t\,e^{t^2/2}, & M'(0)&=0,\\
M''(t)&=(1+t^2)e^{t^2/2}, & M''(0)&=1,\\
M'''(t)&=(3t+t^3)e^{t^2/2}, & M'''(0)&=0,\\
M^{(4)}(t)&=(3+6t^2+t^4)e^{t^2/2}, & M^{(4)}(0)&=3.
\end{aligned}$$
So $E[X]=0$, $E[X^2]=1$, $E[X^3]=0$, $E[X^4]=3$ (mean 0, variance 1, skewness 0, kurtosis 3).

**General normal.** For $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$: $E[X^2]=\operatorname{Var}(X)+(E[X])^2=\sigma^2+\mu^2$. For the exponential moment, complete the square in the exponent:
$$\lambda x-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}=-\frac{\big(x-(\mu+\sigma^2\lambda)\big)^2}{2\sigma^2}+\mu\lambda+\frac{\sigma^2\lambda^2}{2},$$
so
$$E[e^{\lambda X}]=e^{\mu\lambda+\sigma^2\lambda^2/2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu-\sigma^2\lambda)^2/(2\sigma^2)}dx=e^{\mu\lambda+\sigma^2\lambda^2/2},$$
the integrand being the $N(\mu+\sigma^2\lambda,\sigma^2)$ density.

**Special case.** With $\mu=0$ and $\lambda=1$: $E[e^X]=e^{\sigma^2/2}$. Note it is larger than $e^{E[X]}=1$, as Jensen's inequality demands.
SOLUTION: Integration by parts (recursion on moments)
Let $m_n=E[Z^n]$ for $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and $\varphi(x)=e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$. Since $\varphi'(x)=-x\varphi(x)$,
$$m_{n}=\int x^{n-1}\cdot x\varphi(x)\,dx=\Big[-x^{n-1}\varphi(x)\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+(n-1)\int x^{n-2}\varphi(x)\,dx=(n-1)\,m_{n-2}.$$
With $m_0=1$ and $m_1=0$ (odd integrand): $m_2=1\cdot m_0=1$, $m_3=2m_1=0$, $m_4=3m_2=3$. In general $m_{2k}=(2k-1)(2k-3)\cdots3\cdot1$ and the odd moments vanish.
FOLLOWUP: Poisson MGF
Compute the moment generating function of $Y\sim\text{Poisson}(\alpha)$, i.e. $P(Y=k)=\dfrac{e^{-\alpha}\alpha^k}{k!}$, $k=0,1,2,\dots$
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$M_Y(\lambda)=\exp\big(\alpha(e^\lambda-1)\big)$.
$$M_Y(\lambda)=\sum_{k\ge0}e^{\lambda k}\frac{e^{-\alpha}\alpha^k}{k!}=e^{-\alpha}\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(\alpha e^\lambda)^k}{k!}=e^{-\alpha}e^{\alpha e^\lambda}=e^{\alpha(e^\lambda-1)}.$$
Check: $M'(\lambda)=\alpha e^\lambda e^{\alpha(e^\lambda-1)}$ gives $E[Y]=\alpha$, and $M''(\lambda)=(\alpha e^\lambda+\alpha^2e^{2\lambda})e^{\alpha(e^\lambda-1)}$ gives $E[Y^2]=\alpha+\alpha^2$, so $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\alpha$.
FOLLOWUP: Moments from the MGF
Show that if $X$ has moment generating function $M(\lambda)=E[e^{\lambda X}]$, finite for $\lambda$ in a neighbourhood of $0$, then $E[X^n]=\dfrac{d^nM}{d\lambda^n}\Big|_{\lambda=0}$.
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
Formally, differentiate under the expectation: $\dfrac{d^n}{d\lambda^n}e^{\lambda X}=X^ne^{\lambda X}$, so $M^{(n)}(\lambda)=E[X^ne^{\lambda X}]$, and at $\lambda=0$ this is $E[X^n]$.

Justification: suppose $M$ is finite on $(-2\delta,2\delta)$. Since $|x|^n\le C_n e^{\delta|x|}$ for some constant $C_n$, for $|\lambda|<\delta$ we get $|X|^ne^{\lambda X}\le C_n e^{2\delta|X|}\le C_n\big(e^{2\delta X}+e^{-2\delta X}\big)$, which is integrable. By dominated convergence we may differentiate under $E$ as many times as we like. Equivalently, $M(\lambda)=E\big[\sum_n \frac{(\lambda X)^n}{n!}\big]=\sum_n E[X^n]\frac{\lambda^n}{n!}$ (the exchange of sum and expectation being justified by $E[e^{|\lambda X|}]<\infty$), so the $n$-th Taylor coefficient of $M$ at $0$ is $E[X^n]/n!$.
FOLLOWUP: Normal MGF via standardisation
If $Y\sim N(0,1)$ then $X=\sigma Y+\mu\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$. Compute the MGF of $Y$ and use it to recover $E[e^{\lambda X}]$.
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$M_Y(\lambda)=e^{\lambda^2/2}$ (complete the square: $\lambda y-\tfrac{y^2}{2}=-\tfrac{(y-\lambda)^2}{2}+\tfrac{\lambda^2}{2}$, and the $N(\lambda,1)$ density integrates to 1). Then
$$E[e^{\lambda X}]=E[e^{\lambda\mu+\lambda\sigma Y}]=e^{\lambda\mu}M_Y(\lambda\sigma)=e^{\lambda\mu}e^{\lambda^2\sigma^2/2}=\exp\!\Big(\mu\lambda+\frac{\sigma^2\lambda^2}{2}\Big),$$
which matches the direct computation.

TITLE: Connecting noodles
QUESTION:
There are 100 noodles in your soup bowl. Blindfolded, you pick two free noodle ends at random (every free end equally likely) and tie them together. You repeat until there are no free ends left. Each time you tie together the two ends of the same strand, a loop (circle) is closed. What is the expected number of loops at the end?
HINT1:
Look at what one tying step does to the number of noodles. Try $n=1$, $n=2$, $n=3$ noodles first.
HINT2:
With $n$ noodles there are $2n$ free ends. After the first tie you always have $n-1$ "noodles" left (one of them may be made of joined pieces), and you gained a loop only if the two ends belonged to the same noodle. Compute that probability and write a recursion for $E_n$.
ANSWER:
$\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{100}\frac{1}{2k-1}=1+\frac13+\frac15+\cdots+\frac1{199}\approx 3.28$
REFRESH:
- **Linearity of expectation**: $E[\sum_i I_i]=\sum_i P(A_i)$ for indicators $I_i=\mathbf 1_{A_i}$, whether or not the events are dependent.
- **Law of total expectation**: $E[X]=\sum_k E[X\mid B_k]P(B_k)$ for a partition $(B_k)$.
- **Harmonic sums**: $H_m=\sum_{k=1}^m\frac1k\approx\ln m+0.5772$.
EXPLANATION:
The key observation is that every tie reduces the number of free strands by exactly one, whatever happens. If you tie two ends of the same noodle, that noodle becomes a loop and leaves the game; if you tie ends of two different noodles, they merge into one longer noodle. Either way, from $n$ noodles you go to $n-1$ noodles, and the process "restarts" with one fewer.

So the whole process is $n$ steps, at noodle counts $n,n-1,\dots,1$, and at the step with $k$ noodles a loop is created with probability $\frac{1}{2k-1}$: once you pick the first end, the second end is one of the $2k-1$ others, and exactly one of them is the other end of the same noodle. The expected number of loops is the sum of these probabilities, a sum of odd reciprocals, which grows like $\tfrac12\ln n$: very slowly.
SOLUTION: Recursion
Let $E_n$ be the expected number of loops starting with $n$ noodles, so $E_1=1$. With $2n$ free ends there are $\binom{2n}{2}=n(2n-1)$ equally likely pairs; $n$ of them are the two ends of one noodle. Hence
$$P(\text{loop at first tie})=\frac{n}{n(2n-1)}=\frac1{2n-1}.$$
In either case we are left with $n-1$ noodles whose free ends are again equally likely to be picked. By total expectation:
$$E_n=\frac1{2n-1}\,(1+E_{n-1})+\frac{2n-2}{2n-1}\,E_{n-1}=E_{n-1}+\frac1{2n-1}.$$
Check: $E_2=1+\tfrac13$, $E_3=1+\tfrac13+\tfrac15$. Unrolling from $E_1=1$:
$$E_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\frac1{2k-1},\qquad E_{100}=1+\frac13+\cdots+\frac1{199}.$$
Numerically, $\sum_{k=1}^{100}\frac1{2k-1}=H_{200}-\tfrac12H_{100}\approx 5.878-2.594\approx3.284$.
SOLUTION: Indicators
Let $I_k=1$ if a loop is closed at the step where $k$ noodles remain ($k=n,n-1,\dots,1$). The number of loops is $\sum_k I_k$, and $P(I_k=1)=\frac1{2k-1}$: fix one of the two chosen ends; the other is uniform among the $2k-1$ remaining free ends, exactly one of which closes a loop. By linearity,
$$E[\#\text{loops}]=\sum_{k=1}^{100}\frac1{2k-1}\approx3.284.$$

CATEGORY: Variance & correlation

TITLE: Optimal hedge ratio
QUESTION:
You just bought one share of stock $A$ and want to hedge it by shorting stock $B$. How many shares of $B$ should you short to minimise the variance of the hedged position? The variance of $A$'s return is $\sigma_A^2$, the variance of $B$'s return is $\sigma_B^2$, and their correlation coefficient is $\rho$.
HINT1:
Write the hedged position as $r_A-h\,r_B$ and expand its variance.
HINT2:
$\operatorname{Var}(r_A-hr_B)$ is a quadratic in $h$ with a positive leading coefficient. Minimise it by setting the derivative in $h$ to zero.
ANSWER:
$h^*=\rho\,\dfrac{\sigma_A}{\sigma_B}$
REFRESH:
- **Variance of a linear combination**: $\operatorname{Var}(X-hY)=\operatorname{Var}X-2h\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)+h^2\operatorname{Var}Y$.
- **Covariance and correlation**: $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\rho\,\sigma_X\sigma_Y$.
- **Minimum of a quadratic** $ah^2+bh+c$ with $a>0$: at $h=-b/(2a)$.
EXPLANATION:
Shorting $B$ removes the part of $A$'s risk that moves together with $B$. The hedged variance is a parabola in $h$: too little hedge leaves the common risk in, too much hedge adds $B$'s own risk back in with the opposite sign. The best $h$ is the regression coefficient of $r_A$ on $r_B$, $\beta=\operatorname{Cov}(r_A,r_B)/\operatorname{Var}(r_B)=\rho\sigma_A/\sigma_B$: you short exactly the "beta" of $A$ with respect to $B$. What remains is the idiosyncratic risk, with variance $\sigma_A^2(1-\rho^2)$.

(Here $h$ is measured in units of $B$ exposure per unit of $A$ exposure; with prices $P_A\ne P_B$ the number of shares is $h\,P_A/P_B$. The book, like most interviewers, implicitly treats one share of each as the same notional.)
SOLUTION: Minimise the quadratic
Short $h$ units of $B$. The hedged return is $r_A-hr_B$, with variance
$$V(h)=\sigma_A^2-2h\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B+h^2\sigma_B^2.$$
First-order condition:
$$V'(h)=-2\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B+2h\sigma_B^2=0\iff h^*=\rho\frac{\sigma_A}{\sigma_B}.$$
Second-order condition: $V''(h)=2\sigma_B^2>0$, so this is the minimum. The minimal variance is
$$V(h^*)=\sigma_A^2-2\rho^2\sigma_A^2+\rho^2\sigma_A^2=\sigma_A^2(1-\rho^2).$$
SOLUTION: Regression / projection view
Decompose $r_A=\beta r_B+\varepsilon$ with $\beta=\dfrac{\operatorname{Cov}(r_A,r_B)}{\operatorname{Var}(r_B)}$, so that $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon,r_B)=0$. Then
$$\operatorname{Var}(r_A-hr_B)=\operatorname{Var}\big((\beta-h)r_B+\varepsilon\big)=(\beta-h)^2\sigma_B^2+\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon),$$
minimised at $h=\beta=\dfrac{\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B}{\sigma_B^2}=\rho\dfrac{\sigma_A}{\sigma_B}$.

TITLE: Dice game: roll again on 4, 5, 6
QUESTION:
You roll a fair die and are paid its face value for every roll. If the roll is 4, 5 or 6, you roll again; as soon as you roll 1, 2 or 3 (and are paid for it), the game stops. What is the expected total payoff?
HINT1:
After a high roll, the game you face is exactly the game you started with.
HINT2:
Condition on the first roll. Given a low roll you get its value and stop; given a high roll you get its value plus a fresh copy of the game. Write an equation for $E$.
ANSWER:
$7$
REFRESH:
- **Law of total expectation**: $E[X]=E\big[E[X\mid Y]\big]=\sum_y E[X\mid Y=y]P(Y=y)$.
- **Geometric law**: the number of trials until the first success with probability $p$ has mean $1/p$.
- **Wald's identity**: if $X_1,X_2,\dots$ are i.i.d. with finite mean and $N$ is a stopping time with $E[N]<\infty$, then $E\big[\sum_{i=1}^N X_i\big]=E[N]\,E[X_1]$.
EXPLANATION:
The game is self-similar: whenever you roll a 4, 5 or 6 you collect that amount and then face exactly the same game again. So its value $E$ satisfies a one-line equation: half the time you get an average of 2 and stop, half the time you get an average of 5 plus another $E$.

Another way to see it: you roll a geometric number of times (on average 2 rolls, since each roll stops the game with probability $\tfrac12$), and each roll pays 3.5 on average. That gives $2\times3.5=7$ directly, and Wald's identity makes this rigorous even though the number of rolls depends on the faces.
SOLUTION: First-step analysis
Let $E$ be the expected payoff and $Y$ the first roll. With probability $\tfrac12$, $Y\in\{1,2,3\}$ and the game ends: $E[X\mid Y\le3]=\tfrac{1+2+3}{3}=2$. With probability $\tfrac12$, $Y\in\{4,5,6\}$: you earn on average $5$ and restart, so $E[X\mid Y\ge4]=5+E$. Hence
$$E=\tfrac12\cdot2+\tfrac12(5+E)\iff \tfrac12E=\tfrac72\iff E=7.$$
SOLUTION: Wald's identity
Let $N$ be the number of rolls. Each roll ends the game with probability $\tfrac12$, independently, so $N$ is geometric and $E[N]=2$. $N$ is a stopping time for the sequence of faces $X_1,X_2,\dots$ (whether we stop after roll $n$ depends only on $X_1,\dots,X_n$), and $E[X_i]=3.5$. The payoff is $\sum_{i=1}^N X_i$, so by Wald
$$E\Big[\sum_{i=1}^N X_i\Big]=E[N]\,E[X_1]=2\times3.5=7.$$
(Sanity check: the last roll averages 2 and the $N-1$ earlier ones average 5 each, $E[N-1]=1$, total $5+2=7$.)

A different game with the same flavour (Heard on the Street) is given as a follow-up below; its answer is $\tfrac23$, not 7.
FOLLOWUP: Paid the count, unless you stop on a 6
You roll a fair die until you get a 4, 5 or 6, which stops the game. Each 1, 2 or 3 rolled before that adds $+1$ to your score. If the game stops on a 4 or a 5 you are paid your accumulated score; if it stops on a 6 you get nothing. What is the expected payoff?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$\tfrac23$.

Let $N$ be the number of 1–3 rolls before the first 4–6. Each roll is low with probability $\tfrac12$, so $P(N=j)=(\tfrac12)^{j+1}$ and $E[N]=\sum_{j\ge0}j(\tfrac12)^{j+1}=1$ (or by first step: $E[N]=\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12(1+E[N])$, so $E[N]=1$). The stopping roll is uniform on $\{4,5,6\}$ and independent of $N$ (it is the first high roll, whatever came before), so you are paid with probability $\tfrac23$ independently of $N$:
$$E[\text{payoff}]=\tfrac23\,E[N]=\tfrac23.$$

- **Indicator trick**: $E[\mathbf 1_A]=P(A)$.

The first ace sits right after all the non-aces that precede it, so its position is $1+(\text{number of non-aces before every ace})$. Now look at one particular non-ace, say the 7 of clubs, together with the four aces: in a random shuffle, these five cards are in a uniformly random relative order, so the 7 of clubs is first among them with probability $\tfrac15$. Equivalently, the 4 aces split the 48 other cards into 5 gaps (before the first ace, between aces, after the last), and by symmetry every gap has the same expected size $48/5$.

Label the non-aces $1,\dots,48$ and let $X_i=1$ if card $i$ appears before all four aces. The position of the first ace is
$$X=1+\sum_{i=1}^{48}X_i.$$
Among card $i$ and the four aces, each of the five is equally likely to come first, so $E[X_i]=P(\text{card } i\text{ before all aces})=\tfrac15$. Therefore
$$E[X]=1+48\cdot\tfrac15=\tfrac{53}{5}=10.6.$$

The vector of gap sizes $(|G_1|,\dots,|G_5|)$ is exchangeable (reversing or permuting the roles of the gaps does not change the law of a uniform shuffle), and the sizes add to 48. So $E|G_j|=\tfrac{48}{5}$ for every $j$, and the first ace is at position $|G_1|+1$, with expectation $\tfrac{48}{5}+1=10.6$.

TITLE: Sum of uniforms at most 1
QUESTION:

HINT2:
Prove the guess by induction, conditioning on $X_{n+1}$: the event $\{S_n\le 1-x\}$ is a simplex scaled by the factor $1-x$ in each of its $n$ dimensions.
ANSWER:
$\dfrac1{n!}$
REFRESH:
- **Probability as volume**: for i.i.d. $U[0,1]$ variables, $P((X_1,\dots,X_n)\in A)=\operatorname{vol}(A\cap[0,1]^n)$.
- **Scaling of volume**: multiplying all $n$ coordinates by $c$ multiplies $n$-dimensional volume by $c^n$.
- **Conditioning on a continuous variable**: $P(A)=\int P(A\mid X=x)f_X(x)\,dx$.
EXPLANATION:
$P(S_n\le1)$ is the volume of the corner simplex $\{x_i\ge0,\ \sum x_i\le1\}$ inside the unit cube: a segment of length 1, a triangle of area $\tfrac12$, a tetrahedron of volume $\tfrac16$, and so on. The pattern $1/n!$ has a beautiful explanation: the map from $(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ to the partial sums $(s_1,s_2,\dots,s_n)=(x_1,x_1+x_2,\dots)$ preserves volume and sends the simplex to the set $\{0\le s_1\le s_2\le\cdots\le s_n\le1\}$. That is one of the $n!$ possible orderings of $n$ points in the cube, and by symmetry all orderings have the same volume, $1/n!$.
SOLUTION: Induction on n
$n=1$: $P(X_1\le1)=1$. $n=2$: the region $x_1+x_2\le1$ in the unit square is a right triangle of area $\tfrac12$. $n=3$: the corner tetrahedron has volume $\int_0^1\tfrac12z^2\,dz=\tfrac16$ (cross-sections at level $z=1-x_3$ are triangles of area $\tfrac12z^2$).

Claim: $P(S_n\le t)=\dfrac{t^n}{n!}$ for $0\le t\le1$. True for $n=1$. If it holds for $n$, then for $t\in[0,1]$, conditioning on $X_{n+1}=x$ (only $x\le t$ contributes, and then $t-x\in[0,1]$):
$$P(S_{n+1}\le t)=\int_0^t P(S_n\le t-x)\,dx=\int_0^t\frac{(t-x)^n}{n!}\,dx=\frac1{n!}\cdot\frac{t^{n+1}}{n+1}=\frac{t^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.$$
Taking $t=1$: $P(S_n\le1)=\dfrac1{n!}$.
SOLUTION: Ordering symmetry
Change variables to partial sums $s_k=x_1+\cdots+x_k$. The map $(x_1,\dots,x_n)\mapsto(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ is linear with a triangular matrix of ones on the diagonal, so its Jacobian is 1 and it preserves volume. It maps $\{x_i\ge0,\ \sum x_i\le1\}$ bijectively onto $\{0\le s_1\le s_2\le\cdots\le s_n\le1\}$.

Now take $n$ i.i.d. uniforms $U_1,\dots,U_n$: the probability that they come out in the order $U_1\le U_2\le\cdots\le U_n$ is $\tfrac1{n!}$, since all $n!$ orderings are equally likely and ties have probability 0. That probability is exactly the volume of the ordered region, so
$$P(S_n\le1)=\operatorname{vol}\{0\le s_1\le\cdots\le s_n\le1\}=\frac1{n!}.$$

TITLE: Coupon collector
QUESTION:
There are $N$ distinct types of coupons in cereal boxes, and each box contains one coupon whose type is, independently of the others, equally likely to be any of the $N$ types.

A. How many boxes does a child need to buy, on average, to collect at least one coupon of every type?

HINT1:
For A, split the collection into stages: the wait for the 1st new type, then the 2nd, and so on. For B, think about each type separately.
HINT2:
A: once $i-1$ types are in hand, each new box brings a new type with probability $\frac{N-i+1}{N}$, so the wait is geometric. B: let $I_j=1$ if type $j$ appears among the $n$ coupons; compute $P(I_j=0)$ and use linearity.

A: $N\left(1+\frac12+\cdots+\frac1N\right)=N H_N$. B: $N\left[1-\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^n\right]$.
REFRESH:
- **Geometric law**: number of trials to the first success, success probability $p$, has mean $\frac1p$.
- **Linearity of expectation** and $E[\mathbf 1_A]=P(A)$.
- **Harmonic number**: $H_N=\sum_{k=1}^N\frac1k\approx\ln N+0.5772$.
EXPLANATION:
Both parts avoid the (hard) distribution of the answer by writing it as a sum.

In A the total waiting time is a sum of stage waits. Early on almost every box is new, so stages are short; at the end, with only one type missing, you wait on average $N$ boxes for it. Adding $\frac{N}{N}+\frac{N}{N-1}+\cdots+\frac{N}{1}$ gives $NH_N\approx N\ln N$, which is why the last few coupons dominate.

In B the number of distinct types is a sum of indicators "type $j$ appeared". A given type is missed by each coupon with probability $1-\frac1N$, so it is missed by all $n$ with probability $(1-\frac1N)^n$; by linearity the expected number of types seen is $N$ times the probability of seeing one given type.
SOLUTION: Stages and indicators
**A.** Let $X_i$ be the number of boxes needed to go from $i-1$ to $i$ distinct types ($i=1,\dots,N$). Given $i-1$ types collected, each new box shows a new type with probability $p_i=\frac{N-i+1}{N}$, independently, so $X_i$ is geometric and $E[X_i]=\frac{N}{N-i+1}$. The total is $X=\sum_{i=1}^N X_i$, so
$$E[X]=\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{N}{N-i+1}=N\Big(\frac1N+\frac1{N-1}+\cdots+\frac11\Big)=NH_N.$$
For example, $N=6$ gives $6\cdot2.45=14.7$ boxes.

**B.** Let $I_j=1$ if at least one of the $n$ coupons is of type $j$. Each coupon avoids type $j$ with probability $\frac{N-1}{N}$, independently, so $P(I_j=0)=\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^n$. The number of distinct types is $Y=\sum_{j=1}^N I_j$, hence
$$E[Y]=\sum_{j=1}^N P(I_j=1)=N\left[1-\left(\frac{N-1}{N}\right)^n\right].$$

At $q=0.3$: $\rho=+\sqrt{\tfrac37}\approx0.655$.

(c) What is the expected range $E[Z_n-Y_n]$?

"The maximum is at most $x$" is the same as "every $X_i$ is at most $x$". What is the analogous statement for the minimum?
HINT2:
Use independence: $P(Z_n\le x)=\prod_i P(X_i\le x)$ and $P(Y_n\ge x)=\prod_i P(X_i\ge x)$. Differentiate to get densities, then integrate $x f(x)$. For the range use linearity.

A good intuition for the answers: $n$ uniform points split $[0,1]$ into $n+1$ gaps, and by symmetry each gap has the same expected length $\frac1{n+1}$. The minimum is the first gap, $\frac1{n+1}$; the maximum is $1$ minus the last gap, $\frac{n}{n+1}$; and the range is everything but the two end gaps, $\frac{n-1}{n+1}$. SOLUTION: CDF, density, expectation

$$F_{Z_n}(x)=P(X_1\le x,\dots,X_n\le x)=x^n,\qquad f_{Z_n}(x)=nx^{n-1},$$
$$E[Z_n]=\int_0^1x\cdot nx^{n-1}\,dx=\frac{n}{n+1}.$$

$$F_{Y_n}(x)=1-(1-x)^n,\qquad f_{Y_n}(x)=n(1-x)^{n-1},$$
$$E[Y_n]=\int_0^1nx(1-x)^{n-1}dx\overset{y=1-x}{=}\int_0^1n(1-y)y^{n-1}dy=1-\frac{n}{n+1}=\frac1{n+1}.$$

SOLUTION: Tail integrals and symmetry
For a variable in $[0,1]$, $E[X]=\int_0^1P(X>x)\,dx$. So
$$E[Z_n]=\int_0^1(1-x^n)\,dx=1-\frac1{n+1}=\frac{n}{n+1},\qquad E[Y_n]=\int_0^1(1-x)^n\,dx=\frac1{n+1}.$$
Alternatively, $1-X_i$ are also i.i.d. uniform and $\min_i X_i=1-\max_i(1-X_i)$, so $E[Y_n]=1-E[Z_n]=\frac1{n+1}$ without further integration.

If $x_1,x_2$ are independent uniform on $[0,1]$, what is the density of $x_1+x_2$?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:

The positive sign is natural: a large minimum forces a large maximum.
SOLUTION: Areas and the product trick
**Conditional probability.** $P(Z\le z)=P(X_1\le z,X_2\le z)=z^2$. If $y>z$ the event $\{Y\ge y, Z\le z\}$ is empty since $Y\le Z$. For $0\le y\le z$,
$$P(Y\ge y,\ Z\le z)=P(y\le X_1\le z,\ y\le X_2\le z)=(z-y)^2,$$
so
$$P(Y\ge y\mid Z\le z)=\frac{(z-y)^2}{z^2}\quad(0\le y\le z,\ z>0),\qquad 0\text{ if }y>z.$$
**Moments.** With $f_Y(y)=2(1-y)$ and $f_Z(z)=2z$:
$$E[Y^2]=\int_0^12(1-y)y^2\,dy=\frac23-\frac12=\frac16,\qquad E[Z^2]=\int_0^12z^3\,dz=\frac12,$$
$$\operatorname{Var}Y=\frac16-\frac19=\frac1{18},\qquad\operatorname{Var}Z=\frac12-\frac49=\frac1{18}.$$
**Cross moment.** Whatever the order, $\{Y,Z\}=\{X_1,X_2\}$, so $YZ=X_1X_2$ and
$$E[YZ]=E[X_1]E[X_2]=\frac14.$$
**Correlation.** $\operatorname{Cov}(Y,Z)=\frac14-\frac13\cdot\frac23=\frac14-\frac29=\frac1{36}$, hence
$$\operatorname{corr}(Y,Z)=\frac{1/36}{\sqrt{\frac1{18}\cdot\frac1{18}}}=\frac{18}{36}=\frac12.$$
SOLUTION: Joint density
For $0\le y\le z\le1$, $F(y,z)=P(Y\le y,Z\le z)=P(Z\le z)-P(Y>y,Z\le z)=z^2-(z-y)^2=2yz-y^2$. Hence the joint density is $f(y,z)=\dfrac{\partial^2F}{\partial y\,\partial z}=2$ on $\{0\le y\le z\le1\}$, and
$$E[YZ]=\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^z2yz\,dy\,dz=\int_0^1z\cdot z^2\,dz=\frac14,$$
as before; the rest of the computation is identical.
===END===

TITLE: Random ants on a string
QUESTION:
500 ants are placed on a string 1 foot long, each at an independent uniform position. Each ant independently picks a direction (left or right, with probability $\tfrac12$ each) and walks at a constant speed of 1 foot per minute until it falls off an end. The ants are infinitely small; when two ants meet head-on, both instantly reverse direction and keep walking at 1 foot per minute. What is the expected time until all the ants have fallen off the string?
HINT1:
The collisions look like the hard part. What would an observer who cannot tell the ants apart see at a collision?
HINT2:
Swapping the labels of two ants at each collision turns the dynamics into ants that walk straight through each other. Then each "ghost" ant's exit time is a simple function of its starting point, and you need the expectation of the largest of them.
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{500}{501}$ minute $\approx0.998$ min
REFRESH:
- **Maximum of i.i.d. uniforms**: if $T_1,\dots,T_n$ are i.i.d. $U[0,1]$, $P(\max T_i\le t)=t^n$ and $E[\max T_i]=\frac{n}{n+1}$.
- **Symmetry of the uniform**: if $U\sim U[0,1]$ then $1-U\sim U[0,1]$.
EXPLANATION:
When two ants bump and turn around, the picture (a set of unlabelled dots moving on the string) is exactly the same as if they had passed through each other: one dot comes out going left, one going right, from the same place at the same time. Since we only care about when the **last** ant leaves, labels are irrelevant, and we may pretend there are no collisions at all.

Without collisions, an ant starting at $x$ walks straight off: it takes $x$ minutes if it goes left and $1-x$ minutes if it goes right. Either way its exit time is uniform on $[0,1]$, and the 500 exit times are independent. The time for the string to be empty is the maximum of 500 i.i.d. uniforms, with expectation $\frac{500}{501}$.
SOLUTION: Pass-through trick and order statistics
1. **Collisions don't matter.** At a head-on collision, ant $A$ (moving right) and ant $B$ (moving left) meet at a point and both reverse. If we swap their names at that instant, "$A$" continues right and "$B$" continues left, as if nothing happened. The set of positions occupied at each time, and hence the set of exit times, is the same as in the collision-free system where every ant walks straight.

2. **Exit times.** In the collision-free system, the ant starting at $U_i$ with direction $D_i$ exits at time $T_i=U_i$ (left) or $T_i=1-U_i$ (right). Given either direction, $T_i\sim U[0,1]$; since $D_i$ is independent of $U_i$, $T_i\sim U[0,1]$ unconditionally, and the $T_i$ are independent across ants.

3. **Maximum.** The string is empty at time $M=\max_{i\le500}T_i$, with $P(M\le t)=t^{500}$, density $500t^{499}$, and
$$E[M]=\int_0^1t\cdot500t^{499}\,dt=\frac{500}{501}\text{ min}\approx0.998\text{ min}.$$

Note: the book states the answer as $\frac{499}{500}$ min; the correct value is $\frac{500}{501}$ min, since the expected maximum of $n$ i.i.d. uniforms on $[0,1]$ is $\frac{n}{n+1}$ with $n=500$.
FOLLOWUP: How long until you're sure? (ants on a ruler)
10,000 very small ants are dropped at random onto a ruler 100 cm long. Each walks at a steady 1 cm/s along the ruler, initially north or south at random, and two ants meeting head-on both instantly turn around. How long must you wait to be **sure** that every ant has walked off the ruler?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
100 seconds. By the same pass-through argument, collisions only exchange the identities of the ants, so the set of exit times equals that of ants walking straight through each other. A straight-walking ant starting anywhere on the ruler leaves within at most $100$ cm $/\,1$ cm/s $=100$ s. So after 100 s the ruler is certainly empty (and no smaller bound works in general, since an ant may start arbitrarily close to one end facing the other).

Classify the painted cubes: the 8 corners (3 painted faces); the edge cubes that are not corners, $12$ edges $\times(4-2)=24$ (2 painted faces); the face cubes that are not on an edge, $6$ faces $\times(4-2)^2=24$ (1 painted face). Total $8+24+24=56$.

The ant must cross at least two faces (the two corners share no face). Any path over two adjacent faces becomes, after unfolding them into one flat $1\times2$ rectangle, a path between opposite corners of that rectangle, and there the straight diagonal is shortest: $\sqrt{1^2+2^2}=\sqrt5$.

$$\sqrt{2^2+1^2}=\sqrt5\ \text{m}\approx2.236\ \text{m}.$$

Each half is the diagonal of a $1\times\tfrac12$ rectangle, of length $\sqrt{1+\tfrac14}=\tfrac{\sqrt5}2$, and the two halves add to $\sqrt5$. TITLE: Racetrack average speed
QUESTION:
One lap of a racetrack is 1 mile long. A car drives the first lap at an average speed of 30 mph. How fast must it drive the second lap so that its average speed over the two laps is 60 mph?
HINT1:
Average speed is total distance divided by total time. Don't average the two speeds.
HINT2:
How much time is allowed in total for 2 miles at 60 mph? How much time has the first lap already used?
ANSWER:
It is impossible: the car would need infinite speed on the second lap.
REFRESH:
- **Average speed**: $\bar v=\dfrac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}$.
- **Harmonic mean**: over equal distances at speeds $v_1,v_2$, $\bar v=\dfrac{2}{1/v_1+1/v_2}$.
EXPLANATION:
The tempting answer is 90 mph, since $(30+90)/2=60$. But average speed is weighted by time, not by distance, and the slow lap eats a lot of time. To average 60 mph over 2 miles you have 2 minutes in total. Driving the first mile at 30 mph already took exactly 2 minutes. There is no time left, so no finite speed on the second lap achieves the target; as $v_2\to\infty$ the average only approaches 60 mph.
SOLUTION: Count the time
Target: 2 miles at 60 mph takes $\frac{2}{60}$ h $=2$ min. First lap: 1 mile at 30 mph takes $\frac1{30}$ h $=2$ min. The second lap would have to take $2-2=0$ minutes, which requires infinite speed.

In formula form, with second-lap speed $v$:
$$\bar v=\frac{2}{\frac1{30}+\frac1v}<\frac{2}{1/30}=60\quad\text{for every finite }v,$$
so 60 mph is never reached.
FOLLOWUP: With a top speed of 120 mph
If the car's top speed is 120 mph, what is the fastest average speed it can attain over the two laps (first lap still at 30 mph)?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$48$ mph. The second lap at 120 mph takes $\frac1{120}$ h $=0.5$ min, so the 2 miles take $2+0.5=2.5$ min $=\frac1{24}$ h, and the average speed is $\frac{2}{1/24}=48$ mph. (Harmonic mean: $\frac{2}{1/30+1/120}=\frac{2}{5/120}=48$.)
FOLLOWUP: Why averaging speeds fails
Why doesn't averaging the two speeds give the average speed?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
Average speed is total distance over total time, i.e. the average of the instantaneous speed **weighted by time**. The arithmetic mean $\frac{v_1+v_2}2$ weights the two laps equally, which is right only if equal **times** are spent at each speed. Here the laps have equal **distances**, so the slow lap takes longer and gets more weight: the correct average is the harmonic mean $\frac{2}{1/v_1+1/v_2}$, which is always at most the arithmetic mean (with equality only if $v_1=v_2$). For example, 30 and 90 mph over equal distances give $\frac{2}{1/30+1/90}=45$ mph, not 60.
