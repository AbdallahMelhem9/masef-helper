IDS: Q-304
FIRMS: Citadel
SECTION: brainteaser
CATEGORY: Geometry & space

TITLE: Volume common to two crossing cylinders
QUESTION:
Two infinitely long solid cylinders, each of radius $1$, are placed so that their axes meet at a right angle. What is the volume of the region lying inside **both** cylinders?
HINT1:
Put the axes along two coordinate axes and write the region as two inequalities. Then think about slicing the solid instead of integrating over a messy 3D shape.
HINT2:
Take the axes to be the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis: the region is $y^2+z^2\le 1$ and $x^2+z^2\le 1$. Slice at a fixed height $z$: what shape is the cross-section, and what is its area?
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{16}{3}$
REFRESH:
**Volume by slicing (Cavalieri)**: if a solid's cross-section at height $z$ has area $A(z)$, its volume is $\int A(z)\,dz$. In particular two solids with the same cross-sectional area at every height have the same volume.
EXPLANATION:
This solid (the Steinmetz solid) looks awkward, but horizontal slices are simple. At height $z$, the first cylinder only restricts $y$ to $|y|\le\sqrt{1-z^2}$ and the second only restricts $x$ to $|x|\le\sqrt{1-z^2}$. So each slice is a **square** of side $2\sqrt{1-z^2}$, with area $4(1-z^2)$. Integrate that for $z$ from $-1$ to $1$.

A cute way to see it: the unit sphere sits inside the solid, and at each height the sphere's slice is the disc inscribed in the square slice. Disc over square is always $\pi/4$, so the solid's volume is the sphere's volume times $4/\pi$.
SOLUTION: Slicing into squares
Place the cylinders as
$$C_1=\{y^2+z^2\le 1\}\ (\text{axis }= x\text{-axis}),\qquad C_2=\{x^2+z^2\le 1\}\ (\text{axis }= y\text{-axis}).$$
Both force $|z|\le 1$. Fix $z\in[-1,1]$. The slice of $C_1\cap C_2$ at height $z$ is
$$\{(x,y):\ |x|\le\sqrt{1-z^2},\ |y|\le\sqrt{1-z^2}\},$$
a square of side $2\sqrt{1-z^2}$ and area $A(z)=4(1-z^2)$. Hence
$$V=\int_{-1}^{1}4(1-z^2)\,dz=4\Big[z-\frac{z^3}{3}\Big]_{-1}^{1}=4\Big(2-\frac{2}{3}\Big)=\frac{16}{3}.$$
SOLUTION: Compare with the inscribed sphere
The unit ball $x^2+y^2+z^2\le1$ is contained in both cylinders. Its slice at height $z$ is a disc of radius $r=\sqrt{1-z^2}$, area $\pi r^2$, which is exactly the disc inscribed in the square slice of side $2r$ (area $4r^2$). So at every height
$$\frac{A_{\text{solid}}(z)}{A_{\text{ball}}(z)}=\frac{4r^2}{\pi r^2}=\frac{4}{\pi}.$$
Integrating over $z$, the volumes are in the same ratio:
$$V=\frac{4}{\pi}\cdot\frac{4}{3}\pi=\frac{16}{3}.$$
===END===

TITLE: Do two random segments overlap?
QUESTION:
Let $X_1,X_2,X_3,X_4$ be i.i.d. $\text{Unif}(0,1)$. On the real line, draw the segment between $X_1$ and $X_2$ and the segment between $X_3$ and $X_4$. What is the probability that the two segments intersect (share at least one point)?
HINT1:
Only the relative order of the four points matters, not their exact positions.
HINT2:
Sort the four points. Which of them belong to the first segment? By symmetry, every way of splitting the four sorted positions into two pairs is equally likely. In which splits are the segments disjoint?
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{2}{3}$
REFRESH:
**Exchangeability of i.i.d. continuous variables**: for i.i.d. continuous $X_1,\dots,X_n$, ties have probability 0 and all $n!$ orderings are equally likely.
EXPLANATION:
Sort the four points into positions $1<2<3<4$. The two segments are determined by which pair of positions belongs to segment A. There are three ways to split $\{1,2,3,4\}$ into two pairs: $\{1,2\}\{3,4\}$, $\{1,3\}\{2,4\}$, $\{1,4\}\{2,3\}$. Only the first gives two disjoint segments (one entirely to the left of the other); the second gives overlapping segments, the third gives one segment inside the other. By symmetry the three splits are equally likely, so the segments intersect with probability $2/3$.
SOLUTION: Counting orderings
All $4!=24$ orderings of $X_1,\dots,X_4$ are equally likely. The segments are disjoint iff both endpoints of one segment are smaller than both endpoints of the other, i.e. the two smallest values are $\{X_1,X_2\}$ or $\{X_3,X_4\}$.

Number of orderings where $\{X_1,X_2\}$ occupy the two lowest ranks: $2!\cdot 2!=4$. Same for $\{X_3,X_4\}$: $4$. So
$$P(\text{disjoint})=\frac{8}{24}=\frac13,\qquad P(\text{intersect})=1-\frac13=\frac23.$$
SOLUTION: Pairings of the sorted positions
Given the sorted positions, the partner of the smallest point (the other endpoint of its segment) is equally likely to be position 2, 3 or 4, by symmetry. The segments are disjoint only if the partner is position 2. So $P(\text{disjoint})=1/3$ and $P(\text{intersect})=2/3$.

TITLE: Irrational to an irrational power
QUESTION:
Do there exist irrational numbers $a$ and $b$ such that $a^b$ is rational? Justify your answer.
HINT1:
Try $\sqrt2$ as a base. You may not need to know whether a certain number is rational to finish the argument.
HINT2:
Look at $\sqrt2^{\sqrt2}$. Either it is rational, or it is not. In the second case, raise it to the power $\sqrt2$ once more.
ANSWER:
Yes: for example $(\sqrt2^{\sqrt2})^{\sqrt2}=2$ (or $\sqrt2^{\,\log_2 9}=3$).
REFRESH:
**$\sqrt2$ is irrational**: if $\sqrt2=p/q$ in lowest terms then $p^2=2q^2$, so $p$ is even, then $q$ is even — contradiction.
**Power rule**: $(x^y)^z=x^{yz}$ for $x>0$.
EXPLANATION:
There is a famous proof that does not even tell you which pair works. Consider $x=\sqrt2^{\sqrt2}$. If $x$ is rational, then $a=b=\sqrt2$ works. If $x$ is irrational, then take $a=x$, $b=\sqrt2$: $a^b=\sqrt2^{\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2}=\sqrt2^2=2$. Either way a pair exists.

If you want an explicit pair, pick the exponent so the powers cancel: $\sqrt2^{\,b}=2^{b/2}$, so choose $b=2\log_2 3=\log_2 9$, giving $3$. You only have to check that $\log_2 9$ is irrational, which is a one-line parity argument.
SOLUTION: Non-constructive dichotomy
Let $x=\sqrt2^{\sqrt2}$.
- If $x\in\mathbb{Q}$: take $a=b=\sqrt2$, both irrational, and $a^b=x$ is rational.
- If $x\notin\mathbb{Q}$: take $a=x$ (irrational) and $b=\sqrt2$ (irrational). Then
$$a^b=\big(\sqrt2^{\sqrt2}\big)^{\sqrt2}=\sqrt2^{\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2}=\sqrt2^{\,2}=2\in\mathbb{Q}.$$
In both cases irrational $a,b$ with $a^b$ rational exist. (In fact $\sqrt2^{\sqrt2}$ is irrational — even transcendental, by the Gelfond–Schneider theorem — so the second case is the true one, but the argument doesn't need that.)
SOLUTION: Explicit pair
Take $a=\sqrt2$ and $b=\log_2 9$. Then
$$a^b=2^{\frac12\log_2 9}=2^{\log_2 3}=3.$$
$\log_2 9$ is irrational: if $\log_2 9=p/q$ with $p,q$ positive integers, then $2^p=9^q$; the left side is even and the right side is odd, contradiction. And $\sqrt2$ is irrational. So this is an explicit example.

TITLE: Guess when the fifth king appears
QUESTION:
A dealer shuffles $10$ standard decks together ($520$ cards, $40$ of each rank) and turns cards over one at a time from the top. The game ends as soon as the $5$th king is turned over. You win if your guess is closest to the total number of cards turned over (the final king included). Which number should you guess to maximise your chance of winning? (This is **not** the expected value.)
HINT1:
You want the most likely value (the mode) of the stopping time. Write down $P(N=n)$ and compare consecutive values.
HINT2:
$N=n$ means: among the first $n-1$ cards there are exactly $4$ kings, and card $n$ is a king. Equivalently, count the arrangements of the 40 king positions: 4 among the first $n-1$ positions, one at position $n$, 35 among the last $520-n$. Then look at the ratio $P(N=n)/P(N=n-1)$ and find when it drops below $1$.
ANSWER:
$54$
REFRESH:
**Negative hypergeometric distribution**: drawing without replacement from $N$ items of which $K$ are successes, the draw $n$ on which the $r$-th success occurs has
$$P(n)=\frac{\binom{n-1}{r-1}\binom{N-n}{K-r}}{\binom{N}{K}},$$
because the $K$ success positions form a uniformly random $K$-subset of $\{1,\dots,N\}$. Its mean is $r\,\frac{N+1}{K+1}$.
**Mode of a discrete distribution**: if $p(n)/p(n-1)>1$ exactly for $n\le m$, then $m$ is the mode.
EXPLANATION:
The 40 kings occupy a uniformly random set of 40 positions among 520. The game lasts $n$ cards when position $n$ holds a king, 4 kings lie before it and 35 after it. That gives a clean formula, and the ratio of consecutive probabilities simplifies to a ratio of two linear factors. The probabilities increase while the ratio is above $1$, which happens up to $n=54$, and decrease afterwards.

Note that the mean is much larger: $5\cdot 521/41\approx 63.5$. The distribution is right-skewed (a long tail of games where kings are late), so the mode is well below the mean.
SOLUTION: Ratio of consecutive probabilities
The king positions are a uniform random 40-subset of $\{1,\dots,520\}$. The event $\{N=n\}$ means: position $n$ is a king, exactly 4 of the first $n-1$ positions are kings, and the other 35 kings are among the last $520-n$ positions. Hence
$$p(n)=P(N=n)=\frac{\binom{n-1}{4}\binom{520-n}{35}}{\binom{520}{40}},\qquad 5\le n\le 485.$$
Ratio of consecutive terms:
$$\frac{p(n)}{p(n-1)}=\frac{\binom{n-1}{4}}{\binom{n-2}{4}}\cdot\frac{\binom{520-n}{35}}{\binom{521-n}{35}}=\frac{n-1}{n-5}\cdot\frac{486-n}{521-n}.$$
This is $>1$ iff
$$(n-1)(486-n)>(n-5)(521-n)$$
$$-n^2+487n-486>-n^2+526n-2605\iff 2119>39n\iff n<54.33.$$
So $p(n)$ increases up to $n=54$ and decreases from $n=55$ on. Check: $p(54)/p(53)=\frac{53\cdot432}{49\cdot467}=\frac{22896}{22883}>1$ and $p(55)/p(54)=\frac{54\cdot431}{50\cdot466}=\frac{23274}{23300}<1$.

The most likely number of cards is $54$ (with $p(54)\approx 0.01649$), which is the guess that maximises your chance of being exactly right. The mean is $5\cdot\frac{521}{41}\approx 63.5$, so guessing the mean would be a mistake.

TITLE: The roll that crosses 1000
QUESTION:
You roll a fair six-sided die repeatedly and keep a running total, stopping as soon as the total is at least $1000$. Which face is most likely to be the value of the final roll?
HINT1:
Think about where the running total can be just before the last roll, and what roll is needed from there.
HINT2:
The last roll equals $k$ exactly when the total just before it is one of $1000-k,\dots,999$ and you then roll $k$. Write $P(\text{last}=k)$ as a sum over those totals.
ANSWER:
$6$ (with probability about $6/21=2/7$)
REFRESH:
**Renewal density**: for a walk with i.i.d. positive steps of mean $\mu$, the probability of landing exactly on a large value $s$ tends to $1/\mu$. For a fair die, $\mu=3.5$, so each large total is visited with probability $\approx 2/7$.
EXPLANATION:
The final roll is the one that jumps from below $1000$ to $1000$ or more. A roll of $6$ does that from any of six totals ($994,\dots,999$), whereas a roll of $1$ only does it from $999$. A bigger face has more starting points from which it can be the finishing roll, so it is more likely to be the last. It's the same bias as "the bus interval you arrive in tends to be long": long jumps are more likely to be the ones that straddle a fixed point.

Quantitatively each total near $1000$ is hit with probability $\approx 2/7$, so $P(\text{last}=k)\approx \frac{2}{7}\cdot k\cdot\frac16=\frac{k}{21}$.
SOLUTION: Sum over the pre-final totals
Let $v_s=P(\text{the running total ever equals } s)$; $v_s>0$ for every $s\ge0$. The last roll is $k$ iff for some $j\in\{1,\dots,k\}$ the total visits $1000-j$ and the next roll is $k$ (then the total becomes $1000-j+k\ge1000$; and since the total was below $1000$, that roll is indeed the last). For different $j$ these events are disjoint (the roll of $k$ from $1000-j$ ends the game, so the walk cannot also roll $k$ from another total in that window) and the next roll is independent of the past, so
$$P(\text{last}=k)=\frac16\sum_{j=1}^{k}v_{1000-j}.$$
Every term is positive, so $P(\text{last}=k)$ is strictly increasing in $k$: face $6$ is the most likely.

Numerically, $v_s\to 1/3.5=2/7$ very fast, so
$$P(\text{last}=k)\approx\frac16\cdot k\cdot\frac27=\frac{k}{21}:\qquad \frac1{21},\frac2{21},\dots,\frac6{21}.$$
(An exact computation gives $0.0476, 0.0952, 0.1429, 0.1905, 0.2381, 0.2857$.)

TITLE: Type, copy, paste: 200 letters fast
QUESTION:
You start with an empty document. Each second you may do exactly one of:
- **type** one letter,
- **copy** the entire current text to the clipboard (replacing its content),
- **paste** the clipboard at the end of the text.

What is the minimum number of seconds needed to have at least $200$ letters?
HINT1:
A copy followed by several pastes multiplies the length. How much does multiplying by $m$ cost in seconds?
HINT2:
"Copy then $m-1$ pastes" costs $m$ seconds and multiplies the length by $m$. Argue that all typing should happen at the very beginning. Then you want to maximise $a\cdot m_1 m_2\cdots$ subject to $a+m_1+m_2+\dots=t$: a maximal-product partition problem.
ANSWER:
$15$ seconds
REFRESH:
**Maximal product of a partition**: among positive integers summing to $s$, the product is maximised by using as many 3's as possible (with a 2 or two 2's to finish): e.g. $14=3+3+3+3+2$ gives $162$, $15=3\cdot5$ gives $243$.
EXPLANATION:
After the initial typing, the useful operations come in blocks "copy, then paste $m-1$ times", which cost $m$ seconds and multiply the text length by $m$. Typing extra letters later is never better than typing them at the start (where they get multiplied by everything that follows). So in $t$ seconds the best you can do is $a\cdot m_1\cdots m_k$ where $a+m_1+\dots+m_k=t$ — a partition of $t$, and you want the largest product. Products of parts are maximised by 3's: in $14$ seconds the best is $3^4\cdot 2=162<200$, in $15$ seconds $3^5=243\ge200$.
SOLUTION: Reduce to a partition problem
**Normal form.** (i) Moving a "type" keystroke to the very beginning never hurts: the states (text length, clipboard length) along the modified sequence are componentwise at least as large as in the original from that point on, and each operation is monotone in both. (ii) Two copies in a row, or a copy not followed by a paste, waste time; a paste before any copy does nothing. So an optimal sequence is: type $a$ letters, then blocks each consisting of one copy followed by $m_i-1\ge1$ pastes.

A block with $m_i$ seconds turns length $L$ into $m_iL$. After $t=a+m_1+\dots+m_k$ seconds the length is
$$a\cdot m_1\cdots m_k.$$
**Upper bound at 14 seconds.** The length is the product of the parts of a partition of $14$ (the part $a$ included). In a partition with maximal product: a part $1$ can be merged into another part $x$ ($x+1>x\cdot1$); a part $x\ge4$ can be split into $2$ and $x-2$ ($2(x-2)\ge x$); and three 2's can be replaced by two 3's ($9>8$). So the maximum uses 3's and at most two 2's: $14=3+3+3+3+2$, product $81\cdot 2=162<200$. So 14 seconds are not enough.

**15 seconds suffice.** $15=3+3+3+3+3$ gives $3^5=243$. Concretely: type 3 letters (3 s), then 4 times "copy, paste, paste" (3 s each):
$$3\to9\to27\to81\to243\qquad(3+4\cdot3=15\text{ s}).$$
Another 15-second route: type 3, then $\times2,\times2,\times2,\times3,\times3$ ($3+2+2+2+3+3=15$ s), giving $216$. Either way at least 200 letters.

Minimum: $15$ seconds.

- $000$: all 5.
- $100$: rows with the first column off: $000,010,001$ → 3.
- $010$: middle column off: $000,100,001,101$ → 4.
- $001$: 3 (by symmetry with $100$).
- $101$: first and last columns off: $000,010$ → 2.

Top and bottom rows are chosen independently given the middle row, so the number of good grids is
$$5^2+3^2+4^2+3^2+2^2=25+9+16+9+4=63.$$

- **Centre lit.** Its 4 side-neighbours must be off; the 4 corners are not adjacent to each other (nor to the centre), so they are free: $2^4=16$ good grids.

Total $16+47=63$, probability $63/512$.

$$P(K\ge2)=\binom32\Big(\frac23\Big)^2\frac13+\Big(\frac23\Big)^3=3\cdot\frac49\cdot\frac13+\frac8{27}=\frac{12}{27}+\frac{8}{27}=\frac{20}{27}.$$
SOLUTION: Density of the median

$$P\Big(\text{median}<\frac23\Big)=\int_0^{2/3}6x(1-x)\,dx=\big[3x^2-2x^3\big]_0^{2/3}=\frac43-\frac{16}{27}=\frac{20}{27}.$$

- Next new outcome (among 2 unseen evens and "odd") is an even: $\dfrac{2/6}{2/6+3/6}=\dfrac25$.
- Next new outcome (1 unseen even vs "odd") is the even: $\dfrac{1/6}{1/6+3/6}=\dfrac14$.

- $S\le1000$: all calls expire worthless: $0$.

- $S\ge1020$: $(S-1000)-2(S-1010)+(S-1020)=0$.

So the payoff is between $0$ and $10$ (maximal at $S=1010$), never negative. Total profit $=0.25+\text{payoff}\ge0.25$: a guaranteed profit of at least $0.25$ per butterfly (and up to $10.25$), with no risk. (Per listed contract, multiply by the contract multiplier.)

TITLE: When do three planets line up again?
QUESTION:
Three planets orbit a star in the same plane and the same direction, with periods of $40$, $60$ and $90$ years (uniform circular motion). Right now the three planets and the star lie on one straight line. After how many years will the three planets and the star next lie on one straight line again?

The naive LCM of the periods is when everything returns to the starting position; but lining up with the star does not require every planet to be back where it started, nor even on the same side of the star.
HINT2:
Work with angle differences: two planets are collinear with the star when their angle difference is a multiple of half a turn. Compute how often each pair does that.
ANSWER:
$180$ years
REFRESH:
**Relative (synodic) period**: two bodies with periods $T_1<T_2$ moving the same way gain one full lap on each other every $\frac{1}{1/T_1-1/T_2}$ time units.
**LCM of rationals**: the first common positive multiple of $a$ and $b$ is $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$ for integers.
EXPLANATION:
Measure angles in turns. Planet $i$ is at angle $t/T_i$ plus its start angle. They start collinear with the star, so all start angles agree modulo half a turn; we can take them all equal to $0$ modulo $\tfrac12$. The configuration is collinear again when every pairwise angle difference is a multiple of $\frac12$ turn. The pair $(40,60)$ drifts apart at $\frac1{40}-\frac1{60}=\frac1{120}$ turn per year, so it is collinear every $60$ years; the pair $(60,90)$ drifts at $\frac1{180}$, collinear every $90$ years. Both happen first at $\operatorname{lcm}(60,90)=180$, and the third pair then follows automatically. At $t=180$ the fast planet has done $4.5$ laps, so it is on the opposite side of the star from the other two — but all four bodies are on one line.
SOLUTION: Pairwise angle differences
Angles in turns: $\theta_i(t)=\theta_i(0)+t/T_i$ with $T=40,60,90$, and $\theta_i(0)-\theta_j(0)\in\frac12\mathbb{Z}$. Collinearity with the star at time $t$ means $\theta_i(t)-\theta_j(t)\in\frac12\mathbb{Z}$ for all pairs, i.e.
$$t\Big(\frac1{40}-\frac1{60}\Big)=\frac{t}{120}\in\tfrac12\mathbb{Z}\iff 60\mid t,\qquad t\Big(\frac1{60}-\frac1{90}\Big)=\frac{t}{180}\in\tfrac12\mathbb{Z}\iff 90\mid t.$$
(The pair $(40,90)$: $t\cdot\frac{5}{360}=\frac{t}{72}\in\frac12\mathbb{Z}\iff 36\mid t$, implied by the other two conditions since the differences add up.) The smallest positive $t$ with $60\mid t$ and $90\mid t$ is $\operatorname{lcm}(60,90)=180$.

Check at $t=180$: laps $180/40=4.5$, $180/60=3$, $180/90=2$. Planets 2 and 3 are back at their start, planet 1 is diametrically opposite its start: all collinear with the star. Answer: $180$ years.
FOLLOWUP: Same side of the star
Suppose the three planets start on the same ray from the star (all on the same side), with the same periods $40$, $60$, $90$ years. When are they next all on the same side of the star, on one ray?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
Now each pairwise angle difference must be a whole number of turns: $t/120\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $t/180\in\mathbb{Z}$, so $t$ is a multiple of $\operatorname{lcm}(120,180)=360$ (and then $t/72=5\in\mathbb{Z}$ too). Answer: **$360$ years**, which here coincides with $\operatorname{lcm}(40,60,90)=360$, when every planet is back at its starting point ($9$, $6$ and $4$ laps).
