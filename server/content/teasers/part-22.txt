TITLE: Why a maker rebate tightens quotes
QUESTION:
On a maker-taker exchange, whoever posts a resting limit order that gets filled (the *maker*) receives a small rebate per share, while whoever crosses the spread (the *taker*) pays a fee. Why might a market maker on such an exchange be willing to quote a tighter bid-ask spread than the "natural" spread they would need without the rebate? What are the limits of this?
HINT1:
Write down what a market maker earns, per share, on a round trip (buy on the bid, later sell on the offer). Which terms does the exchange change?
HINT2:
A market maker's quoted half-spread has to cover adverse selection and inventory risk. If each passive fill also pays a rebate $r$, how much of that cost is already covered before the spread itself contributes anything?
ANSWER:
Each passive fill earns the rebate $r$, so the maker's effective half-spread is $h + r$; to earn the same compensation for adverse selection and inventory risk it can quote a half-spread $r$ narrower, and competition between makers pushes quotes to do exactly that (until quotes hit the tick size).
REFRESH:
Half-spread: if the market is bid $B$, offered $A$, the half-spread is $h = (A-B)/2$; a maker who buys on the bid and sells on the offer earns $2h$ per share on a round trip before costs.
Adverse selection: the expected loss per fill to better-informed takers — after you are filled on the bid, the price tends to move down.
EXPLANATION:
A market maker does not quote a spread because it likes spreads; the spread is the price of the risks it takes. Per share filled passively, the maker must earn at least $c$ = (expected adverse-selection loss) + (inventory/holding cost). Without rebates it needs a half-spread $h \ge c$. With a rebate $r$ per passive fill, the total take per fill is $h + r$, so the break-even condition becomes $h + r \ge c$, i.e. $h \ge c - r$. The rebate *subsidises* the quote: the maker can post a narrower market and still be compensated. And because several makers compete, the one who passes the rebate on to the takers (by quoting tighter) gets priority, so in equilibrium quoted spreads narrow by roughly the rebate. The takers pay a fee $f \ge r$ (the exchange keeps $f - r$), so the *all-in* cost for a taker — quoted half-spread plus fee — hardly changes; it is just repackaged.
SOLUTION: Break-even accounting
1. Per share bought on the bid, the maker's P&L relative to fair value $V$ is $(V - B) + r - L$, where $L$ is the expected adverse move against it after the fill (adverse selection) plus inventory cost.
2. Without rebate ($r = 0$) the maker needs $V - B = h \ge L$.
3. With rebate $r$, it needs only $h \ge L - r$. So the same maker is indifferent between quoting half-spread $L$ with no rebate and half-spread $L - r$ with the rebate.
4. Competition: if one maker quotes at $h = L$, a rival can quote one tick better, still break even thanks to the rebate, and take the queue priority. Iterating, quoted half-spreads fall toward $L - r$.
5. Limits:
 - The tick size: once the spread is one tick wide it cannot tighten further; the rebate then shows up as longer queues (more makers competing for fills at the same price) instead of tighter prices.
 - Adverse selection is unchanged: quoting tighter against informed flow still loses; the rebate only offsets it by $r$.
 - The taker's all-in cost is $h + f$ with $f \ge r$, so tighter *displayed* quotes are partly cosmetic — this is why brokers routing on displayed price alone can be misled, and why inverted (taker-maker) venues exist.
6. Conclusion: the rebate is a per-fill income that covers part of the maker's cost of liquidity provision, so it can quote up to $r$ tighter per side and still break even.

Square-root impact law: expected price impact of a metaorder of size $Q$ is $\Delta P / P \approx Y\,\sigma_{\text{daily}}\sqrt{Q / V_{\text{daily}}}$ with $Y$ of order 1 and $V_{\text{daily}}$ the average daily volume.

A small order just pays half the spread. A large order eats through the book and, more importantly, keeps pushing the price as other participants notice the persistent buying. Empirically the cost does not grow linearly with size but like its square root, measured in units of daily volatility and daily volume. 3. $\sqrt{0.026} \approx 0.16$.
4. Assume a daily volatility $\sigma \approx 2\%$ (typical large-cap stock) and $Y \approx 1$.
5. Impact $\approx 1 \times 0.02 \times 0.16 = 0.0032 = 32$ bp, i.e. roughly 30 bp.
6. Sanity checks: the spread cost is only a few bp, so impact dominates. SOLUTION: Book-walking intuition
1. If you send the whole order as a market order you consume every resting offer until 100,000 shares are filled. Displayed depth at the touch in a liquid stock is typically a few thousand shares, so you would walk many ticks up — easily 50–100+ bp. That is the worst case.
2. If you slice it into child orders of, say, 1,000 shares over about 100 minutes (10% of volume), each child pays roughly half the spread, but the persistent buying drifts the price up; the empirical size of that drift is the square-root figure above, about 30 bp.

By symmetry, $P(Y > X) = P(X > Y)$. So it is enough to know $P(X = Y)$.
HINT2:
Both of us need exactly $k$ flips with probability $(1/2)^k \cdot (1/2)^k$. Sum over $k$.
ANSWER:
$1/3$
REFRESH:
Geometric distribution: flips until first head with a fair coin, $P(X = k) = (1/2)^k$ for $k \ge 1$, and $P(X > k) = (1/2)^k$.
Geometric series: $\sum_{k\ge1} q^k = \frac{q}{1-q}$ for $|q|<1$.
EXPLANATION:
The three events "you need more", "I need more", "tie" partition everything, and the first two have equal probability by symmetry. A tie at $k$ flips means both sequences are $T\dots TH$ of length $k$, probability $4^{-k}$; summing gives $1/3$. So the remaining $2/3$ splits equally: $1/3$ each. A slicker view: flip both coins together each round. Each round is one of four equally likely outcomes; the first round that is not TT decides everything, and among HH, HT, TH each is equally likely, giving $1/3$ for "you got your head and I didn't".
SOLUTION: Symmetry and the tie probability
1. $P(X = Y) = \sum_{k\ge1} P(X=k)P(Y=k) = \sum_{k \ge 1} (1/2)^k (1/2)^k = \sum_{k\ge1} (1/4)^k = \dfrac{1/4}{3/4} = \dfrac13$.
2. By symmetry $P(Y > X) = P(X > Y)$, and the three probabilities sum to 1, so $P(Y > X) = \dfrac{1 - 1/3}{2} = \dfrac13$.
SOLUTION: Simultaneous rounds
1. Flip both coins at the same time, round after round. Each round gives HH, HT, TH, TT with probability $1/4$ each.
2. While the round is TT, nobody is done; the game restarts identically.
3. The first round that is not TT is uniformly one of HH, HT, TH (conditioning on "not TT"). "I need strictly more flips than you" means you get a head and I don't in that round: outcome (you H, me T), one of three.
4. $P(Y > X) = 1/3$.
SOLUTION: Direct sum
$P(Y > X) = \sum_{k\ge1} P(X = k)\,P(Y > k) = \sum_{k \ge 1} (1/2)^k (1/2)^k = \dfrac13.$
FOLLOWUP: Biased coins
You flip a coin with head probability $p$, I flip one with head probability $q$. What is $P(Y > X)$?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
Each simultaneous round ends the comparison unless both show tails. $P(Y > X) = \sum_{k\ge1} (1-p)^{k-1}p\,(1-q)^k = \dfrac{p(1-q)}{1-(1-p)(1-q)} = \dfrac{p(1-q)}{p+q-pq}$. For $p = q = 1/2$: $\frac{1/4}{3/4} = 1/3$.

TITLE: What are the Greeks?
QUESTION:
What are the option Greeks, what does each measure, and why do traders care about them?
HINT1:
An option price depends on several inputs: spot, time, volatility, interest rate. Each Greek is a sensitivity to one of them.
HINT2:
Think partial derivatives of the option value $V(S, t, \sigma, r)$: first derivative in $S$, second derivative in $S$, derivative in $t$, in $\sigma$, in $r$.
ANSWER:
Delta $\partial V/\partial S$, Gamma $\partial^2 V/\partial S^2$, Theta $\partial V/\partial t$, Vega $\partial V/\partial \sigma$, Rho $\partial V/\partial r$: the sensitivities of the option value to its inputs, used to hedge, to decompose P&L and to set risk limits.
REFRESH:
Taylor expansion: $\Delta V \approx V_S\,\Delta S + \tfrac12 V_{SS}(\Delta S)^2 + V_t\,\Delta t + V_\sigma\,\Delta\sigma + V_r\,\Delta r$.
Black-Scholes call delta: $N(d_1)$, with $N$ the standard normal CDF.
EXPLANATION:
An option book's value moves when the stock moves, when time passes, when implied volatility changes, and when rates change. The Greeks are the partial derivatives that tell you how much. They matter because they let you (1) hedge — e.g. hold $-\Delta$ shares per option to neutralise small stock moves; (2) explain P&L after the fact ("we made money on gamma, lost on theta"); (3) set limits (max vega per desk). The key trade-off for a delta-hedged option is gamma against theta: a long option position gains from large moves (positive gamma) but loses value every day (negative theta), and in Black-Scholes these two exactly offset when realised volatility equals implied.
SOLUTION: The five main Greeks
1. **Delta** $\Delta = \partial V / \partial S$: change in option value per unit move in the underlying. Call: between 0 and 1 (Black-Scholes: $N(d_1)$); put: between −1 and 0. Hedge ratio: short $\Delta$ shares per long call.
2. **Gamma** $\Gamma = \partial^2 V/\partial S^2$: how fast delta changes. Largest for at-the-money options near expiry. Long options have positive gamma: a delta-hedged long option gains $\tfrac12\Gamma(\Delta S)^2$ on any move.
3. **Theta** $\Theta = \partial V/\partial t$: time decay; usually negative for long options (you pay for gamma through theta).
4. **Vega** $\nu = \partial V/\partial \sigma$: sensitivity to implied volatility; positive for long calls and puts, largest at the money and for long maturities.
5. **Rho** $\rho = \partial V/\partial r$: sensitivity to interest rates; positive for calls, negative for puts; usually minor for short-dated equity options.
6. Why they matter:
 - Hedging: neutralise delta with the underlying, gamma and vega with other options.
 - P&L attribution: $\Delta V \approx \Delta\,\delta S + \tfrac12\Gamma(\delta S)^2 + \Theta\,\delta t + \nu\,\delta\sigma$.
 - Gamma–theta link: for a delta-hedged option in Black-Scholes, $\Theta + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma = rV - rS\Delta$, so with $r \approx 0$, theta pays for gamma exactly at the implied volatility.
 - Risk limits and scenario analysis for the whole book.
7. Second-order Greeks traders also watch: vanna ($\partial^2 V/\partial S\partial\sigma$), volga ($\partial^2 V/\partial\sigma^2$), charm (delta decay).

Correlation can come from a common factor (two stocks both driven by the market), reverse causality (flows chasing returns) or chance (data mining), so it does not imply causation.

EXPLANATION:
Correlation is symmetric and observational: it says two quantities tend to move together in the data. Causation is directional and interventional: if we forced $X$ up, $Y$ would move. The same correlation is produced by $X \to Y$, by $Y \to X$, by $Z \to X$ and $Z \to Y$, or by coincidence in a finite sample. SOLUTION: Definitions and examples

3. Why they differ, with finance examples:
 - **Common factor (confounder):** two airline stocks are highly correlated not because one drives the other but because oil prices and the overall market drive both. Buying one does not raise the other.
 - **Reverse causality:** fund inflows and past returns are correlated; is it inflows pushing prices up, or good returns attracting inflows? Mostly the latter.
 - **Spurious correlation / data mining:** test thousands of signals (e.g. butter production in Bangladesh vs the S&P 500) and some will correlate strongly by chance; two trending random walks often show large sample correlation.

TITLE: The bias-variance trade-off
QUESTION:
What is the bias-variance trade-off? Derive the decomposition of the mean squared error and explain what it means for choosing a model.
HINT1:
Write the error of an estimator as (estimator − its own mean) + (its mean − the truth).
HINT2:
Expand the square; the cross term has zero expectation. For prediction, also keep track of the irreducible noise in $Y$.
ANSWER:
$E[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2] = \operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta)^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)$ (plus noise variance $\sigma^2$ for prediction error); flexible models reduce bias but increase variance, simple ones do the reverse, and the best model balances the two (chosen e.g. by cross-validation).
REFRESH:
$E[(Z - c)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(Z) + (E[Z] - c)^2$ for any constant $c$.
EXPLANATION:
An estimator can be wrong in two ways: it can be systematically off (bias) or it can jump around from sample to sample (variance). Mean squared error adds the two. Complex models (many parameters, deep trees, high-degree polynomials) can fit any pattern, so their bias is small, but they also fit the noise of the particular sample, so their variance is large (overfitting). Simple models are stable but miss real structure (underfitting). Regularisation (ridge, lasso, shrinkage) deliberately adds a little bias to remove a lot of variance. In finance, with noisy and short data, variance usually dominates — which is why shrunk covariance matrices and simple signals often beat complex fits out of sample.
SOLUTION: Derivation
1. Let $\hat\theta$ be an estimator of $\theta$, $m = E[\hat\theta]$. Then $\hat\theta - \theta = (\hat\theta - m) + (m - \theta)$.
2. $E[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2] = E[(\hat\theta - m)^2] + 2(m - \theta)E[\hat\theta - m] + (m - \theta)^2$.
3. The middle term vanishes since $E[\hat\theta - m] = 0$. So $\operatorname{MSE} = \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) + \operatorname{Bias}^2$.
4. Prediction version: $Y = f(x) + \varepsilon$, $E\varepsilon = 0$, $\operatorname{Var}\varepsilon = \sigma^2$, $\varepsilon$ independent of the fitted $\hat f$. Then
$$E[(Y - \hat f(x))^2] = \sigma^2 + \big(E\hat f(x) - f(x)\big)^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\hat f(x)).$$
5. Trade-off: as model complexity grows, bias² falls and variance rises, so test error is U-shaped; training error keeps falling, which is why it cannot be used to choose complexity.
6. Example: estimating a mean $\mu$ from $n$ observations with variance $s^2$. The sample mean has bias 0 and variance $s^2/n$. The shrunk estimator $c\bar X$ ($0<c<1$) has bias $(c-1)\mu$ and variance $c^2 s^2/n$; its MSE $(1-c)^2\mu^2 + c^2 s^2/n$ is minimised at $c = \dfrac{\mu^2}{\mu^2 + s^2/n} < 1$, so a little shrinkage always lowers the MSE, and the gain is large when $\mu^2$ is small compared with the noise $s^2/n$ — exactly the situation for expected stock returns (in practice $c$ must itself be estimated, as in James-Stein shrinkage).

TITLE: How to test for stationarity
QUESTION:
How would you test whether a time series is stationary?
HINT1:
Start informally: what would a plot, rolling statistics and the autocorrelation function look like for a stationary versus a non-stationary series?
HINT2:
Formally, the classic tests ask whether there is a unit root: fit $\Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma y_{t-1} + \sum_i \phi_i\Delta y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t$ and test $\gamma = 0$. There is also a test with the opposite null.
ANSWER:
Look at the plot, rolling mean/variance and ACF, then use unit-root tests (augmented Dickey-Fuller, Phillips-Perron: null = unit root) together with KPSS (null = stationary); if non-stationary, difference or detrend and test again.
REFRESH:
Weak stationarity: $E[y_t]$ constant, $\operatorname{Var}(y_t)$ constant and finite, $\operatorname{Cov}(y_t, y_{t+h})$ depends only on $h$.
AR(1) $y_t = \phi y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ is stationary iff $|\phi| < 1$; $\phi = 1$ is a random walk (unit root).
EXPLANATION:
A stationary series has a fixed mean and variance and a correlation structure that depends only on the lag, so it keeps coming back to its mean. The canonical non-stationary example is a random walk: its variance grows linearly in time and shocks never die out. Testing stationarity is therefore mostly testing for a unit root: in the AR(1) $y_t = \phi y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$, rewrite as $\Delta y_t = (\phi - 1)y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ and test whether the coefficient $\gamma = \phi - 1$ is zero. Because under the null the regressor is itself non-stationary, the t-statistic does not have a Student distribution — it has the Dickey-Fuller distribution with more negative critical values (about −2.86 at 5% with a constant). Unit-root tests have low power, so pairing them with KPSS (whose null is stationarity) gives a more reliable verdict. In practice (pairs trading, mean reversion) this is how you check whether a spread is tradeable.
SOLUTION: Workflow
1. **Plot** the series; look for trends, changing variance, structural breaks.
2. **Rolling statistics:** rolling mean and rolling SD should be roughly flat if stationary.
3. **ACF:** for a stationary series the autocorrelation decays quickly; for a unit-root series it stays near 1 and decays very slowly.
4. **Augmented Dickey-Fuller:** regress $\Delta y_t = \alpha + \beta t + \gamma y_{t-1} + \sum_{i=1}^p \phi_i \Delta y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t$ (lags $p$ chosen by AIC/BIC to whiten residuals; include $\alpha$, $\beta t$ as appropriate). $H_0: \gamma = 0$ (unit root), $H_1: \gamma < 0$ (stationary). Compare the t-statistic of $\hat\gamma$ with Dickey-Fuller critical values (not normal ones).
5. **Phillips-Perron:** same null, corrects for autocorrelation/heteroskedasticity non-parametrically.
6. **KPSS:** $H_0$: (trend-)stationary, $H_1$: unit root. Combine: ADF rejects and KPSS does not reject → stationary; ADF does not reject and KPSS rejects → unit root; both reject or neither → inconclusive (maybe long memory or breaks).
7. **Remedies:** if non-stationary, take differences (prices → returns) or log-differences, or remove a deterministic trend, and re-test. Watch structural breaks (Zivot-Andrews test) which can fool ADF into accepting a unit root.
8. For a pair of assets: regress one price on the other and run ADF on the residual spread (Engle-Granger cointegration test, with its own critical values).

TITLE: Derive the Black-Scholes PDE
QUESTION:
Derive the Black-Scholes partial differential equation for the price $V(S,t)$ of a European option from first principles. State your assumptions.
HINT1:
Assume the stock follows geometric Brownian motion and build a portfolio of the option and some shares whose value has no random part over a short time step.
HINT2:
Hold one option and $-\Delta$ shares. Use Itô's lemma for $dV$, choose $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S$ to cancel the $dW$ terms, and then argue that a riskless portfolio must earn the risk-free rate $r$.
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2 \dfrac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + rS\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0$ with terminal condition $V(S,T) = \text{payoff}(S)$; the drift $\mu$ does not appear.
REFRESH:
Geometric Brownian motion: $dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t$, $W$ a standard Brownian motion ($W_{t+h} - W_t \sim N(0,h)$, independent increments).
Itô's lemma (heuristic): for smooth $f(S,t)$, $df = f_t\,dt + f_S\,dS + \tfrac12 f_{SS}(dS)^2$, with the rules $(dW)^2 = dt$, $dt\,dW = 0$, $(dt)^2 = 0$; so $(dS)^2 = \sigma^2S^2\,dt$.
No arbitrage: a portfolio with no risk over $[t, t+dt]$ must earn exactly $r$.
EXPLANATION:
The key insight is that the option and the stock are driven by the same single source of randomness $dW$. So over an instant you can hold the right number of shares, $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S$, to cancel the option's randomness exactly. The resulting portfolio is riskless, and no arbitrage forces it to grow at the risk-free rate. Writing that down gives a PDE in which the stock's expected return $\mu$ has vanished — the option price does not depend on anyone's view of the stock's drift, only on its volatility. The second-derivative term comes from Itô's lemma: because $(dW)^2 = dt$ is not negligible, the convexity of $V$ in $S$ (its gamma) contributes a deterministic drift $\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\,dt$.
SOLUTION: Delta-hedged portfolio
1. **Assumptions.** $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$ with constant $\mu, \sigma$; constant risk-free rate $r$; continuous trading, no transaction costs, shares divisible, short selling allowed; no dividends; no arbitrage; $V(S,t)$ smooth.
2. **Itô's lemma for the option.** Since $(dS)^2 = \sigma^2S^2\,dt$,
$$dV = V_t\,dt + V_S\,dS + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\,dt = \big(V_t + \mu S V_S + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\big)dt + \sigma S V_S\,dW.$$
3. **Portfolio.** $\Pi = V - \Delta S$, held with $\Delta$ fixed over $[t, t+dt]$ (self-financing). Then
$$d\Pi = dV - \Delta\,dS = \big(V_t + \mu S V_S + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS} - \Delta\mu S\big)dt + \sigma S(V_S - \Delta)\,dW.$$
4. **Remove the risk.** Choose $\Delta = V_S$. The $dW$ term vanishes and so does the $\mu$ term:
$$d\Pi = \big(V_t + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\big)dt.$$
5. **No arbitrage.** $\Pi$ is riskless over $dt$, so it must earn the risk-free rate: $d\Pi = r\Pi\,dt = r(V - S V_S)\,dt$. (If it earned more, borrow at $r$ and buy it; if less, short it and lend.)
6. **Equate.** $V_t + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS} = rV - rSV_S$, i.e.
$$V_t + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS} + rSV_S - rV = 0.$$
7. **Boundary condition.** $V(S,T) = (S - K)^+$ for a call, $(K - S)^+$ for a put. Solving gives the Black-Scholes formula $C = S N(d_1) - Ke^{-r(T-t)}N(d_2)$, $d_{1,2} = \dfrac{\ln(S/K) + (r \pm \sigma^2/2)(T-t)}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}}$.
SOLUTION: Replication (stock plus bond)
1. Try to replicate the option with $a_t$ shares and $b_t$ in a bank account $B$ with $dB = rB\,dt$: $X = aS + bB$, self-financing so $dX = a\,dS + b\,rB\,dt$.
2. Write $dX = \big(a\mu S + r(X - aS)\big)dt + a\sigma S\,dW$.
3. Match $dX$ with $dV$ from Itô's lemma: the $dW$ terms give $a = V_S$; the $dt$ terms with $X = V$ give $V_t + \mu S V_S + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2 V_{SS} = \mu S V_S + r(V - S V_S)$.
4. The $\mu S V_S$ terms cancel, leaving the same PDE $V_t + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS} + rSV_S - rV = 0$. If the option traded at any price other than $X_0$, buying the cheaper of (option, replicating portfolio) and selling the other would be an arbitrage.
===END===

TITLE: How to model the volatility smile
QUESTION:
Implied volatilities of listed options vary with strike (a smile or skew) and with maturity. How would you model the volatility smile? Compare the main approaches.
HINT1:
Black-Scholes assumes one constant volatility, so it can produce only a flat implied-vol surface. What assumption would you relax to create a smile?
HINT2:
Three families: make volatility a deterministic function of spot and time; make volatility itself random (possibly correlated with the stock); add jumps. There are also purely descriptive parametrisations of the surface.
ANSWER:
Local volatility (Dupire: $\sigma(S,t)$ calibrated exactly to the surface), stochastic volatility (Heston, SABR: random vol correlated with spot, gives skew via correlation and smile via vol-of-vol), jump-diffusion (Merton, Bates: short-dated skew), or a parametric fit (SVI) for interpolation; choice depends on the product and on the dynamics you need.
REFRESH:
Implied volatility: the $\sigma$ that makes the Black-Scholes price equal the market price of an option.
Risk-neutral density from calls: $q(K) = e^{rT}\,\dfrac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}$ (Breeden-Litzenberger).
EXPLANATION:
The smile is the market saying the risk-neutral distribution of the stock is not lognormal: fatter tails and, for equities, a heavier left tail (crashes). Any model that generates those features produces a smile. Local vol puts the whole effect into a deterministic volatility $\sigma(S,t)$: it can fit every quoted option exactly, but it predicts that the smile flattens and moves unrealistically as spot moves. Stochastic vol makes volatility random: negative correlation between stock and vol creates the equity skew (vol goes up when the stock falls), and the vol-of-vol creates curvature; its forward smile dynamics are more realistic but it cannot fit very short maturities well. Jumps fix the short end, because a diffusion cannot move far in a short time. Practitioners often combine them (stochastic local vol, SVJ) and use parametric fits like SVI to interpolate the surface without arbitrage.
SOLUTION: The main approaches
1. **Why a smile exists.** Black-Scholes with constant $\sigma$ gives a lognormal terminal distribution and a flat implied-vol surface. Equity markets show downside skew (fear of crashes, leverage effect); FX shows more symmetric smiles.
2. **Parametric surface fits** (descriptive): SVI (stochastic volatility inspired) total variance $w(k) = a + b\big(\rho(k - m) + \sqrt{(k-m)^2 + s^2}\big)$ in log-moneyness $k$, fitted per maturity with no-arbitrage constraints. Good for interpolation and risk, not a dynamic model.
3. **Local volatility** (Dupire 1994): $dS = rS\,dt + \sigma(S,t) S\,dW$. Given a continuum of call prices $C(K,T)$ (zero dividends),
$$\sigma^2(K,T) = \frac{\partial_T C + rK\,\partial_K C}{\tfrac12 K^2\,\partial_{KK} C}.$$
Pros: fits the whole surface exactly; complete market, so hedging is simple. Cons: unrealistic smile dynamics (forward smiles flatten, wrong delta for skew), sensitive to noisy prices.
4. **Stochastic volatility.** Heston: $dS = rS\,dt + \sqrt{v}S\,dW_1$, $dv = \kappa(\theta - v)dt + \xi\sqrt v\,dW_2$, $d\langle W_1,W_2\rangle = \rho\,dt$. $\rho < 0$ gives skew, $\xi$ gives curvature, $\kappa, \theta$ the term structure; semi-closed-form prices via Fourier. SABR: $dF = \alpha F^\beta dW_1$, $d\alpha = \nu\alpha\,dW_2$, with Hagan's closed-form implied-vol approximation; the standard for rates smiles per expiry. Cons: struggles with steep short-dated skew; market incomplete (vol risk must be hedged with options).
5. **Jumps.** Merton jump-diffusion, Bates (Heston + jumps), or pure-jump Lévy (variance gamma). Jumps produce steep short-maturity skew that diffusions cannot.
6. **Hybrids.** Stochastic local volatility: $\sigma = L(S,t)\sqrt{v_t}$ with leverage function $L$ calibrated to the surface exactly, while $v_t$ gives realistic dynamics — the industry standard for exotic pricing.
7. **How to choose:** vanilla interpolation and risk → SVI; path-dependent exotics sensitive to forward skew (cliquets, barriers) → stochastic or stochastic-local vol; short-dated or event-driven → include jumps; rates → SABR per expiry.

TITLE: Risk-neutral versus real-world probabilities
QUESTION:
Explain the difference between the risk-neutral probability measure $\mathbb Q$ and the real-world (physical) probability measure $\mathbb P$. When do you use each?
HINT1:
Start with a one-period model: a stock at 100 goes to 120 or 90, and a bond pays interest $r$. Can you price a call without knowing the real probability of going up?
HINT2:
Find the unique probability $q$ that makes the discounted stock price a fair game, $100 = \frac{1}{1+r}\big(q \cdot 120 + (1-q)\cdot 90\big)$, and show that pricing the option with $q$ gives the replication price.
ANSWER:
$\mathbb P$ describes how likely outcomes really are (used for forecasting, risk, VaR, strategy); $\mathbb Q$ is an equivalent measure under which all discounted traded prices are martingales (every asset drifts at $r$) — it is a pricing device implied by no-arbitrage and replication, with the risk premium absorbed into the probabilities.
REFRESH:
Equivalent measures: $\mathbb P$ and $\mathbb Q$ agree on which events have probability zero.
Martingale: $E[X_{t+s}\mid\mathcal F_t] = X_t$.
Fundamental theorem of asset pricing: no arbitrage iff there exists an equivalent measure under which discounted prices are martingales; it is unique iff the market is complete.
EXPLANATION:
Under $\mathbb P$ a risky stock is expected to grow at $\mu > r$ because investors demand a risk premium. If you priced a derivative as the $\mathbb P$-expected discounted payoff, you would need to know that premium and the right discount rate for this particular risk — hard. Replication avoids this: a derivative that can be copied by trading the stock and the bond must cost what the copy costs, whatever the real probabilities. The risk-neutral measure is just the bookkeeping that makes this automatic: it tilts the probabilities so that every traded asset has expected return $r$, and then price = discounted $\mathbb Q$-expectation of the payoff. The two measures agree on what is possible, but not on how likely it is — in Black-Scholes they share the same volatility $\sigma$ and differ only in the drift ($\mu$ under $\mathbb P$, $r$ under $\mathbb Q$).
SOLUTION: One-period binomial illustration
1. Stock 100 today, 120 (up) or 90 (down) next period; bond rate $r = 5\%$. Call with strike 100 pays 20 or 0. Real probability of up: $p$ (say 0.8).
2. **Replication:** hold $a$ shares and $b$ in bonds with $120a + 1.05b = 20$ and $90a + 1.05b = 0$. Subtract: $30a = 20$, $a = 2/3$; $b = -60/1.05 \approx -57.14$. Cost: $100 \cdot \tfrac23 - 57.14 \approx 9.52$. This is the no-arbitrage price, and $p$ never appeared.
3. **Risk-neutral probability:** solve $100 = \tfrac{1}{1.05}\big(120q + 90(1-q)\big)$: $105 = 90 + 30q$, $q = 0.5$. Then $\tfrac{1}{1.05}(0.5 \cdot 20) = 9.52$, the same price.
4. **Real-world expectation:** $\tfrac{1}{1.05}(0.8 \cdot 20) = 15.24 \ne 9.52$ — discounting at $r$ under $\mathbb P$ is wrong, because the call is riskier than the bond and should be discounted at a higher rate that we do not know a priori.
5. **Continuous time:** under $\mathbb P$, $dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW^{\mathbb P}$; under $\mathbb Q$, $dS = rS\,dt + \sigma S\,dW^{\mathbb Q}$ with $W^{\mathbb Q}_t = W^{\mathbb P}_t + \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma}t$ (Girsanov); $\frac{\mu - r}{\sigma}$ is the market price of risk. Price $= e^{-rT}E^{\mathbb Q}[\text{payoff}]$.
6. **When to use which:** $\mathbb Q$ for pricing and hedging derivatives relative to traded instruments (it is what implied vols and option prices encode). $\mathbb P$ for forecasting returns, risk management (VaR, stress tests), portfolio construction and judging whether an option is cheap relative to your view of realised volatility. Implied (risk-neutral) default probabilities from CDS, for example, are typically much larger than historical default rates because they include a risk premium.

TITLE: Building a pairs trade
QUESTION:
How would you build a pairs trading strategy, from choosing the pair to entry, exit and risk management?
HINT1:
You want two assets whose prices individually wander but whose combination tends to come back to a stable level.
HINT2:
Estimate a hedge ratio $\beta$ from $\log P^A_t = \alpha + \beta\log P^B_t + \varepsilon_t$, test whether the spread $\varepsilon_t$ is stationary (cointegration), and trade its z-score.
ANSWER:
Choose economically linked assets, estimate a hedge ratio, test that the spread is stationary (Engle-Granger or Johansen cointegration), then short the spread when its z-score is high and buy it when low (e.g. enter at ±2, exit near 0, stop at ±4 or on a time limit), with out-of-sample validation, costs and a break-down stop.
REFRESH:
Cointegration: two non-stationary (unit-root) series $X, Y$ are cointegrated if some combination $Y - \beta X$ is stationary.
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck / AR(1) spread: $s_{t+1} - \mu = \phi(s_t - \mu) + \varepsilon$, half-life of mean reversion $= \ln 2 / (-\ln\phi)$.
EXPLANATION:
Two related stocks (Coca-Cola and Pepsi, two share classes of the same company, a stock and its ETF) are exposed to the same economic forces, so although each price is a random walk, the difference (suitably weighted) should be tethered. When the spread is unusually wide, bet on it narrowing: short the rich leg and buy the cheap leg in the ratio $\beta$, which also makes the position roughly market-neutral. The statistics check that the tether exists (cointegration, not mere correlation — two correlated random walks can drift apart forever), and the half-life tells you how long a trade should take. The main risk is that the relationship breaks (a merger, a lawsuit, a change of business), so you need stops.
SOLUTION: Step-by-step recipe
1. **Universe and economics:** candidates from the same sector, same supply chain, dual listings, or stock vs index/ETF. Require a reason for the link.
2. **Screen:** on a formation window (e.g. 1–2 years of daily log prices), compute distances or correlations of returns to shortlist.
3. **Hedge ratio:** regress $\log P^A_t = \alpha + \beta\log P^B_t + \varepsilon_t$ (OLS, or total least squares, or a Kalman filter for a time-varying $\beta$).
4. **Cointegration test:** ADF on the residuals $\hat\varepsilon_t$ with Engle-Granger critical values (or Johansen for more than two assets). Reject the unit root → tradeable spread. Also estimate the half-life from an AR(1) fit; discard pairs with half-lives too long for your horizon.
5. **Signal:** $z_t = (s_t - \bar s)/\hat\sigma_s$ with rolling mean and SD. Enter short spread (short A, long $\beta$ of B) when $z > 2$; long spread when $z < -2$; exit when $z$ crosses 0 (or ±0.5).
6. **Risk management:** stop loss at $|z| > 4$ or after 2–3 half-lives without reversion (relationship may have broken); position size by spread volatility; diversify across many pairs; dollar- or beta-neutral legs.
7. **Validation:** test out of sample (walk-forward), include transaction costs, borrow fees and slippage, beware of data-snooping when screening thousands of pairs (many will look cointegrated by chance).
8. **Monitoring:** re-estimate $\beta$ and re-test cointegration periodically; corporate events can break the pair overnight.
