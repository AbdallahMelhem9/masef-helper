TITLE: Seven ages around a round table
QUESTION:
Seven people, all of different ages, sit down at random around a circular table with seven seats. What is the probability that, going around the table, they are seated in increasing order of age? Either direction counts: the ages may increase clockwise or counterclockwise.
HINT1:
On a round table, what matters is who sits next to whom, not which physical seat anyone takes. How many genuinely different circular arrangements are there?
HINT2:
Fix the youngest person's seat to remove the rotational symmetry. The other six people can then be arranged in $6!$ equally likely ways. How many of these give increasing ages clockwise, and how many counterclockwise?
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{2}{6!} = \dfrac{1}{360}$
REFRESH:
**Circular arrangements**: $n$ distinct people around a round table (seats identical up to rotation) can be arranged in $(n-1)!$ distinct ways. If the seats are labelled, there are $n!$ seatings, and each circular arrangement corresponds to exactly $n$ of them (its $n$ rotations).
EXPLANATION:
The trick is to kill the symmetry: wherever the youngest person sits, look at the table from their seat. Everyone else is then placed relative to them, and all $6! = 720$ relative orderings are equally likely. Being "in increasing order" pins down the arrangement completely once a direction is chosen: going clockwise from the youngest you must meet the 2nd youngest, the 3rd, …, the oldest. There are exactly two such arrangements (clockwise or counterclockwise), so the probability is $2/720$.
SOLUTION: Fix the youngest person
Condition on the seat taken by the youngest person (call them 1); by symmetry this seat does not matter. Reading the other seats clockwise starting next to person 1, the remaining six people $2,\dots,7$ occupy the six seats in a uniformly random order, so each of the $6! = 720$ orders has probability $1/720$.

Ages increase clockwise iff the order read clockwise is $2,3,4,5,6,7$: one order.
Ages increase counterclockwise iff the order read clockwise is $7,6,5,4,3,2$: one order.

These are distinct, so
$$P = \frac{2}{720} = \frac{1}{360}.$$
SOLUTION: Count labelled seatings
Label the seats $1$ to $7$. There are $7! = 5040$ equally likely seatings. An increasing clockwise arrangement is determined by the seat of the youngest person ($7$ choices), and likewise for counterclockwise ($7$ choices), giving $14$ favourable seatings:
$$P = \frac{14}{5040} = \frac{1}{360}.$$
===END===

TITLE: First multiple of 77 above 700,000
QUESTION:
Find the smallest multiple of $77$ that is greater than or equal to $700\,000$. Explain why no smaller multiple of $77$ works.
HINT1:
You want the smallest integer $n$ with $77n \ge 700\,000$. Is there a product involving $77$ that you already know is very close to a power of ten?
HINT2:
$7 \times 11 \times 13 = 1001$, so $77 \times 13 = 1001$. Alternatively, $11 \times 9091 = 100\,001$. Either way, find a multiple of $77$ just around $700\,000$, then check the neighbours.
ANSWER:
$700\,007 = 77 \times 9091$
REFRESH:
**Ceiling division**: the smallest multiple of $d$ that is $\ge N$ is $d\lceil N/d\rceil$. If $N = qd + r$ with $0 \le r < d$, it equals $N$ when $r=0$ and $N + (d - r)$ otherwise.
EXPLANATION:
We need the remainder of $700\,000$ modulo $77$: the answer is $700\,000$ plus whatever is missing to reach the next multiple. The shortcut is that $100\,001 = 11 \times 9091$, so $700\,007 = 7 \times 100\,001 = 7 \times 11 \times 9091 = 77 \times 9091$ is a multiple of $77$ sitting just $7$ above $700\,000$. The previous multiple is $77$ lower, i.e. $699\,930$, which is below $700\,000$, so $700\,007$ is the smallest.
SOLUTION: Division with remainder
Divide: $77 \times 9000 = 693\,000$, leaving $700\,000 - 693\,000 = 7\,000$. Then $77 \times 90 = 6\,930$, leaving $70$. Since $70 < 77$,
$$700\,000 = 77 \times 9090 + 70.$$
So $77 \times 9090 = 699\,930 < 700\,000$, and the next multiple is
$$77 \times 9091 = 699\,930 + 77 = 700\,007 \ge 700\,000.$$
Any multiple $77n$ with $n \le 9090$ is at most $699\,930 < 700\,000$, so none smaller works. The answer is $700\,007$.
SOLUTION: Using 100001 = 11 × 9091
Since $10^5 \equiv -1 \pmod{11}$ (because $10 \equiv -1$ and $5$ is odd), $11$ divides $100\,001$; indeed $100\,001 = 11 \times 9091$. Then
$$700\,007 = 7 \times 100\,001 = 77 \times 9091.$$
It is a multiple of $77$, only $7$ above $700\,000$. The multiple below it is $700\,007 - 77 = 699\,930 < 700\,000$. Hence $700\,007$ is the smallest multiple of $77$ that is at least $700\,000$.
FOLLOWUP: The 70,000 version
What is the smallest multiple of $77$ that is not less than $70\,000$?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$70\,070$. Using $77 \times 13 = 1001$: $77 \times 910 = 77 \times 13 \times 70 = 1001 \times 70 = 70\,070$. The previous multiple is $70\,070 - 77 = 69\,993 < 70\,000$, so $70\,070$ is the smallest. (Equivalently $70\,000 = 77 \times 909 + 7$, so we need $77 - 7 = 70$ more.)

TITLE: Three pancakes, burnt side up
QUESTION:
There are three pancakes: one is burnt on both sides, one is burnt on exactly one side, and one is not burnt at all. They are stacked in a random order, each with a random side facing up, and you can see only the top side of the top pancake. That side is burnt. What is the probability that the top pancake is the one burnt on both sides (i.e. that its hidden side is burnt too)?
HINT1:
The intuitive answer "it is one of two pancakes, so 1/2" is wrong. Think about what you actually observe: a *side*, not a pancake.
HINT2:
There are six equally likely (pancake, side-up) possibilities for the top. Count how many of them show a burnt side, and how many of those belong to the doubly burnt pancake.
ANSWER:
$2/3$
REFRESH:
**Bayes' rule**: $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}$, where by total probability $P(B) = \sum_i P(B \mid A_i) P(A_i)$ for a partition $(A_i)$.
EXPLANATION:
The evidence "burnt side up" is twice as likely to come from the doubly burnt pancake (it shows burnt whatever way it lies) as from the singly burnt one (it shows burnt only half the time). The unburnt pancake is ruled out. Starting from equal prior weights $1/3, 1/3$, the likelihoods $1$ and $1/2$ reweight the odds to $2 : 1$ in favour of the doubly burnt pancake, i.e. probability $2/3$. The tempting answer $1/2$ ignores that the two candidates don't explain the evidence equally well.
SOLUTION: Count the burnt sides
Each pancake is equally likely to be on top, and each of its two sides is equally likely to face up, so the visible top side is a uniformly random one of the $6$ sides.

Of these $6$ sides, $3$ are burnt: two belong to the doubly burnt pancake (call it BB) and one to the singly burnt pancake (BU). Given that we see a burnt side, it is uniformly one of these $3$, and in $2$ of the $3$ cases the pancake is BB:
$$P(\text{BB} \mid \text{burnt up}) = \frac{2}{3}.$$
SOLUTION: Bayes' rule
Priors for the top pancake: $P(\text{BB}) = P(\text{BU}) = P(\text{UU}) = 1/3$. Likelihoods of seeing a burnt side: $1$, $1/2$, $0$. Then
$$P(\text{burnt up}) = \tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac13 \cdot 0 = \tfrac12,$$
$$P(\text{BB} \mid \text{burnt up}) = \frac{\tfrac13 \cdot 1}{\tfrac12} = \frac23.$$
FOLLOWUP: Three boxes of balls
Three boxes: one holds two white balls, one holds two black balls, one holds one white and one black ball. You pick a box at random and draw one ball at random from it. It is white. What is the probability that the other ball in that box is also white?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$2/3$. This is the same problem: there are $6$ equally likely balls, $3$ are white, and $2$ of those $3$ sit in the white–white box. By Bayes: $\dfrac{\frac13 \cdot 1}{\frac13 \cdot 1 + \frac13 \cdot \frac12} = \dfrac23$.
FOLLOWUP: Draw again with replacement
Three bowls: one with $2$ blue balls, one with $2$ red balls, one with $1$ red and $1$ blue. You choose a bowl at random and draw a blue ball. You put the ball back and draw again from the same bowl. What is the probability that the second draw is blue as well?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$5/6$. After the first blue draw, the posterior is $P(\text{blue–blue bowl}) = 2/3$ and $P(\text{mixed bowl}) = 1/3$ (as above). Since the ball is replaced, the second draw is blue with probability $1$ from the blue–blue bowl and $1/2$ from the mixed bowl:
$$\tfrac23 \cdot 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac56.$$
===END===

TITLE: Every other number on a circle of 2000
QUESTION:
The numbers $1, 2, \dots, 2000$ are written clockwise around a circle. Starting at $1$, you go around the circle clockwise, alternately **removing** a number and **keeping** it: remove $1$, keep $2$, remove $3$, keep $4$, and so on. The alternation continues without interruption as you go round and round (skipping numbers already removed), until every number has been removed. Which number is removed last?
HINT1:
Try small circles ($n = 2, 3, 4, 5, 8$) and look for a pattern. What happens to the circle after one complete trip around?
HINT2:
After you remove $1$, you are left with a circle of $1999$ numbers starting at $2$ in which the *first* number is kept. That is the classic Josephus problem ("$1$ kills $2$, $3$ kills $4$, …"). For that problem, if $m = 2^a + \ell$ with $0 \le \ell < 2^a$, the survivor is in position $2\ell + 1$.
ANSWER:
$1952$
REFRESH:
**Josephus problem ($k=2$)**: $m$ people in a circle, the first is kept, the second eliminated, and so on alternately. Writing $m = 2^a + \ell$ with $0 \le \ell < 2^a$, the survivor is $J(m) = 2\ell + 1$. It follows from $J(2m) = 2J(m) - 1$, $J(2m+1) = 2J(m)+1$, $J(1) = 1$.
EXPLANATION:
The last number removed is the one that "survives" everything else. After the very first removal (number $1$), we are in the standard Josephus setup on the $1999$ numbers $2, 3, \dots, 2000$, where the first one ($2$) is kept. The key fact of Josephus: when the count is a power of two, one full lap removes exactly half and the starting person is again first in line, so the starting person survives. With $m = 2^a + \ell$, you just eliminate $\ell$ people first; the person you are standing at then is the survivor, and that is position $2\ell + 1$. Here $1999 = 1024 + 975$, so the survivor is at position $2 \cdot 975 + 1 = 1951$ of the list $2,3,\dots,2000$, i.e. the number $1952$.
SOLUTION: Reduce to the Josephus formula
Step 1. Remove $1$. The remaining numbers $2, 3, \dots, 2000$ form a circle of $m = 1999$ numbers, and the alternation continues with "keep $2$, remove $3$, keep $4$, …". Relabel them $1, \dots, 1999$ (label $j$ stands for number $j+1$). This is exactly the Josephus problem where the first is kept.

Step 2. Josephus with $m = 2^a$: in one lap all even labels are removed, and since $m$ is even the last label $m$ is removed, so the next lap starts again by keeping label $1$. The circle halves with label $1$ still first, so by induction label $1$ survives: $J(2^a) = 1$.

Step 3. General $m = 2^a + \ell$, $0 \le \ell < 2^a$: after $\ell$ removals (labels $2, 4, \dots, 2\ell$), $2^a$ labels remain and the next one to be kept is label $2\ell + 1$. By Step 2 it survives: $J(m) = 2\ell + 1$.

Step 4. $1999 = 1024 + 975$, so $J(1999) = 2 \cdot 975 + 1 = 1951$. Label $1951$ is the number $1952$.

So $1952$ is the last number removed.

Note: the source states the answer $20$; the correct value is $1952$ (checked by direct simulation). Even if the rule is read as "restart each new lap by removing", the answer would be $1024$, never $20$.
SOLUTION: Halving recursion
Let $f(n)$ be the last number removed when the circle is $1, \dots, n$ and we start by removing $1$.

If $n$ is even, the first lap removes all odd numbers and keeps $n$; the next number, $2$, is then removed. So we have the same game on $2, 4, \dots, n$ starting by removing $2$: $f(n) = 2f(n/2)$.

If $n = 2k+1$ is odd, the first lap removes $1, 3, \dots, 2k+1$ and the next number $2$ is kept: the game continues on $2, 4, \dots, 2k$ starting by keeping $2$. This equals: remove $4$ first on the circle $4, 6, \dots, 2k, 2$, so $f(2k+1) = 2\,\big(f(k) + 1\big)$ with indices taken cyclically (i.e. $2(f(k)+1)$, replaced by $2$ if $f(k) = k$).

Now $f(2000) = 2 f(1000) = 4 f(500) = 8 f(250) = 16 f(125)$, and $125 = 2\cdot 62 + 1$ gives $f(125) = 2(f(62)+1)$; $f(62) = 2f(31)$; $f(31) = 2(f(15)+1)$; $f(15) = 2(f(7)+1)$; $f(7) = 2(f(3)+1)$; $f(3) = 2(f(1)+1)$ with cyclic rule: $f(1) = 1 = k$, so $f(3) = 2$. Check: circle $1,2,3$: remove $1$, keep $2$, remove $3$, remove $2$. Yes, $f(3) = 2$.

Then $f(7) = 2(2+1) = 6$, $f(15) = 2(6+1) = 14$, $f(31) = 2(14+1) = 30$, $f(62) = 60$, $f(125) = 2(60+1) = 122$, and
$$f(2000) = 16 \times 122 = 1952.$$
FOLLOWUP: 100 people, 1 eliminates 2
One hundred people stand in a circle, labelled $1$ to $100$. Starting with person $1$, each living person eliminates the next living person clockwise: $1$ eliminates $2$, $3$ eliminates $4$, and so on around the circle. Who survives?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$73$. This is Josephus with the first person kept: $100 = 64 + 36$, so the survivor is $2 \cdot 36 + 1 = 73$. (After $36$ eliminations, $64$ people remain and it is person $73$'s turn; with a power of two remaining, whoever starts survives.)

FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$976$. After removing $1$ we have Josephus on $999$ numbers $2, \dots, 1000$ with the first kept. $999 = 512 + 487$, so the survivor is in position $2\cdot 487 + 1 = 975$ of that list, which is the number $976$.

TITLE: HTH versus HHT
QUESTION:
You and a friend flip a fair coin repeatedly and write down the results. You win if the pattern $HTH$ appears before $HHT$; your friend wins if $HHT$ appears first. What is the probability that your friend wins?
HINT1:
Both patterns start with $H$. Think about what can happen once two heads in a row have appeared.
HINT2:
Set up a small Markov chain whose state is the useful suffix of the flips so far: $\varnothing$, $H$, $HH$, $HT$. Write one equation per state for the probability that $HHT$ wins from there.
ANSWER:
$2/3$
REFRESH:
**First-step analysis**: for a Markov chain and a target event, let $p_s$ be the probability of the event starting from state $s$. Then $p_s = \sum_{s'} P(s \to s')\,p_{s'}$, with boundary values $1$ on winning states and $0$ on losing ones.
EXPLANATION:
The patterns have the same length and each appears with probability $1/8$ at any given position, yet the race is not fair. The reason is what happens at $HH$: once two heads in a row appear, $HHT$ is guaranteed to win, because extra heads keep you at $HH$ and the first tail completes $HHT$, while $HTH$ would need a tail followed by a head. On the other side, $HTH$ can be ruined: from $HT$ a tail sends you back to the start. So $HHT$ has a "trap" state in its favour, and working out the equations gives it $2/3$.
SOLUTION: Markov chain on suffixes
States (the relevant end of the sequence): $S$ (start / nothing useful), $H$, $HH$, $HT$. Let $p_X$ be the probability that $HHT$ comes first from state $X$.

- From $HH$: a $T$ completes $HHT$; an $H$ leaves us at $HH$. So $p_{HH} = 1$.
- From $H$: $H \to HH$, $T \to HT$: $p_H = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 p_{HT}$.
- From $HT$: $H$ completes $HTH$ (friend loses), $T$ gives $HTT$, which has no useful suffix: $p_{HT} = \tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12 p_S$.
- From $S$: $H \to H$, $T \to S$: $p_S = \tfrac12 p_H + \tfrac12 p_S$, so $p_S = p_H$.

Substituting: $p_H = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 p_H = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 p_H$, so $\tfrac34 p_H = \tfrac12$ and $p_H = \tfrac23$.

Hence your friend (HHT) wins with probability $2/3$.
SOLUTION: Conway's correlation numbers
For patterns $A, B$ define the correlation $AB$ as the binary number whose $k$-th digit (from the left, $k = $ pattern length down to $1$) is $1$ if the last $k$ letters of $A$ equal the first $k$ letters of $B$. Conway's rule: the odds that $B$ appears before $A$ are $(AA - AB) : (BB - BA)$.

With $A = HTH$, $B = HHT$:
- $AA$: suffixes of $HTH$ vs prefixes of $HTH$: length 3 yes, 2 ($TH$ vs $HT$) no, 1 ($H$ vs $H$) yes: $101_2 = 5$.
- $AB$: $HTH$ suffixes vs $HHT$ prefixes: 3 no, 2 ($TH$ vs $HH$) no, 1 yes: $001_2 = 1$.
- $BB$: $HHT$ vs $HHT$: 3 yes, 2 ($HT$ vs $HH$) no, 1 ($T$ vs $H$) no: $100_2 = 4$.
- $BA$: $HHT$ suffixes vs $HTH$ prefixes: 3 no, 2 ($HT$ vs $HT$) yes, 1 ($T$ vs $H$) no: $010_2 = 2$.

Odds that $HHT$ comes first: $(5-1):(4-2) = 4:2 = 2:1$, i.e. probability $2/3$.
FOLLOWUP: HHT versus THH
A fair coin is flipped repeatedly. What is the probability that $HHT$ appears before $THH$?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$1/4$. If the first two flips are $HH$, then $HHT$ must win (the first tail completes it, and $THH$ would need a $T$ before an $HH$, which is impossible before the $HHT$ is complete). This has probability $1/4$. Otherwise a $T$ appears before any $HH$ has occurred; from then on, the first time $HH$ appears it is preceded by a $T$, which completes $THH$ before $HHT$ can finish. So $P(HHT \text{ first}) = 1/4$. Check with Conway's rule, $A = THH$, $B = HHT$: $AA = 100_2 = 4$, $AB = 011_2 = 3$, $BB = 100_2 = 4$, $BA = 001_2 = 1$, so the odds that $HHT$ comes first are $(4-3):(4-1) = 1:3$, i.e. probability $1/4$.
===END===

Each round uses exactly two tosses. How likely is a round to settle the question?
HINT2:
The number of rounds is geometric with success probability $3/4$.
ANSWER:
$8/3$ tosses
REFRESH:
**Geometric distribution**: if each trial succeeds independently with probability $p$, the number of trials $R$ up to and including the first success satisfies $P(R = r) = (1-p)^{r-1}p$ and $\mathbb{E}[R] = 1/p$.
EXPLANATION:
This is rejection sampling: the four outcomes $HH, HT, TH, TT$ are equally likely; we throw away $TT$ and keep the other three, which remain equally likely, so $HH$ has conditional probability $1/3$. The price is that a round is wasted with probability $1/4$. The number of rounds is geometric with success probability $3/4$, mean $4/3$ rounds, i.e. $2 \times 4/3 = 8/3$ tosses.
SOLUTION: Geometric number of rounds
A round (two tosses) ends the procedure unless it is $TT$, so it ends with probability $p = 3/4$, independently of previous rounds. The number of rounds $R$ is geometric: $\mathbb{E}[R] = 1/p = 4/3$. Each round costs two tosses, so
$$\mathbb{E}[\text{tosses}] = 2\,\mathbb{E}[R] = \frac{8}{3}.$$
SOLUTION: First-step equation
Let $E$ be the expected number of tosses. After the first two tosses, with probability $3/4$ we are done, and with probability $1/4$ we start over:
$$E = 2 + \tfrac14 E \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tfrac34 E = 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E = \tfrac83.$$

Conditional on stopping, the $5$ kept outcomes are equally likely, so $P(\text{event}) = 2/5$. A round succeeds with probability $5/8$, so the expected number of tosses is $3 \cdot 8/5 = 24/5$. (Any dyadic block works: with $2^k$ outcomes, keep a multiple of $5$ of them and split $2:3$.)

$1/72$. The experiment has $6 \times 6 \times 2 = 72$ equally likely outcomes, and any event you can build is a union of them, so its probability is $k/72$ for an integer $k$. The smallest positive value is $1/72$, achieved e.g. by "first die $1$, second die $1$, coin $H$".

QUESTION:

HINT2:

**Perfect correlation**: $\rho(X,Y) = 1$ iff $Y = a + bX$ almost surely with $b = \sigma_Y/\sigma_X > 0$.
EXPLANATION:
If $\rho = 1$ then $Y$ is an increasing affine function of $X$: $Y = a + bX$ with $b = \sigma_Y/\sigma_X$. Then both $X + Y = a + (1+b)X$ and $X - Y = -a + (1-b)X$ are affine functions of the same $X$. Two affine functions of one variable have correlation $\pm 1$, with the sign being the sign of the product of the slopes. Since $1 + b > 0$, the sign is that of $1 - b$, i.e. positive when $\sigma_Y < \sigma_X$. The answer is $1$.
SOLUTION: Direct covariance computation
With $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y = \sigma_X\sigma_Y$:
$$\operatorname{Cov}(X+Y, X-Y) = \sigma_X^2 - \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) + \operatorname{Cov}(Y,X) - \sigma_Y^2 = \sigma_X^2 - \sigma_Y^2.$$
$$\operatorname{Var}(X+Y) = \sigma_X^2 + 2\sigma_X\sigma_Y + \sigma_Y^2 = (\sigma_X+\sigma_Y)^2,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X-Y) = (\sigma_X - \sigma_Y)^2.$$
So
$$\rho(X+Y, X-Y) = \frac{\sigma_X^2 - \sigma_Y^2}{(\sigma_X + \sigma_Y)\,|\sigma_X - \sigma_Y|} = \frac{(\sigma_X-\sigma_Y)(\sigma_X+\sigma_Y)}{(\sigma_X + \sigma_Y)|\sigma_X - \sigma_Y|} = \operatorname{sign}(\sigma_X - \sigma_Y).$$
With $\sigma_Y < \sigma_X$ this equals $1$. (If $\sigma_X = \sigma_Y$, $X - Y$ is constant and the correlation is undefined.)
SOLUTION: Affine representation
Write $Y = a + bX$, $b = \sigma_Y/\sigma_X \in (0,1)$. Then $X+Y = a + (1+b)X$ and $X - Y = -a + (1-b)X$, both increasing functions of $X$ since $1+b > 0$ and $1 - b > 0$. Two increasing affine functions of the same variable have correlation exactly $1$.

FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:

Every bet is fair, so when the pattern first appears at roll $N$, the casino's expected net gain is $0$: $\mathbb{E}[N]$ (the total money brought in) equals the expected total payout. Upper bound. $R^2 \le 1$ always. To attain it, take centred, uncorrelated, unit-variance data vectors $u$ and $v$, and set $c = \sqrt{0.05}$, $s = \sqrt{0.95}$,
$$y = u,\qquad x_1 = c\,u + s\,v,\qquad x_2 = c\,u - s\,v.$$
Then $\operatorname{corr}(y, x_1) = \operatorname{corr}(y, x_2) = c$, so each simple $R^2 = c^2 = 0.05$. But $x_1 + x_2 = 2c\,u = 2c\,y$, so $y = \frac{1}{2c}(x_1 + x_2)$ lies exactly in the span of the regressors and the combined $R^2 = 1$.

So the greatest lower bound is $0.05$ (i.e. $5\%$) and the least upper bound is $1$.
SOLUTION: Formula in terms of the correlation between regressors
With $r_1 = r_2 = r$, $r^2 = 0.05$:
$$R^2 = \frac{2r^2 - 2r^2 r_{12}}{1 - r_{12}^2} = \frac{2r^2(1 - r_{12})}{(1-r_{12})(1+r_{12})} = \frac{0.1}{1 + r_{12}}.$$
The correlation matrix of $(y, x_1, x_2)$ must be positive semidefinite, which here forces $R^2 \le 1$, i.e. $r_{12} \ge -0.9$. As $r_{12}$ ranges over $[-0.9, 1]$, $R^2 = 0.1/(1+r_{12})$ ranges over $[0.05, 1]$: $0.05$ at $r_{12} = 1$ (identical regressors) and $1$ at $r_{12} = -0.9$ (the construction above, where $\operatorname{corr}(x_1,x_2) = c^2 - s^2 = -0.9$). (With $r_1 = -r_2$ the same range comes out with $r_{12} \to -r_{12}$.)
Note: the source's "5" for the lower bound means $5\%$, i.e. $R^2 = 0.05$.

$$P(S + X_n \text{ even}) = P(S \text{ even})\cdot\tfrac12 + P(S \text{ odd})\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12.$$

SOLUTION: Binomial sums
$P(\text{even}) - P(\text{odd}) = \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{k=0}^n \binom nk (-1)^k = \frac{(1-1)^n}{2^n} = 0$ for $n \ge 1$, and $P(\text{even}) + P(\text{odd}) = 1$, so both equal $1/2$. For $n=4$: even counts $\binom40 + \binom42 + \binom44 = 1 + 6 + 1 = 8$ out of $16$.
SOLUTION: Biased coins via the sign trick

$$P(H \text{ even}) - P(H \text{ odd}) = \mathbb{E}\big[(-1)^H\big] = \prod_{i=1}^n (1 - 2p_i).$$
Adding $P(\text{even}) + P(\text{odd}) = 1$:
$$P(H \text{ even}) = \frac{1 + \prod_i (1-2p_i)}{2}.$$

The process continues exactly as long as the sequence is increasing. Split according to the length $k$ of the initial increasing run.
HINT2:
The density of $X_k$ at $x$ on the event $\{X_1 < X_2 < \dots < X_k\}$ is $\frac{x^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}$. Multiply by the probability $x$ that the next draw is $\le x$, sum over $k$, and integrate $x \cdot(\dots)$.

**Useful integral**: $\int_0^1 x^2 e^x\,dx = \big[e^x(x^2 - 2x + 2)\big]_0^1 = e - 2$.
EXPLANATION:
The process stops right after the first descent, and the last record is the top of the initial increasing run. For a run of exact length $k$ ending at height $x$, we need an increasing sequence of length $k$ ending at $x$ (weight $x^{k-1}/(k-1)!$) and then a drop below $x$ (probability $x$). Summing over $k$ turns the $x^{k-1}/(k-1)!$ into $e^x$, so the density of the last record is $x e^x$ on $(0,1)$ (a quick check: $\int_0^1 xe^x dx = 1$). Its mean is $\int_0^1 x^2 e^x\,dx = e - 2$.
SOLUTION: Sum over the run length
For $k \ge 1$, $\{N - 1 = k\} = \{X_1 < \dots < X_k,\ X_{k+1} \le X_k\}$. On this event $X_{N-1} = X_k$. Using the density of $X_k$ on the increasing event and independence of $X_{k+1}$:
$$\mathbb{E}\big[X_{N-1}\mathbf 1_{\{N-1=k\}}\big] = \int_0^1 x \cdot \frac{x^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\cdot x\,dx.$$
Summing over $k \ge 1$ and using $\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{x^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} = e^x$:
$$\mathbb{E}[X_{N-1}] = \int_0^1 x^2 e^x\,dx.$$

$$\int_0^1 x^2 e^x\,dx = \big[e^x(x^2 - 2x + 2)\big]_0^1 = e(1 - 2 + 2) - 2 = e - 2.$$

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{payout}] = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 2^n\cdot 2^{-n} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty 1 = +\infty.$$

Ken tosses a fair coin until the first head. If this takes $k$ tosses, he earns $\min(64, 2^k)$ dollars. What are his expected earnings?

$\$7$. For $k = 1, \dots, 6$ the payout is $2^k$ with probability $2^{-k}$: each contributes $1$, total $6$. In general a cap of $2^m$ gives value $m + 1$: the paradox dissolves once the bank has finite money (a cap of about $\$10^{12} \approx 2^{40}$ gives only about $\$41$).

TITLE: Distance between two uniform points
QUESTION:
Two numbers $x$ and $y$ are chosen independently and uniformly from $[0, 60]$. Find the expected value and the variance of $|x - y|$.
HINT1:
Draw the square $[0,60]^2$. Where is $|x - y| > d$?
HINT2:
$P(|x-y| > d) = \left(1 - \frac{d}{60}\right)^2$: two corner triangles. For the variance, note that $\mathbb{E}[(x-y)^2]$ is easy without absolute values.
ANSWER:
$\mathbb{E}|x-y| = 20$ and $\operatorname{Var}|x - y| = 200$.
REFRESH:
**Uniform moments**: for $U \sim \text{Unif}[0,L]$, $\mathbb{E}U = L/2$ and $\operatorname{Var}U = L^2/12$.
**Tail formula**: for $D \ge 0$, $\mathbb{E}D = \int_0^\infty P(D > d)\,dd$.
EXPLANATION:
In the square $[0,60]^2$, the region where $|x-y| > d$ consists of two right triangles with legs $60 - d$, total area $(60-d)^2$. So $D = |x-y|$ has survival function $(1 - d/60)^2$: it is distributed like $60$ times the minimum of two uniforms, with mean $60/3 = 20$. For the variance, drop the absolute value: $D^2 = (x-y)^2$, whose mean is $2\operatorname{Var}(x) = 2 \cdot 300 = 600$.
SOLUTION: Tail integral and second moment
Scale: $x = 60U$, $y = 60V$ with $U, V$ i.i.d. uniform on $[0,1]$, $D = 60|U - V|$.
$P(|U - V| > t) = (1-t)^2$ for $t \in [0,1]$ (two triangles of legs $1-t$, each of area $(1-t)^2/2$). Hence
$$\mathbb{E}|U - V| = \int_0^1 (1-t)^2\,dt = \frac13, \qquad \mathbb{E}D = \frac{60}{3} = 20.$$
Second moment: $\mathbb{E}[(U-V)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(U - V) = \frac1{12} + \frac1{12} = \frac16$, so $\mathbb{E}D^2 = 3600\cdot\frac16 = 600$.
$$\operatorname{Var}D = 600 - 20^2 = 200.$$
SOLUTION: Density of the difference
$T = |U - V|$ has density $f(t) = 2(1 - t)$ on $[0,1]$ (derivative of $1 - (1-t)^2$). Then $\mathbb{E}T = \int_0^1 2t(1-t)\,dt = 1 - \frac23 = \frac13$ and $\mathbb{E}T^2 = \int_0^1 2t^2(1-t)\,dt = \frac23 - \frac12 = \frac16$, so $\operatorname{Var}T = \frac16 - \frac19 = \frac1{18}$. Scaling by $60$: $\mathbb{E}D = 20$, $\operatorname{Var}D = \frac{3600}{18} = 200$.
FOLLOWUP: Uniforms on [3, 4]
$X, Y$ are i.i.d. uniform on $(3, 4)$. Find $\mathbb{E}|X - Y|$.
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$1/3$. Shifting both by $3$ doesn't change the difference: $X - 3$, $Y - 3$ are i.i.d. uniform on $(0,1)$, and $\mathbb{E}|U - V| = \int_0^1 (1-t)^2 dt = \frac13$. In general, for uniforms on an interval of length $L$, $\mathbb{E}|X - Y| = L/3$.
