IDS: R3S-51, R3S-52, R3S-53, R3S-54
SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Brownian motion
DIFFICULTY: hard
TITLE: Why is Itô's lemma true?
QUESTION:
Why is Itô's lemma true? State it and give an (informal but convincing) argument for it.
HINT1:
Start from an ordinary second-order Taylor expansion of $f(t,X_t)$. In classical calculus the second-order terms disappear in the limit; ask yourself whether that is still true when the driving noise is a Brownian motion.
HINT2:
Over a small step $\Delta t$, the Brownian increment $\Delta W$ has size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, not $\Delta t$. Compute the mean and variance of $\sum_i (\Delta W_i)^2$ over a partition of $[0,t]$ and see what it converges to; then decide which terms of the Taylor expansion survive.
ANSWER:
If $dX_t=\mu\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t$ and $f\in C^{1,2}$, then $df(t,X_t)=\big(f_t+\mu f_x+\tfrac12\sigma^2 f_{xx}\big)dt+\sigma f_x\,dW_t$; the extra $\tfrac12\sigma^2 f_{xx}$ term exists because Brownian motion has quadratic variation $(dW_t)^2=dt$.
REFRESH:
- **Taylor's theorem (order 2)**: $f(x+h)=f(x)+f'(x)h+\tfrac12 f''(\xi)h^2$ for some $\xi$ between $x$ and $x+h$ (for $f\in C^2$).
- **Brownian motion**: $W_0=0$, continuous paths, independent increments, $W_t-W_s\sim\mathcal N(0,t-s)$. In particular $E[(\Delta W)^2]=\Delta t$ and $E[(\Delta W)^4]=3(\Delta t)^2$.
- **Variance of a sum of independent variables** is the sum of the variances.
- **Convergence in $L^2$**: $Y_n\to Y$ in $L^2$ if $E[(Y_n-Y)^2]\to 0$; it suffices that $E[Y_n]\to Y$ (constant) and $\mathrm{Var}(Y_n)\to 0$.
EXPLANATION:
**The statement.** Let $X_t$ be an Itô process, $dX_t=\mu(t,X_t)\,dt+\sigma(t,X_t)\,dW_t$, and let $f(t,x)$ be once continuously differentiable in $t$ and twice in $x$. Then $Y_t=f(t,X_t)$ satisfies
$$dY_t=\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\,dt+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dX_t+\frac12\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\,(dX_t)^2,$$
and with the multiplication rules $(dW_t)^2=dt$, $dt\,dW_t=0$, $(dt)^2=0$,
$$dY_t=\Big(f_t+\mu f_x+\tfrac12\sigma^2 f_{xx}\Big)dt+\sigma f_x\,dW_t .$$

**Why there is an extra term.** Write the ordinary Taylor expansion:
$$df=f_t\,dt+f_x\,dx+\tfrac12 f_{xx}\,(dx)^2+f_{tx}\,dt\,dx+\tfrac12 f_{tt}(dt)^2+\dots$$
In classical calculus $x$ moves smoothly, so $dx$ is of order $dt$ and $(dx)^2$ is of order $(dt)^2$: summed over the $t/dt$ steps of $[0,t]$, such terms add up to something of order $dt\to 0$. They vanish, and we get the usual chain rule.

A Brownian path is much rougher: over a step $dt$, the increment $dW$ has standard deviation $\sqrt{dt}$. So $(dW)^2$ is of order $dt$ — the *same* order as the drift term. Summed over $t/dt$ steps, the squared increments do **not** vanish; they add up to (almost exactly) $t$. That is the whole content of the rule "$(dW)^2=dt$": the sum of squared increments of Brownian motion (its *quadratic variation*) is deterministic and equal to elapsed time. Everything of higher order ($dt\,dW\sim dt^{3/2}$, $(dt)^2$, $(dW)^3\sim dt^{3/2}$) still disappears.

So in the Taylor expansion, the second-order term $\tfrac12 f_{xx}(dX)^2$ survives, and since $(dX)^2=\sigma^2(dW)^2+(\text{negligible})=\sigma^2dt$, it contributes the famous $\tfrac12\sigma^2 f_{xx}\,dt$.

**Why $(dW)^2=dt$ really holds.** Take a partition $0=t_0<t_1<\dots<t_n=t$ with mesh $\delta=\max_i\Delta t_i$ and let $Q_n=\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2$. Then $E[Q_n]=\sum_i\Delta t_i=t$, and by independence
$$\mathrm{Var}(Q_n)=\sum_i\mathrm{Var}\big((\Delta W_i)^2\big)=\sum_i\big(3(\Delta t_i)^2-(\Delta t_i)^2\big)=2\sum_i(\Delta t_i)^2\le 2\delta\,t\longrightarrow 0 .$$
So $Q_n\to t$ in $L^2$: the random sum of squares becomes deterministic. By contrast $E\big[\sum_i\Delta t_i\,|\Delta W_i|\big]=\sum_i\Delta t_i\sqrt{2\Delta t_i/\pi}\le\sqrt{\delta}\,t$ and $\sum_i(\Delta t_i)^2\le\delta t$ both go to $0$, which is the meaning of $dt\,dW=0$ and $(dt)^2=0$.

Interviewers usually want exactly this: Taylor expansion plus the order-of-magnitude argument that $(dW)^2$ behaves like $dt$.
SOLUTION: Telescoping sum with a second-order Taylor expansion
We prove the simplest case $Y_t=f(W_t)$ with $f\in C^2$ and $f''$ bounded (the general case $f(t,X_t)$ follows the same steps with more bookkeeping). Claim:
$$f(W_t)=f(W_0)+\int_0^t f'(W_s)\,dW_s+\frac12\int_0^t f''(W_s)\,ds .$$

**Step 1: telescoping.** Take a partition $0=t_0<\dots<t_n=t$ with mesh $\delta\to 0$, $\Delta W_i=W_{t_{i+1}}-W_{t_i}$. Then
$$f(W_t)-f(W_0)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\big[f(W_{t_{i+1}})-f(W_{t_i})\big].$$

**Step 2: Taylor.** For each $i$ there is $\xi_i$ between $W_{t_i}$ and $W_{t_{i+1}}$ with
$$f(W_{t_{i+1}})-f(W_{t_i})=f'(W_{t_i})\,\Delta W_i+\tfrac12 f''(\xi_i)(\Delta W_i)^2 .$$
So $f(W_t)-f(W_0)=A_n+B_n+C_n$ with
$$A_n=\sum_i f'(W_{t_i})\Delta W_i,\quad B_n=\tfrac12\sum_i f''(W_{t_i})(\Delta W_i)^2,\quad C_n=\tfrac12\sum_i\big[f''(\xi_i)-f''(W_{t_i})\big](\Delta W_i)^2 .$$

**Step 3: first-order term.** $A_n$ is a Riemann-type sum with the integrand evaluated at the *left* endpoint; its limit is by definition the Itô integral $\int_0^t f'(W_s)\,dW_s$.

**Step 4: second-order term.** Compare $B_n$ with the ordinary Riemann sum $\tfrac12\sum_i f''(W_{t_i})\Delta t_i\to\tfrac12\int_0^t f''(W_s)ds$. The difference is
$$D_n=\tfrac12\sum_i g_i\,Z_i,\qquad g_i=f''(W_{t_i}),\quad Z_i=(\Delta W_i)^2-\Delta t_i .$$
$Z_i$ is independent of $\mathcal F_{t_i}$ (hence of $g_i$) and has mean $0$ and variance $2(\Delta t_i)^2$. For $i<j$, $g_ig_jZ_i$ is $\mathcal F_{t_j}$-measurable and $E[Z_j\mid\mathcal F_{t_j}]=0$, so the cross terms vanish:
$$E[D_n^2]=\tfrac14\sum_i E[g_i^2]\,E[Z_i^2]\le\tfrac14\|f''\|_\infty^2\sum_i 2(\Delta t_i)^2\le\tfrac12\|f''\|_\infty^2\,\delta\,t\to 0 .$$
So $B_n\to\tfrac12\int_0^t f''(W_s)\,ds$ in $L^2$.

**Step 5: the remainder.** $|C_n|\le\tfrac12\max_i|f''(\xi_i)-f''(W_{t_i})|\cdot\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2$. The path $s\mapsto W_s$ is continuous on $[0,t]$, so the $\xi_i$ get uniformly close to $W_{t_i}$ and, $f''$ being uniformly continuous on the (bounded) range of the path, the max goes to $0$; meanwhile $\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2\to t$ (quadratic variation, see the Explanation). Hence $C_n\to 0$ in probability.

**Conclusion.** Letting $\delta\to0$,
$$f(W_t)=f(W_0)+\int_0^t f'(W_s)\,dW_s+\frac12\int_0^t f''(W_s)\,ds,$$
i.e. $d f(W_t)=f'(W_t)\,dW_t+\tfrac12 f''(W_t)\,dt$. For a general Itô process the same argument uses $\sum(\Delta X_i)^2\to\int_0^t\sigma^2(s,X_s)\,ds$ and adds the (harmless) $t$-derivative, giving
$$dY_t=\Big(f_t+\mu f_x+\tfrac12\sigma^2 f_{xx}\Big)dt+\sigma f_x\,dW_t .$$
SOLUTION: Check it exactly on f(x) = x², then extend
For $f(x)=x^2$ the telescoping identity is exact, with no remainder:
$$W_t^2=\sum_i\big(W_{t_{i+1}}^2-W_{t_i}^2\big)=\sum_i\big(2W_{t_i}\Delta W_i+(\Delta W_i)^2\big)=2\sum_iW_{t_i}\Delta W_i+\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2 .$$
(Use $b^2-a^2=2a(b-a)+(b-a)^2$.) As the mesh goes to $0$, the first sum converges to $2\int_0^tW_s\,dW_s$ and the second to $t$, so
$$W_t^2=2\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s+t,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad d(W_t^2)=2W_t\,dW_t+dt .$$
This is exactly Itô's formula with $f'=2x$, $f''=2$: the correction $\tfrac12 f''\,dt=dt$ is literally the quadratic variation. Sanity check: $E[W_t^2]=t$, and the Itô integral has mean $0$, so the $+t$ is forced — the classical chain rule $d(W^2)=2W\,dW$ would wrongly give $E[W_t^2]=0$.

The same algebra gives the formula for every monomial $x^k$ (by induction, using the product rule $d(UV)=U\,dV+V\,dU+dU\,dV$), hence for every polynomial by linearity, and finally for every $C^2$ function by approximating $f,f',f''$ uniformly on compacts by polynomials.
FOLLOWUP: The Itô isometry
What is the Itô isometry? Prove that it holds for piecewise-constant (simple) integrands and then for continuous integrands.
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
**Statement.** For an adapted integrand $H$ with $E\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds<\infty$,
$$E\Big[\Big(\int_0^T H_s\,dW_s\Big)^2\Big]=E\Big[\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds\Big].$$
The map $H\mapsto\int H\,dW$ is an isometry from $L^2(\Omega\times[0,T])$ into $L^2(\Omega)$.

**Simple integrands.** Let $H_s=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\xi_i\,\mathbf 1_{(t_i,t_{i+1}]}(s)$ with $\xi_i$ $\mathcal F_{t_i}$-measurable and square-integrable. Then $I=\int_0^T H\,dW=\sum_i\xi_i\Delta W_i$ and
$$E[I^2]=\sum_i E[\xi_i^2(\Delta W_i)^2]+2\sum_{i<j}E[\xi_i\xi_j\Delta W_i\,\Delta W_j].$$
Cross terms ($i<j$): $\xi_i\xi_j\Delta W_i$ is $\mathcal F_{t_j}$-measurable and $\Delta W_j$ is independent of $\mathcal F_{t_j}$ with mean $0$, so $E[\xi_i\xi_j\Delta W_i\Delta W_j]=E\big[\xi_i\xi_j\Delta W_i\,E[\Delta W_j\mid\mathcal F_{t_j}]\big]=0$. Diagonal terms: $E[\xi_i^2(\Delta W_i)^2]=E\big[\xi_i^2E[(\Delta W_i)^2\mid\mathcal F_{t_i}]\big]=E[\xi_i^2](t_{i+1}-t_i)$. Therefore
$$E[I^2]=\sum_iE[\xi_i^2](t_{i+1}-t_i)=E\Big[\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds\Big].$$

**Continuous integrands.** Let $H$ be adapted with continuous paths and (for simplicity) bounded, $|H|\le K$. Define simple approximations $H^{(n)}_s=H_{t_i}$ for $s\in(t_i,t_{i+1}]$ on partitions with mesh $\to0$. By path continuity $H^{(n)}_s\to H_s$ for every $s,\omega$, and $|H^{(n)}-H|^2\le 4K^2$, so by dominated convergence $E\int_0^T(H^{(n)}-H)^2ds\to0$. Applying the simple-case isometry to $H^{(n)}-H^{(m)}$ (which is simple),
$$E\big[(I(H^{(n)})-I(H^{(m)}))^2\big]=E\int_0^T(H^{(n)}-H^{(m)})^2ds\to0,$$
so $I(H^{(n)})$ is Cauchy in $L^2(\Omega)$ and converges; its limit is by definition $\int_0^T H\,dW$. Both sides of the isometry are continuous under these $L^2$ limits (norms converge when the elements converge), so
$$E\Big[\Big(\int_0^TH\,dW\Big)^2\Big]=\lim_n E\big[I(H^{(n)})^2\big]=\lim_nE\int_0^T(H^{(n)})^2ds=E\int_0^TH^2ds .$$
(Unbounded continuous $H$ with $E\int H^2<\infty$ is handled by truncating $H$ at level $K$ and letting $K\to\infty$.) Example: $E[(\int_0^TW_s\,dW_s)^2]=\int_0^Ts\,ds=T^2/2$.
FOLLOWUP: Properties of the Itô integral
What are some other properties of the Itô integral?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
For adapted $H,K$ with $E\int_0^TH^2\,ds<\infty$ (and likewise $K$), with $I_t=\int_0^tH\,dW$:
- **Linearity**: $\int(aH+bK)\,dW=a\int H\,dW+b\int K\,dW$, and additivity over intervals.
- **Zero mean**: $E[I_t]=0$ (true for simple integrands since $E[\xi_i\Delta W_i]=E[\xi_iE[\Delta W_i\mid\mathcal F_{t_i}]]=0$, and it passes to the $L^2$ limit).
- **Isometry and covariance**: $E[I_t^2]=E\int_0^tH^2ds$ and $E\big[\int H\,dW\int K\,dW\big]=E\int HK\,ds$ (polarisation).
- **Martingale**: $t\mapsto I_t$ is a martingale with continuous paths (a genuine martingale under the $L^2$ condition, a local martingale in general).
- **Quadratic variation**: $\langle I\rangle_t=\int_0^tH_s^2\,ds$, so $d I\,dI=H^2dt$; this is what makes Itô's formula work for Itô processes.
- **Gaussian for deterministic integrands**: if $H=h(s)$ is deterministic, $\int_0^th(s)\,dW_s\sim\mathcal N\big(0,\int_0^th^2ds\big)$.
- **Not a pathwise integral**: Brownian paths have infinite total variation, so $\int H\,dW$ is not a Riemann–Stieltjes integral path by path; it is an $L^2$ (or in-probability) limit, and the choice of the left endpoint matters. Evaluating at the midpoint gives the Stratonovich integral, which differs by $\tfrac12\langle H,W\rangle_t$ (e.g. $\int_0^tW\circ dW=W_t^2/2$ versus $\int_0^tW\,dW=(W_t^2-t)/2$).
- **Non-anticipation**: the integrand must be adapted; this is what kills the cross terms and gives the martingale property.
FOLLOWUP: What can we integrate against?
What sorts of quantities can integrals be defined against?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
- **Ordinary functions of $t$** (Riemann integral, $\int f(t)\,dt$).
- **Functions of bounded variation** $g$: Riemann–Stieltjes $\int f\,dg$ for continuous $f$ (e.g. $g$ a CDF gives expectations $\int x\,dF(x)$); more generally **measures** via Lebesgue–Stieltjes integration. Pathwise, this covers any finite-variation process (e.g. $\int H_s\,dA_s$ with $A$ increasing, or a Poisson process $N$: $\int H\,dN=\sum_{\text{jumps}}H_{\tau_k}$).
- **Rough deterministic paths**: Young integration works pathwise when the integrand and integrator are Hölder with exponents adding to more than $1$; rough-path theory goes beyond.
- **Brownian motion**, and more generally **continuous (local) martingales** $M$: the Itô integral $\int H\,dM$ built by the $L^2$ procedure above with $dt$ replaced by $d\langle M\rangle_t$.
- **Semimartingales** (local martingale plus finite-variation process, possibly with jumps): the most general class of "good integrators" — the Bichteler–Dellacherie theorem says these are exactly the processes for which a stochastic integral with reasonable continuity properties exists. All asset-price models used in no-arbitrage pricing are semimartingales, which is why the gains from trading $\int\theta\,dS$ make sense.

TITLE: Two bells
QUESTION:
There are two bells. One rings five times per minute, and the other rings four times per minute. If they start at the same time, how long will it be until they next ring together?
HINT1:
Don't multiply 4 by 5 on reflex. Convert each bell's rate into the time between two consecutive rings.
HINT2:
One bell rings every 12 seconds, the other every 15 seconds (assuming evenly spaced rings). You want the first positive time that is a multiple of both.
ANSWER:
One minute (60 seconds).
REFRESH:
- **Least common multiple**: the smallest positive integer divisible by both $a$ and $b$; $\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)=\dfrac{ab}{\gcd(a,b)}$.
EXPLANATION:
The tempting answer "20 minutes" comes from multiplying the two rates, which has no meaning here. What matters is the *period* of each bell. Ringing five times a minute at equal intervals means one ring every $60/5=12$ seconds; four times a minute means every $60/4=15$ seconds. The bells ring together exactly at times that are multiples of both 12 and 15 seconds, and the first such time is their least common multiple.
SOLUTION: Least common multiple of the periods
Assume each bell rings at equally spaced times and both ring at $t=0$.

- Bell 1 rings at $t=0,12,24,36,48,60,\dots$ seconds.
- Bell 2 rings at $t=0,15,30,45,60,\dots$ seconds.

They coincide at the multiples of $\mathrm{lcm}(12,15)$. Since $12=2^2\cdot3$ and $15=3\cdot5$, $\mathrm{lcm}(12,15)=2^2\cdot3\cdot5=60$. The first common time after the start is $60$ seconds, i.e. **one minute**.

(Quick check with rates: in one minute bell 1 completes exactly 5 periods and bell 2 exactly 4, both whole numbers, and no shorter time makes both counts whole since the periods 12 and 15 first align at 60.)
===END===

TITLE: Sum of 1 to 100
QUESTION:
What is the sum of the integers from 1 to 100?
HINT1:
Look at the first and last numbers of the list together, then the second and second-to-last.
HINT2:
Pair $1$ with $100$, $2$ with $99$, and so on. How many pairs are there, and what does each pair add up to?
ANSWER:
$5{,}050$
REFRESH:
NONE
EXPLANATION:
Adding 100 numbers one by one is slow; the trick is that the list is symmetric. Reading it forwards and backwards at the same time, each forward term plus the matching backward term is always $101$: $1+100=2+99=3+98=\dots=101$. So the total is "number of pairs times 101". This is Gauss's classic schoolboy trick, and it gives the general formula $1+2+\dots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.

Mental arithmetic tip: one of $n$ and $n+1$ is even; halve it and multiply by the other ($\frac{100}{2}\times101=50\times101=5050$).
SOLUTION: Pairing the ends
Write $S=1+2+\dots+100$. Group the terms in pairs $(1,100),(2,99),\dots,(50,51)$. Each pair sums to $101$, and there are $100/2=50$ pairs, so
$$S=50\times101=5{,}050 .$$
SOLUTION: Reverse and add
Write the sum twice, once reversed:
$$\begin{aligned}S&=1+2+\dots+99+100\\ S&=100+99+\dots+2+1\end{aligned}$$
Adding column by column, each of the $100$ columns sums to $101$, so $2S=100\times101$ and $S=\frac{100\times101}{2}=5{,}050$.
SOLUTION: Marble triangle
Represent the number $i$ as a row of $i$ marbles and stack the rows $1,\dots,100$: you get a triangle that is the lower half of a $100\times100$ square, *including* the diagonal. Two such triangles cover the square with the diagonal counted twice: $2S=100^2+100$. Hence $S=\frac{10{,}000+100}{2}=5{,}050$.
FOLLOWUP: Sum of 1 to n
More generally, what is the sum of the integers from $1$ to $n$?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$$1+2+\dots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}.$$
Reverse-and-add: writing $S=1+\dots+n$ above $S=n+\dots+1$, each of the $n$ columns sums to $n+1$, so $2S=n(n+1)$. Check by induction: true for $n=1$, and if $S_n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ then $S_{n+1}=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}+(n+1)=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2}$.

TITLE: Inscribed circle and rectangle
QUESTION:
A circle is inscribed in a square, touching all four sides. There is exactly enough room to fit a $5\times10$ rectangle into one corner of the square so that the rectangle just touches the circle.

Figure (described): a square of side $S$ with its inscribed circle. In the top-right corner sits a rectangle whose top and right sides lie along the square's sides; it is 10 wide (along the top edge) and 5 tall. Its lower-left corner touches the circle.

What is the side length of the square?
HINT1:
Call the circle's radius $R$ (so the square has side $2R$) and look at the corner of the rectangle that touches the circle. How far is it from the centre?
HINT2:
Draw the right triangle from the circle's centre to that corner of the rectangle. Express its horizontal and vertical legs in terms of $R$, and use Pythagoras with hypotenuse $R$.
ANSWER:
The square has side $50$ (radius $25$).
REFRESH:
- **Quadratic formula**: $ax^2+bx+c=0\Rightarrow x=\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$.
EXPLANATION:
Put the origin at the centre of the circle. The top-right corner of the square is at $(R,R)$. The rectangle occupies the region from the top-right corner going 10 left and 5 down, so its inner corner (the one touching the circle) is at $(R-10,\,R-5)$. "Just touches the circle" means this point lies on the circle, i.e. at distance $R$ from the centre. That gives one equation in one unknown. It is quadratic and has two roots; only one matches the picture.
SOLUTION: Pythagoras from the centre
The touching corner is at $(R-10,R-5)$ relative to the centre, so
$$R^2=(R-10)^2+(R-5)^2=R^2-20R+100+R^2-10R+25 .$$
Simplifying: $0=R^2-30R+125$, so
$$R=\frac{30\pm\sqrt{900-500}}{2}=\frac{30\pm20}{2}\in\{5,\,25\}.$$
Reject $R=5$: then the square has side $10$, the rectangle's inner corner is at $(-5,0)$, on the far side of the circle — the rectangle would span the whole width of the square and overlap the circle, which is not the picture. So $R=25$ and the side is $S=2R=\mathbf{50}$.

Check: the corner is at $(15,20)$ from the centre and $\sqrt{15^2+20^2}=25=R$. ✓

SLUG: continuing-the-male-line
IDS: H1A-09
SECTION: brainteaser
CATEGORY: Logic & deduction
DIFFICULTY: medium
TITLE: Continuing the male line
QUESTION:
A mythical city contains 100,000 married couples but no children. Each family wishes to "continue the male line", but they do not wish to over-populate. So each family has one baby per year until the arrival of the first boy. For example, if (at some future date) a family has five children, then either they are all girls and another child is planned, or there are four girls and one boy and no more children are planned. Assume that children are equally likely to be born male or female.

Let $p(t)$ be the percentage of *children* that are male at the end of year $t$. How is this percentage expected to evolve through time?
HINT1:
Forget the families for a moment and look at the individual births. Does the stopping rule change the sex of any single baby?
HINT2:
In each year, compute the expected number of boys and the expected number of girls born that year among the families still trying.
ANSWER:
It stays at 50% in every year: stopping rules cannot change the fact that each birth is a fair coin flip.
REFRESH:
- **Linearity of expectation**: $E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]$ for any random variables, independent or not.
- **Geometric series derivative**: $\sum_{k\ge1}kq^{k-1}=\frac{1}{(1-q)^2}$ for $|q|<1$, so $\sum_{k\ge1}k\,2^{-k}=\tfrac12\cdot\frac1{(1/2)^2}=2$.
EXPLANATION:
Many people expect a surplus of girls, picturing big families with many daughters and one son. But the rule only decides *whether* a family has another baby, never *what sex* the baby is. Every birth that happens is an independent 50/50 event, so each year the births are, in expectation, half boys and half girls — and so are the cumulative totals.

The "big families with many girls" intuition fails because big families are rare: half of the families stop after one child (a boy), a quarter after two, and so on. The average family size is only $\sum_k k/2^k=2$ children: one boy and, on average, one girl.
SOLUTION: Year-by-year expected counts
Let $N=100{,}000$. In year 1 every family has a baby: expected $N/2$ boys and $N/2$ girls. The $N/2$ families that had a girl continue.

In year 2 those $N/2$ families have a baby: expected $N/4$ boys and $N/4$ girls. In general, in year $t$ the number of families still trying is expected to be $N/2^{t-1}$, and they produce $N/2^t$ boys and $N/2^t$ girls in expectation.

Cumulative expected totals at the end of year $t$:
$$E[\text{boys}]=E[\text{girls}]=N\Big(\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\dots+\tfrac1{2^t}\Big)=N\big(1-2^{-t}\big).$$
So the expected numbers of boys and girls are equal at all times and $p(t)$ is expected to stay at **50%** forever (e.g. $50{,}000/50{,}000$ after year 1, $75{,}000/75{,}000$ after year 2).

Note (fine print): strictly, the *ratio* of expected counts is exactly $1/2$; the expectation of the random ratio $\text{boys}/\text{children}$ differs from $1/2$ by an amount of order $1/N$ (about $10^{-6}$ here) because a ratio is a nonlinear function of the counts. For 100,000 families this is invisible, and the intended answer is 50%.

QUESTION:
Suppose that $f(z)$ is a complex-valued function on the complex plane which is both bounded and entire. Prove that $f$ must be constant.

Write $f$ as its power series and try to show that every coefficient $a_n$ with $n\ge1$ is zero. How can you extract a single coefficient from $f$?
HINT2:

REFRESH:
- **Orthogonality of $e^{ik\phi}$**: $\int_0^{2\pi}e^{ik\phi}\,d\phi=2\pi$ if $k=0$ and $0$ for any other integer $k$.
- **Uniform convergence**: a power series converges uniformly on any circle strictly inside its disc of convergence, so it can be integrated term by term there.
- **Triangle inequality for integrals**: $\left|\int g\right|\le\int|g|$.
EXPLANATION:
This is Liouville's theorem. The idea: a power series coefficient $a_n$ can be written as an average of $f$ over a circle of radius $r$, divided by $r^n$. If $|f|\le M$ everywhere, that average is at most $M$ in modulus for *every* radius, so $|a_n|\le M/r^n$. Now let the circle grow: $r^n\to\infty$ for $n\ge1$, which squeezes $a_n$ to $0$. Only the constant term survives.

Intuitively: a non-constant power series must grow at least like $|z|^n$ somewhere far away; a function trapped in a bounded region has no room to do that. (A much stronger result, Picard's little theorem, says a non-constant entire function takes every complex value with at most one exception, so it certainly cannot be bounded.)
SOLUTION: Cauchy estimates on the coefficients
Let $|f(z)|\le M$ for all $z$ and $f(z)=\sum_{k\ge0}a_kz^k$.

**Step 1: coefficient formula.** Fix $r>0$ and $n\ge0$. On $|z|=r$ write $z=re^{i\phi}$. The series converges uniformly on the circle, so
$$\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{f(re^{i\phi})}{(re^{i\phi})^n}\,d\phi=\sum_{k\ge0}a_k\,r^{k-n}\,\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(k-n)\phi}\,d\phi=a_n ,$$
since the inner integral is $2\pi$ for $k=n$ and $0$ otherwise.

**Step 2: bound.** By the triangle inequality,
$$|a_n|\le\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{|f(re^{i\phi})|}{r^n}\,d\phi\le\frac{M}{r^n}.$$

**Step 3: let $r\to\infty$.** The bound holds for every $r>0$ because $f$ is entire. For $n\ge1$, $M/r^n\to0$, so $a_n=0$. Therefore $f(z)=a_0$ for all $z$: $f$ is constant. $\blacksquare$
SOLUTION: Cauchy integral formula for the derivative
For any $z_0\in\mathbb C$ and $r>0$, Cauchy's formula gives
$$f'(z_0)=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{|z-z_0|=r}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^2}\,dz .$$
The contour has length $2\pi r$, and on it $\left|\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^2}\right|\le\frac{M}{r^2}$, so
$$|f'(z_0)|\le\frac1{2\pi}\cdot2\pi r\cdot\frac{M}{r^2}=\frac Mr\xrightarrow[r\to\infty]{}0 .$$
Hence $f'(z_0)=0$ for every $z_0$, and since the plane is connected $f$ is constant (e.g. $f(z)-f(0)=\int_0^1f'(tz)\,z\,dt=0$). $\blacksquare$

- **Exponential growth**: an area doubling daily is $A_0\,2^t$ after $t$ days, so multiplying the starting area by $2^k$ saves exactly $k$ days.
EXPLANATION:
With doubling, the only thing that matters is the total area, and area grows by a factor 2 per day regardless of how it is split among pads. Eight pads have $8=2^3$ times the area of one pad — exactly what one pad reaches after 3 days. So starting with eight pads is like starting with a 3-day-old single pad, which needs the remaining $30-3=27$ days. (This assumes the pads never overlap, so the covered area is the sum of the pads' areas.)

Another book asks the same idea backwards: if a lily pad doubles every second and fills the pond after one minute, when is the pond a quarter full? A quarter is two halvings, so 2 seconds before the end, at 58 seconds (see the follow-up).
SOLUTION: Time shift
One pad: area $a\,2^t$, with $a\,2^{30}=P$ (the pond's area). Eight pads: total area $8a\,2^t=a\,2^{t+3}$. Setting $a\,2^{t+3}=P=a\,2^{30}$ gives $t+3=30$, so $t=\mathbf{27}$ days.
FOLLOWUP: Why not 3,000 days?
Someone suggests making the puzzle harder by saying the single lily pad takes 3,000 days (instead of 30) to cover the pond. What is wrong with that?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
It is physically absurd because of compound growth. After 3,000 doublings the area is multiplied by $2^{3000}\approx10^{903}$. Even after only 100 days the factor is $2^{100}\approx1.3\times10^{30}$: a pad of $1\ \text{mm}^2=10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2$ would cover about $10^{24}\ \text{m}^2$, far more than the Earth's surface ($\approx5\times10^{14}\ \text{m}^2$). No pond — indeed nothing in the universe — could take 3,000 days to be covered by a visible pad that doubles daily.
FOLLOWUP: Quarter-filling the pond
A lily pad doubles in area every second. After one minute it fills the pond. How long does it take to quarter-fill the pond?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
**58 seconds.** Going backwards in time, the area halves every second: the pond is half full at 59 seconds and a quarter full at 58 seconds. Formally $A(t)=A(60)\,2^{t-60}$, and $A(t)=\tfrac14A(60)=A(60)\,2^{-2}$ gives $t=58$.
FOLLOWUP: Fly between two trains
Two trains start 60 miles apart and head toward each other on the same track, each at 30 mph. A fly starts at the front of one train and flies at 45 mph until it meets the other train, then reverses, and keeps doing this until it gets squashed. How far does the fly fly?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
**45 miles.** The trains close the gap at $30+30=60$ mph, so they collide after $60/60=1$ hour. The fly flies at a constant 45 mph for that hour, so it covers 45 miles. (Summing the infinite geometric series of back-and-forth legs gives the same answer with much more work.)
FOLLOWUP: Finite geometric series
What is the formula for the sum of $N$ terms of a geometric series?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
For first term $a$ and ratio $r\ne1$:
$$S_N=a+ar+\dots+ar^{N-1}=a\,\frac{1-r^N}{1-r}.$$
Proof: $S_N-rS_N=a-ar^N$ (all other terms cancel), so $(1-r)S_N=a(1-r^N)$. If $r=1$, $S_N=Na$. If $|r|<1$, letting $N\to\infty$ gives $\frac{a}{1-r}$. Lily-pad example: $1+2+\dots+2^{N-1}=2^N-1$.

SOLUTION: Law of cosines and difference of squares
Let $C$ be the angle between sides $a$ and $b$. Then $A=\tfrac12ab\sin C$, so
$$16A^2=4a^2b^2\sin^2C=4a^2b^2(1-\cos^2C)=4a^2b^2-(2ab\cos C)^2 .$$
By the law of cosines $2ab\cos C=a^2+b^2-c^2$, hence
$$16A^2=(2ab)^2-(a^2+b^2-c^2)^2=\big(2ab-a^2-b^2+c^2\big)\big(2ab+a^2+b^2-c^2\big).$$
Each factor is again a difference of squares:
$$2ab-a^2-b^2+c^2=c^2-(a-b)^2=(c-a+b)(c+a-b),$$
$$2ab+a^2+b^2-c^2=(a+b)^2-c^2=(a+b-c)(a+b+c).$$
Now $a+b+c=2s$, $-a+b+c=2(s-a)$, $a-b+c=2(s-b)$, $a+b-c=2(s-c)$. Therefore
$$16A^2=2s\cdot2(s-a)\cdot2(s-b)\cdot2(s-c)=16\,s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c),$$
and taking square roots (all factors are positive by the triangle inequality) gives $A=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}$. $\blacksquare$
SOLUTION: Base, height and Pythagoras
Take $b$ as the base, with height $h$. Put the base on the $x$-axis from $(0,0)$ to $(b,0)$ and the apex at $(p,h)$, where side $a$ joins $(0,0)$ to the apex and side $c$ joins $(b,0)$ to the apex. Then
$$a^2=p^2+h^2,\qquad c^2=(b-p)^2+h^2 .$$

$$A^2=\tfrac14b^2h^2=\tfrac14b^2(a^2-p^2)=\tfrac14b^2a^2-\tfrac1{16}(a^2+b^2-c^2)^2,$$
i.e. $16A^2=4a^2b^2-(a^2+b^2-c^2)^2$, which factors exactly as in the first method to $16\,s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)$. (The book reaches the same identity by substituting $a=2s-b-c$ etc. and expanding a quartic in $s$; factoring as differences of squares avoids that algebra.)

TITLE: Rock in a boat
QUESTION:
A small boat is floating in a swimming pool. The boat contains a small but very heavy rock (or, in another version, an anchor). The rock is thrown out of the boat into the pool and sinks to the bottom. What happens to the water level in the pool?
HINT1:
Archimedes: a floating object displaces water according to one property of the object, a sunken object according to another. Which property in each case?
HINT2:
In the boat, the rock makes the boat displace a *mass* of water equal to the rock's mass. On the bottom of the pool, the rock displaces only its own *volume* of water. Compare the two volumes, given that the rock is denser than water.
ANSWER:
The water level falls.
REFRESH:
- **Archimedes' principle**: a body immersed in a fluid feels an upward force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces. A floating body therefore displaces a mass of fluid equal to its own mass; a fully submerged body resting on the bottom displaces exactly its own volume.
EXPLANATION:
While the rock rides in the boat, it is *floating* (via the boat), so it pushes aside water of the same **mass** as the rock: volume $m/\rho_{\text{water}}$. Once it lies on the bottom, it pushes aside only its own **volume**: $m/\rho_{\text{rock}}$. A heavy rock (or anchor) is much denser than water, so $m/\rho_{\text{rock}}<m/\rho_{\text{water}}$: less water is displaced after the throw, and the level goes down. It's as if a mass of water equal to the rock's mass were removed and only a rock-sized volume put back.
SOLUTION: Compare displaced volumes
Let $m$ be the rock's mass, $\rho_r$ its density, $\rho_w$ the water's density ($\rho_r>\rho_w$) and $S$ the pool's surface area.

- Rock in the boat: boat plus rock float, so the extra displaced volume due to the rock is $V_1=m/\rho_w$.
- Rock on the bottom: it displaces its own volume $V_2=m/\rho_r$, and the boat rises back by the corresponding $V_1$.

Change in level:
$$\Delta h=\frac{V_2-V_1}{S}=\frac{m}{S}\Big(\frac1{\rho_r}-\frac1{\rho_w}\Big)<0 .$$
The level **falls**. Example: a 10 kg stone of density 2,500 kg/m³ in a pool of 50 m² gives $V_1=10$ L, $V_2=4$ L, so the level drops by $6\ \text{L}/50\ \text{m}^2=0.12$ mm.
FOLLOWUP: Melting iceberg
An iceberg floating in the ocean melts. What happens to the sea level?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
If the ice floated in *fresh* water, the level would be exactly unchanged: floating ice of mass $M$ displaces water of mass $M$, i.e. volume $M/\rho_{\text{fresh}}$, and when it melts it becomes exactly that volume of water. In the real ocean, icebergs are fresh water floating in denser salt water: the ice displaces $M/\rho_{\text{sea}}$, but melts into $M/\rho_{\text{fresh}}$, which is slightly larger ($\rho_{\text{sea}}\approx1.025\,\rho_{\text{fresh}}$). So the sea level rises very slightly — by about 2.5% of the displaced volume. (Ice melting on land, by contrast, adds its full volume to the sea.)
FOLLOWUP: Why a pool and not a lake?
Why do you think this question is phrased for a swimming pool and not a lake?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
Several reasons. (1) The answer relies on the rock or anchor ending up **resting on the bottom**. In a shallow pool it certainly does; in a deep lake an anchor might hang on its rope from the boat, and then the boat still carries its weight minus buoyancy — boat plus anchor float together and the total displaced mass is unchanged, so the level does not change at all. (2) A pool is a closed container with vertical walls, so the level change $\Delta V/S$ is well defined and in principle measurable; in a huge lake it is utterly negligible and swamped by waves, inflows, outflows and evaporation. (3) A lake bottom may be mud the anchor sinks into, and its shores are sloped, muddying the geometry. The pool makes it a clean physics question.
===END===

TITLE: Tower of Hanoi, 64 rings
QUESTION:
There are three poles. One pole holds 64 rings, weighing from 1 ounce (at the top) to 64 ounces (at the bottom). You must move all the rings to one of the other poles so that they end up in the same order. You may move only one ring at a time, only from one pole to another, and you may never place a ring on top of a lighter ring, even temporarily.

What is the minimum number of moves needed?
HINT1:
Solve it for 1, 2 and 3 rings and look for a pattern. Think about the moment the heaviest ring moves.
HINT2:
When the heaviest ring moves, all the other $n-1$ rings must be stacked on the third pole. So $V(n)=2V(n-1)+1$ with $V(1)=1$.
ANSWER:
$2^{64}-1=18{,}446{,}744{,}073{,}709{,}551{,}615$ moves.
REFRESH:
- **Proof by induction**: prove $P(1)$, then prove $P(N)\Rightarrow P(N+1)$; then $P(n)$ holds for all $n\ge1$.
- **Linear recursion**: $V(n)=2V(n-1)+1$ becomes $V(n)+1=2(V(n-1)+1)$, a geometric sequence.
EXPLANATION:
Focus on the heaviest ring. It can only move when it is alone on its pole and the destination pole is empty, so all $n-1$ lighter rings must sit, correctly stacked, on the third pole. Getting them there is itself a Tower of Hanoi with $n-1$ rings; after the big ring moves, the $n-1$ rings must be moved on top of it — another $(n-1)$-ring puzzle. Hence $V(n)=2V(n-1)+1$, and this is both achievable and unavoidable. Each extra ring doubles the work, giving $2^n-1$.

For 64 rings that is about $1.8\times10^{19}$ moves: at one move per second, roughly 585 billion years.
SOLUTION: Recursion with matching lower bound
**Upper bound (a strategy).** To move $n$ rings from pole 1 to pole 3: move the top $n-1$ rings to pole 2 ($V(n-1)$ moves), move the heaviest ring to pole 3 (1 move), then move the $n-1$ rings from pole 2 onto pole 3 ($V(n-1)$ moves). So $V(n)\le2V(n-1)+1$.

**Lower bound.** In any solution, the heaviest ring must move at least once. Just before its first move, the other $n-1$ rings must all be on the one remaining pole (none can be under it or on its destination), which needs at least $V(n-1)$ moves. After its last move, those $n-1$ rings must be brought on top of it, at least another $V(n-1)$ moves. So $V(n)\ge2V(n-1)+1$.

**Solving.** $V(1)=1$ and $V(n)=2V(n-1)+1$. Claim $V(n)=2^n-1$: true for $n=1$; if $V(N)=2^N-1$ then $V(N+1)=2(2^N-1)+1=2^{N+1}-1$. By induction $V(n)=2^n-1$ for all $n$.

For $n=64$: $V(64)=2^{64}-1=\mathbf{18{,}446{,}744{,}073{,}709{,}551{,}615}$ moves ($\approx1.84\times10^{19}$; at one move per second, about $5.8\times10^{11}$ years).

SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Variance & correlation
DIFFICULTY: medium
TITLE: Minimum-variance weights
QUESTION:
Let $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu_X,\sigma_X^2)$ and $Y\sim\mathcal N(\mu_Y,\sigma_Y^2)$ with correlation $\rho$. How should you choose constants $a$ and $b$ to minimise the variance of $S=aX+bY$ subject to $a+b=1$, $0\le a\le1$, $0\le b\le1$?
HINT1:
Use $b=1-a$ to get a one-variable problem, and write $\mathrm{Var}(S)$ with the covariance term.
HINT2:
$\mathrm{Var}(S)$ is a quadratic in $a$ with non-negative leading coefficient. Set its derivative to zero, then think about what to do if the minimiser falls outside $[0,1]$.
ANSWER:
$a^*=\dfrac{\sigma_Y^2-\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X^2-2\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y+\sigma_Y^2}$, clipped to $[0,1]$, and $b^*=1-a^*$.
REFRESH:
- **Variance of a linear combination**: $\mathrm{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\sigma_X^2+b^2\sigma_Y^2+2ab\,\rho\,\sigma_X\sigma_Y$.
- **Minimising a convex quadratic on an interval**: if the unconstrained minimiser lies outside the interval, the constrained minimum is at the nearest endpoint.
EXPLANATION:
This is the two-asset minimum-variance portfolio. Normality is irrelevant: only variances and the correlation matter. Diversification lowers variance because the cross term $2a(1-a)\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y$ is smaller than it would be with perfect correlation. The variance is a parabola in $a$ opening upwards (its curvature is $2\,\mathrm{Var}(X-Y)\ge0$), so the first-order condition gives the unconstrained minimum, and the no-short-selling constraint $0\le a\le1$ simply clips it.

Special cases: with $\rho=0$, $a^*=\frac{\sigma_Y^2}{\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2}$ (weights inversely proportional to variances); with $\rho=-1$, $a^*=\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X+\sigma_Y}$ and the variance drops to zero (a perfect hedge). If $\rho$ is high, the minimiser can fall outside $[0,1]$ — it would short the riskier asset — and then you put everything in the less volatile asset.
SOLUTION: One-variable quadratic minimisation
Substitute $b=1-a$:
$$V(a)=a^2\sigma_X^2+2a(1-a)\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y+(1-a)^2\sigma_Y^2 .$$
**First-order condition.**
$$V'(a)=2a\sigma_X^2+2(1-2a)\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y-2(1-a)\sigma_Y^2=2\Big[a\big(\sigma_X^2-2\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y+\sigma_Y^2\big)+\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y-\sigma_Y^2\Big]=0,$$
so
$$a^*=\frac{\sigma_Y^2-\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X^2-2\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y+\sigma_Y^2}.$$
**Second-order condition.** $\tfrac12V''(a)=\sigma_X^2-2\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y+\sigma_Y^2=\mathrm{Var}(X-Y)\ge(\sigma_X-\sigma_Y)^2\ge0$, strictly positive unless $\rho=1$ and $\sigma_X=\sigma_Y$ (then $X-Y$ is constant and every $a$ gives the same variance). So $a^*$ is a minimum.

**Constraints.** $V$ is a convex parabola, so the constrained minimiser is $a^*$ clipped to $[0,1]$:
- $a^*<0\iff\sigma_Y^2<\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y\iff\sigma_Y/\sigma_X<\rho$: take $a=0$ (all in $Y$, the less volatile one);
- $a^*>1\iff\sigma_X^2<\rho\sigma_X\sigma_Y\iff\sigma_X/\sigma_Y<\rho$: take $a=1$ (all in $X$).

**Check $\rho=-1$:** $a^*=\frac{\sigma_Y^2+\sigma_X\sigma_Y}{(\sigma_X+\sigma_Y)^2}=\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X+\sigma_Y}$, and then $V(a^*)=(a^*\sigma_X-(1-a^*)\sigma_Y)^2=0$.
FOLLOWUP: Where to sit on the axle
You drive with one wheel on gravel and one on pavement. The vertical bumps of the gravel and pavement have variances $\sigma_G^2$ and $\sigma_P^2$ (correlation $\rho$). Where on the axle, between $x=0$ (above the gravel wheel) and $x=1$ (above the pavement wheel), should you sit for the most comfortable ride?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
The axle is a rigid rod, so the vertical displacement at position $x$ is the linear interpolation $(1-x)G+xP$. This is the previous problem with $a=1-x$ on $G$ and $b=x$ on $P$:
$$\mathrm{Var}=(1-x)^2\sigma_G^2+x^2\sigma_P^2+2x(1-x)\rho\sigma_G\sigma_P,$$
minimised at
$$x^*=\frac{\sigma_G^2-\rho\sigma_G\sigma_P}{\sigma_G^2-2\rho\sigma_G\sigma_P+\sigma_P^2},$$
clipped to $[0,1]$. With independent surfaces ($\rho=0$), $x^*=\frac{\sigma_G^2}{\sigma_G^2+\sigma_P^2}$: you sit closer to the pavement wheel, the more so the bumpier the gravel.

TITLE: Lighthouse beam speed
QUESTION:
A straight coastline lies $L=3$ miles from a lighthouse. The light revolves at one revolution per minute. How fast does the spot of light travel along the coastline? In particular, how fast is it moving when it is $3L$ away from the point of the coast closest to the lighthouse?
HINT1:
The speed is not constant. Write the position of the spot along the coast as a function of the beam's angle.
HINT2:
If $\theta$ is the angle between the beam and the perpendicular to the coast, the spot is at $y=L\tan\theta$, with $\theta=\omega t$ and $\omega=2\pi$ rad/min. Differentiate with respect to $t$.
ANSWER:
$v=\omega L\sec^2\theta=\omega\frac{L^2+y^2}{L}$; at the nearest point $\pi/10\approx0.31$ mi/s ($\approx1{,}130$ mph), and at $y=3L$ exactly $\pi$ mi/s ($\approx11{,}300$ mph).
REFRESH:
- **Derivative of tan**: $\frac{d}{d\theta}\tan\theta=\sec^2\theta=1+\tan^2\theta=\frac1{\cos^2\theta}$.
- **Chain rule**: $\frac{d}{dt}g(\theta(t))=g'(\theta)\,\theta'(t)$.
EXPLANATION:
The beam turns at a constant angular speed, but the coast is a straight line at varying distance from the lighthouse. Near the closest point the spot moves at $\omega L$ (like a point on a circle of radius $L$). Further along, two effects speed it up: the coast is farther away ($R=\sqrt{L^2+y^2}$), and the beam hits the coast at an increasingly glancing angle. Both effects combine into the factor $\sec^2\theta=R^2/L^2$. As the beam approaches parallel to the coast the spot's speed goes to infinity — no physical object is moving, so nothing forbids it.
SOLUTION: Differentiate the spot's position
Set the closest coastal point at $y=0$ and let $\theta(t)=\omega t$ be the beam's angle from the perpendicular, with $\omega=2\pi$ rad/min $=\frac{2\pi}{60}=\frac{\pi}{30}$ rad/s. The spot is at $y=L\tan\theta$, so
$$v=\frac{dy}{dt}=L\sec^2\theta\cdot\omega=\frac{\omega L}{\cos^2\theta}=\omega\,\frac{L^2+y^2}{L}=\frac{\omega R^2}{L},$$
where $R=\sqrt{L^2+y^2}$ is the distance from the lighthouse to the spot.

- At the nearest point ($y=0$): $v=\omega L=\frac{\pi}{30}\times3=\frac{\pi}{10}\approx0.314$ miles per second ($\approx1{,}131$ mph).
- At $y=3L=9$ miles: $R^2=L^2+9L^2=90$, so $v=\frac{\pi}{30}\cdot\frac{90}{3}=\mathbf{\pi}$ **miles per second** ($\approx11{,}310$ mph), ten times faster.
SOLUTION: Small-triangle geometry
In a short time $dt$ the beam sweeps an arc of length $R\,\omega\,dt$ perpendicular to the beam at distance $R$. On the coast, this corresponds to a displacement $v\,dt$ along the shore. The small triangle formed by these two segments has angle $\alpha$ between the beam and the coastline, with $\sin\alpha=\frac{R\omega\,dt}{v\,dt}$. From the big triangle (lighthouse, closest point, spot), $\sin\alpha=\frac{L}{R}$. Equating: $\frac{\omega R}{v}=\frac{L}{R}$, so $v=\frac{\omega R^2}{L}$, the same formula; plugging $R^2=90$, $L=3$, $\omega=\pi/30$ rad/s gives $\pi$ miles per second.
