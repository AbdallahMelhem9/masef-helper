TITLE: Sixty cents in coins
QUESTION:
Brian has exactly 60 cents in his pocket, made up only of nickels (5 cents), dimes (10 cents) and quarters (25 cents). Let $N$ be the number of coins he holds.

For some values of $N$, knowing "60 cents in $N$ coins" pins down exactly how many nickels, dimes and quarters he has; for other values of $N$ at least two different mixes are possible.

What is the smallest $N$ for which the total (60 cents) and the number of coins $N$ are **not** enough to determine the mix?
HINT1:
There are only three possible numbers of quarters. List everything.
HINT2:
Fix the number of quarters $q\in\{0,1,2\}$. The rest $60-25q$ cents is made of dimes and nickels; write the coin count $N$ as a function of the number of dimes $d$, and look for the smallest value of $N$ that appears twice.
ANSWER:
$N = 6$
REFRESH:
NONE
EXPLANATION:
Everything is small enough to enumerate. Once the number of quarters $q$ is fixed, the remaining amount is split between dimes and nickels, and swapping one dime for two nickels adds exactly one coin. So for each $q$ the achievable coin counts form a run of consecutive integers. The three runs (one per value of $q$) overlap, and the smallest number in an overlap is the answer.
SOLUTION: Enumerate by number of quarters
Let $q, d, n$ be the numbers of quarters, dimes and nickels, with $25q + 10d + 5n = 60$, i.e. $5q + 2d + n = 12$.

- $q = 0$: $n = 12 - 2d$ with $d = 0,\dots,6$, so $N = d + n = 12 - d \in \{12, 11, 10, 9, 8, 7, 6\}$.
- $q = 1$: $2d + n = 7$, $n = 7 - 2d$ with $d = 0,\dots,3$, so $N = 1 + d + 7 - 2d = 8 - d \in \{8, 7, 6, 5\}$.
- $q = 2$: $2d + n = 2$, so $(d,n) = (1,0)$ or $(0,2)$, giving $N = 3$ or $N = 4$.

So $N = 3, 4, 5$ each occur once (unique mix), while $N = 6$ occurs twice:
$$q=0,\ d=6,\ n=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad q=1,\ d=2,\ n=3,$$
both 6 coins worth 60 cents ($60 = 6\times 10$ and $60 = 25 + 20 + 15$). The smallest ambiguous $N$ is $\boxed{6}$.
===END===

TITLE: Area of a ring from one chord
QUESTION:
Two circles have the same centre. A chord of the outer circle has length 8 and is tangent to the inner circle (it touches it at exactly one point). What is the area of the ring between the two circles?
HINT1:
You are not told either radius. Maybe you only need a combination of them.
HINT2:
Draw the radius of the inner circle to the point of tangency and the radius of the outer circle to one end of the chord. Which angle is a right angle?
ANSWER:
$16\pi$
REFRESH:
- A tangent line to a circle is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.
- The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord.
EXPLANATION:
The ring's area is $\pi R^2 - \pi r^2 = \pi(R^2 - r^2)$, so you do not need $R$ and $r$ separately, only $R^2 - r^2$. The tangency point, the centre and an end of the chord form a right triangle with hypotenuse $R$, one leg $r$ and the other leg half the chord, 4. Pythagoras gives $R^2 - r^2 = 16$ directly. A quick sanity check is the degenerate case $r = 0$: the chord becomes a diameter, $R = 4$, and the "ring" is a disc of area $16\pi$.
SOLUTION: Right triangle
Let $O$ be the centre, $r$ and $R$ the inner and outer radii, $T$ the tangency point and $A$ an end of the chord.

Since the chord is tangent to the inner circle at $T$, $OT \perp$ chord and $OT = r$. The perpendicular from the centre bisects the chord, so $TA = 8/2 = 4$. Also $OA = R$.

In the right triangle $OTA$:
$$R^2 = r^2 + 4^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R^2 - r^2 = 16.$$
The ring's area is
$$\pi R^2 - \pi r^2 = \pi(R^2 - r^2) = 16\pi.$$
SOLUTION: Shrink the inner circle
The problem does not give the radii, so if it is well posed the answer cannot depend on them. Take the limiting case $r \to 0$: the tangent chord passes through the centre, so it is a diameter, $R = 4$, and the area is $\pi\cdot 4^2 = 16\pi$. (This is a sanity check, not a proof; the first method is the proof.)

TITLE: Variance of the Monte Carlo estimate of pi
QUESTION:
To estimate $\pi$, you draw $N$ independent points uniformly in the unit square $[0,1]\times[0,1]$. Let $I_i$ be the indicator that the $i$-th point lands inside the quarter disc $x^2 + y^2 \le 1$, and let
$$T_N = \frac{I_1 + \dots + I_N}{N}.$$
Build the natural unbiased estimator of $\pi$ from $T_N$ and compute its variance. It can be written as $\dfrac{a\pi^2 + b\pi}{N}$ with integers $a, b$. What is $a + b$?

(The variance involves $\pi$ itself, so it is not directly usable in practice, but it tells you how fast the estimate converges.)
HINT1:
What is the probability that one point lands in the quarter disc? What is $\mathbb{E}[T_N]$, and so what multiple of $T_N$ estimates $\pi$?
HINT2:
Each $I_i$ is Bernoulli($p$) with $p = \pi/4$, so $\operatorname{Var}(T_N) = p(1-p)/N$. Multiplying a variable by $c$ multiplies its variance by $c^2$.
ANSWER:
$\operatorname{Var}(4T_N) = \dfrac{4\pi - \pi^2}{N}$, so $a = -1$, $b = 4$, $a + b = 3$.
REFRESH:
- If $I \sim$ Bernoulli($p$), then $\mathbb{E}[I] = p$ and $\operatorname{Var}(I) = p(1-p)$.
- For independent $X_i$: $\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum X_i\right) = \sum \operatorname{Var}(X_i)$, and $\operatorname{Var}(cX) = c^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$.
EXPLANATION:
A uniform point in the unit square lands in the quarter disc with probability equal to its area, $\pi/4$. So $T_N$ estimates $\pi/4$ and $\hat\pi = 4T_N$ is an unbiased estimator of $\pi$. The variance of an average of $N$ i.i.d. Bernoullis is $p(1-p)/N$, and the factor 4 in front multiplies the variance by 16. Numerically $4\pi - \pi^2 \approx 2.70$, so the standard error is about $1.64/\sqrt N$: to get $\pi$ to 3 decimals you need millions of points, which is why plain Monte Carlo is slow.
SOLUTION: Bernoulli variance
Each point is uniform on the unit square, so
$$p = \mathbb{P}(x^2 + y^2 \le 1) = \text{area of quarter disc} = \frac{\pi}{4}.$$
Hence $I_i \sim$ Bernoulli($\pi/4$), i.i.d., and $\mathbb{E}[T_N] = \pi/4$. The unbiased estimator of $\pi$ is $\hat\pi_N = 4T_N$.

Variance:
$$\operatorname{Var}(T_N) = \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_{i=1}^N \operatorname{Var}(I_i) = \frac{p(1-p)}{N},$$
$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\pi_N) = 16\,\frac{p(1-p)}{N} = \frac{16}{N}\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}\left(1 - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{4\pi - \pi^2}{N}.$$
So $a = -1$, $b = 4$ and $a + b = 3$.

TITLE: Bracelets with 3 red and 3 blue beads
QUESTION:
A bracelet has 6 equally spaced positions around a circle. You place 3 red beads and 3 blue beads on it, one per position. Two arrangements count as the same if one can be **rotated** into the other; reflections (flipping the bracelet over) do **not** count as the same. How many distinct bracelets are there?
HINT1:
There are only $\binom{6}{3} = 20$ labelled arrangements. Group them into rotation classes, carefully.
HINT2:
Classify by how the 3 red beads sit: all together, two together and one apart, or all separated. Check which of these patterns have a mirror image that is not a rotation of itself.
ANSWER:
4
REFRESH:
- Burnside's lemma: the number of classes of objects under a group $G$ of symmetries equals $\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\operatorname{Fix}(g)$, the average number of objects left unchanged by a symmetry.
EXPLANATION:
Counting circular arrangements is tricky because some patterns have fewer distinct rotations than others (a symmetric pattern like RBRBRB has only 2 rotations, not 6), so you cannot just divide 20 by 6. Either list the classes by the shape of the red block, or use Burnside, which averages the number of arrangements fixed by each rotation and handles symmetric patterns automatically. The subtle point is that "two red together, one apart" splits into two classes that are mirror images of each other: without reflections they are different bracelets.
SOLUTION: Direct classification
Label positions $0,\dots,5$ around the circle and look at the gaps of blue beads between consecutive red beads going clockwise. The three gaps are non-negative integers summing to 3, read cyclically.

- Gaps $(0,0,3)$: RRRBBB, all reds together. Its 6 rotations are all different: 6 arrangements.
- Gaps $(0,1,2)$: RRBRBB. Its 6 rotations are all different: 6 arrangements.
- Gaps $(0,2,1)$: RRBBRB. Again 6 different rotations: 6 arrangements. This is the mirror image of RRBRBB, but no rotation turns one into the other (reading clockwise from the RR pair, one has the single blue first and the other has the double blue first).
- Gaps $(1,1,1)$: RBRBRB, alternating. Only 2 distinct rotations: 2 arrangements.

Check: $6 + 6 + 6 + 2 = 20 = \binom{6}{3}$. So there are $\boxed{4}$ distinct bracelets.

Note: the source's example claims RRBRBB and RRBBRB are the same under rotation; they are actually mirror images, not rotations, of each other. The count 4 is correct precisely because they are different.
SOLUTION: Burnside's lemma
The rotation group has 6 elements: rotation by $k$ positions, $k = 0,\dots,5$. Count the arrangements (3 red, 3 blue) each one fixes.

- $k = 0$: all $\binom{6}{3} = 20$.
- $k = 1, 5$: a single 6-cycle, which would force all beads to be the same colour: 0.
- $k = 2, 4$: two 3-cycles $\{0,2,4\}, \{1,3,5\}$; each cycle must be monochrome, and one must be red: 2 each.
- $k = 3$: three 2-cycles $\{0,3\},\{1,4\},\{2,5\}$; monochrome pairs give an even number of reds, never 3: 0.

$$\#\text{bracelets} = \frac{20 + 0 + 2 + 0 + 2 + 0}{6} = \frac{24}{6} = 4.$$
FOLLOWUP: If reflections count too
How many bracelets are there if two arrangements are identified when one can be rotated **or flipped** into the other?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
3. The classes RRBRBB and RRBBRB are mirror images, so they merge; RRRBBB and RBRBRB are each their own mirror images. Burnside with the dihedral group of 12 elements gives the same: the 6 rotations fix $24$ arrangements in total; each of the 3 reflections through two opposite beads fixes those 2 beads and swaps 2 pairs, and 3 reds need exactly one fixed bead red plus one swapped pair red: $2\times 2 = 4$ arrangements each, total 12; the 3 reflections through edge midpoints swap 3 pairs, giving an even number of reds: 0. So $(24 + 12 + 0)/12 = 3$.

TITLE: Race to three heads with a head start
QUESTION:
Two players, A and B, take turns flipping a fair coin, A first. The first player to collect a total of 3 heads (on their own flips) wins.

A has just made the first flip of the game and got heads. It is now B's turn. What is the probability that A wins?
HINT1:
The game only depends on how many heads each player still needs and whose turn it is. Set up probabilities for those states.
HINT2:
Let $a(i,j)$ be A's winning probability when A needs $i$ more heads, B needs $j$ more, and A is to flip; $b(i,j)$ the same with B to flip. Write one-step equations and solve upward from small $i, j$. You want $b(2,3)$.
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{19}{27}$
REFRESH:
- First-step analysis: condition on the outcome of the next flip and use the law of total probability to get linear equations between state probabilities.
- Negative binomial: the number of fair-coin flips needed to get the $r$-th head is $k$ with probability $\binom{k-1}{r-1}2^{-k}$.
EXPLANATION:
The state of the game is (heads A still needs, heads B still needs, whose turn). A tails leaves the counts unchanged and hands the turn over, so each state is linked to itself through the other player's turn; solving the two equations together removes that loop. Alternatively, think of each player as flipping their own private coin sequence: A wins exactly when A reaches 2 more heads in strictly fewer of his own flips than B needs for 3, because B flips first in each round from now on.
SOLUTION: First-step recursion
Let $x = b(i-1, j)$ (A just scored) and $y = a(i, j-1)$ (B just scored). Conditioning on the next flip:
$$a(i,j) = \tfrac12 b(i-1,j) + \tfrac12 b(i,j), \qquad b(i,j) = \tfrac12 a(i,j-1) + \tfrac12 a(i,j).$$
Substituting the second into the first: $a = \frac x2 + \frac y4 + \frac a4$, so
$$a(i,j) = \frac{2x + y}{3}, \qquad b(i,j) = \frac{x + 2y}{3}.$$
Boundaries: $a(0,j) = b(0,j) = 1$ and $a(i,0) = b(i,0) = 0$.

- $(1,1)$: $x = 1$, $y = 0$: $a = 2/3$, $b = 1/3$.
- $(1,2)$: $x = 1$, $y = a(1,1) = 2/3$: $a = 8/9$, $b = 7/9$.
- $(1,3)$: $x = 1$, $y = a(1,2) = 8/9$: $a = 26/27$, $b = 25/27$.
- $(2,1)$: $x = b(1,1) = 1/3$, $y = 0$: $a = 2/9$, $b = 1/9$.
- $(2,2)$: $x = b(1,2) = 7/9$, $y = a(2,1) = 2/9$: $a = 16/27$, $b = 11/27$.
- $(2,3)$: $x = b(1,3) = 25/27$, $y = a(2,2) = 16/27$:
$$b(2,3) = \frac{25/27 + 32/27}{3} = \frac{57}{81} = \frac{19}{27}.$$
SOLUTION: Compare waiting times
From now on the order is B, A, B, A, ... Let $K_A$ be the number of A's further flips until his 2nd additional head and $K_B$ the number of B's flips until her 3rd head; they are independent. A's $k$-th flip comes right after B's $k$-th flip, so A wins iff $K_A < K_B$.

$\mathbb{P}(K_A = k) = (k-1)2^{-k}$ for $k \ge 2$, and $\mathbb{P}(K_B > k) = \mathbb{P}(\text{at most 2 heads in } k \text{ flips}) = \left(1 + k + \binom k2\right)2^{-k}$. Hence
$$\mathbb{P}(\text{A wins}) = \sum_{k\ge 2}\frac{(k-1)\left(1 + k + \frac{k(k-1)}{2}\right)}{4^k}.$$
Using $\sum_{k\ge0} x^k = \frac1{1-x}$ and its derivatives at $x = 1/4$, this sum evaluates to $19/27 \approx 0.7037$ (a quick numerical check of the first 50 terms confirms it).
FOLLOWUP: No head start
Same game, A flips first, but nobody has flipped yet. What is the probability that A wins?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
We need $a(3,3)$. Continue the recursion. $(3,1)$: $x = b(2,1) = 1/9$, $y = 0$: $a(3,1) = 2/27$. $(3,2)$: $x = b(2,2) = 11/27$, $y = a(3,1) = 2/27$: $a(3,2) = (22/27 + 2/27)/3 = 8/27$. $(3,3)$: $x = b(2,3) = 19/27$, $y = a(3,2) = 8/27$: $a(3,3) = (38/27 + 8/27)/3 = 46/81 \approx 0.568$. Moving first is worth about 7 percentage points.

TITLE: Coin decides how many die rolls
QUESTION:
Toss a fair coin until the first head appears; say this happens on toss $n$. Then roll a fair six-sided die $n$ times. What is the probability that at least one of the die rolls shows a 1?
HINT1:
Condition on $n$. It is easier to compute the probability of never seeing a 1.
HINT2:
$\mathbb{P}(N = n) = 2^{-n}$ and, given $N = n$, no 1 appears with probability $(5/6)^n$. Sum a geometric series.
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{2}{7}$
REFRESH:
- Law of total probability: $\mathbb{P}(A) = \sum_n \mathbb{P}(A \mid N = n)\,\mathbb{P}(N = n)$.
- Geometric series: $\sum_{n\ge1} x^n = \dfrac{x}{1-x}$ for $|x| < 1$.
EXPLANATION:
The number of rolls is random (geometric), so condition on it. Given $n$ rolls, "no 1" has probability $(5/6)^n$, and averaging over $n$ with weights $2^{-n}$ gives a geometric series in $5/12$. There is also a neat step-by-step view: pair each coin toss with one die roll; the process stops either when the die shows a 1 (success) or when the coin shows heads after a non-1 roll (failure).
SOLUTION: Condition on the number of rolls
$N$, the toss of the first head, satisfies $\mathbb{P}(N = n) = (1/2)^n$ for $n \ge 1$. Given $N = n$, the probability of no 1 in $n$ rolls is $(5/6)^n$. So
$$\mathbb{P}(\text{no } 1) = \sum_{n\ge1}\left(\frac12\right)^n\left(\frac56\right)^n = \sum_{n\ge1}\left(\frac5{12}\right)^n = \frac{5/12}{1 - 5/12} = \frac{5/12}{7/12} = \frac57.$$
Hence $\mathbb{P}(\text{at least one } 1) = 1 - \frac57 = \frac27$.
SOLUTION: Interleave coin and die

TITLE: Stop when heads pass one half
QUESTION:
You toss a fair coin repeatedly. After each toss, compute $r = \dfrac{\text{number of heads so far}}{\text{number of tosses so far}}$. HINT2:

$\dfrac{\pi}{4} \approx 0.785$
REFRESH:
- Catalan numbers: $C_k = \frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}$ counts $\pm1$ paths of length $2k$ from 0 back to 0 that never go above 0.
- A symmetric simple random walk started at 0 reaches $+1$ with probability 1.
- Binomial series: $\sum_{k\ge0}\binom{2k}{k}\frac{y^k}{4^k} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-y}}$ for $|y| < 1$.
EXPLANATION:
Let $S_m$ = heads minus tails after $m$ tosses, a symmetric random walk. $r > 1/2$ is the same as $S_m > 0$, and since $S$ moves by $\pm1$ you stop exactly when $S$ first hits $+1$. SOLUTION: First passage and an integral
Let $\tau$ be the stopping time. $S_\tau = 1$ and $\tau = 2k+1$ for some $k \ge 0$, with $k+1$ heads, so the recorded value is $r = \frac{k+1}{2k+1}$.

Distribution of $\tau$: the first $2k$ steps must go from 0 to 0 without ever exceeding 0 (there are $C_k$ such paths), then the next step is up. So
$$\mathbb{P}(\tau = 2k+1) = \frac{C_k}{2^{2k+1}} = \frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}\frac{1}{2^{2k+1}}.$$
Since the walk hits $+1$ almost surely, these probabilities sum to 1. Then
$$\mathbb{E}[r] = \sum_{k\ge0}\frac{k+1}{2k+1}\cdot\frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}\frac{1}{2^{2k+1}} = \frac12\sum_{k\ge0}\binom{2k}{k}\frac{1}{4^k}\cdot\frac{1}{2k+1}.$$

$$\mathbb{E}[r] = \frac12\int_0^1\sum_{k\ge0}\binom{2k}{k}\left(\frac{x^2}{4}\right)^k dx = \frac12\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} = \frac12\arcsin(1) = \frac12\cdot\frac\pi2 = \frac\pi4.$$

SOLUTION: Recognise the arcsine series
The Taylor series of $\arcsin$ is
$$\arcsin x = \sum_{k\ge0}\binom{2k}{k}\frac{x^{2k+1}}{4^k(2k+1)}, \qquad |x| \le 1.$$
From the first method, $\mathbb{E}[r] = \frac12\sum_{k\ge0}\binom{2k}{k}\frac{1}{4^k(2k+1)} = \frac12\arcsin(1) = \frac\pi4$. The first terms are $\frac12\left(1 + \frac16 + \frac{3}{40} + \frac{5}{112} + \dots\right)$; they converge slowly (terms decay like $k^{-3/2}$), which is why truncating early suggests a smaller number.
