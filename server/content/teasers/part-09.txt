IDS: H1B-05
SECTION: brainteaser
CATEGORY: Geometry & space

TITLE: Cubes on a chessboard
QUESTION:
You have a $20\times20$ chessboard and a huge box of identical cubes, each cube's face being exactly the size of one square. One edge of the board runs north–south. You put one cube on the northwest corner square. Every time you step one square south or one square east, the pile you place has one more cube than the pile on the square you came from.

So the first row reads $1, 2, 3, \dots, 20$, the second row $2, 3, 4, \dots, 21$, and so on down to the last row $20, 21, \dots, 39$: the square in row $i$ and column $j$ holds $i+j-1$ cubes.

How many cubes are on the board in total?
HINT1:
Picture the piles as a 3D solid. Is there a height at which you could cut it so that the part above the cut fits exactly into the gaps below?
HINT2:
Cut the solid horizontally at height $20$. The piles taller than $20$ sit in the southeast triangle; the piles shorter than $20$ sit in the northwest triangle. Compare the excess above 20 on square $(i,j)$ with the shortfall below 20 on the square reflected across the anti-diagonal. Alternatively, just compute $\sum_{i,j}(i+j-1)$.
ANSWER:
$20^3 = 8{,}000$ cubes.
REFRESH:
- Arithmetic sum: $\sum_{j=1}^{n} j = \dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$.
- A double sum of a sum splits: $\sum_{i}\sum_{j}(a_i + b_j) = n\sum_i a_i + n\sum_j b_j$ when both indices run over $n$ values.
EXPLANATION:
The heights $i+j-1$ grow linearly along both directions, so the solid is a "staircase ramp" that rises from $1$ in one corner to $39$ in the opposite corner. The average height is exactly the middle value $20$: indeed the height at $(i,j)$ plus the height at the "reflected" square $(21-j,\,21-i)$ is $(i+j-1)+(41-i-j)=40$. So every tall pile is paired with a short pile whose heights average to 20. Levelling the solid (moving the excess above 20 into the matching shortfall) gives a perfect $20\times20\times20$ block, i.e. $8{,}000$ cubes. For an $n\times n$ board the same argument gives $n^3$.
SOLUTION: Cut and flip (pairing)
Put square $(i,j)$ in row $i$ (counted from the north) and column $j$ (counted from the west); it carries $h(i,j)=i+j-1$ cubes, with $1\le i,j\le 20$.

Pair square $(i,j)$ with $(i',j')=(21-j,\,21-i)$, its reflection across the anti-diagonal. This is a bijection of the board onto itself (applying it twice gives back $(i,j)$), and
$$h(i,j)+h(i',j')=(i+j-1)+\big((21-j)+(21-i)-1\big)=40.$$
So if a pile has $20+d$ cubes, its partner has $20-d$. Cutting every pile at height 20 and moving the $d$ excess cubes onto the partner (which lacks exactly $d$) makes every pile exactly $20$ high. The squares on the anti-diagonal ($i+j=21$) already have height 20 and are their own partners.

The result is a $20\times20$ board with every pile of height 20, i.e. a solid cube of $20^3=8{,}000$ unit cubes. Since we only moved cubes, the total is $8{,}000$.
SOLUTION: Direct summation
For an $n\times n$ board,
$$\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(i+j-1)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} i+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} j-n^2 = n\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}+n\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-n^2.$$
This equals $n^2(n+1)-n^2=n^3$. For $n=20$ this is $8{,}000$ cubes.
===END===

CATEGORY: Clocks, rates & motion

TITLE: Escaping the dog
QUESTION:
You stand at the centre of a circular field of radius $R$ enclosed by a low wire fence. A large, hungry dog is attached to the fence and can only run along the perimeter. You run at speed $v$; the dog runs at $4v$ and will always do its best to be where you reach the fence. Find a running strategy that lets you get out of the field without being caught.
HINT1:
Running straight to the fence doesn't work: compare the time you need ($R/v$) with the time the dog needs to run half the perimeter. So first try to get into a better position. Think about angular speed rather than linear speed.
HINT2:
On a circle of radius $r$ your angular speed is $v/r$, the dog's is $4v/R$. For which $r$ can you circle faster (in angle) than the dog? Use this to get diametrically opposite to it, then run straight for the fence and check the times.
ANSWER:
Run in circles of radius $r$ slightly less than $R/4$ (any $r$ with $R(1-\pi/4)<r<R/4$) until the dog is diametrically opposite you, then sprint straight to the fence away from it.
REFRESH:
- Angular speed: moving at speed $v$ on a circle of radius $r$ sweeps angle at rate $\omega=v/r$ (radians per unit time).
- Half the circumference of a circle of radius $R$ is $\pi R$.
EXPLANATION:
The naive dash fails: you need $R/v$ to reach the fence, the dog needs at most $\pi R/(4v)\approx 0.785\,R/v$ to get to any point of the perimeter. You must start your dash closer to the fence *and* with the dog as far away as possible (on the opposite side).

The key is angular speed. Near the centre you can change your angle very quickly by running a small circle, while the dog, stuck on the big circle, sweeps angle at rate $4v/R$. On a circle of radius $r<R/4$ your angular rate $v/r$ beats the dog's, so you can lap it in angle and put yourself exactly opposite to it. From radius $r$ the dash is only $R-r$ long, while the dog must still run half the fence, $\pi R$. The dash works as long as $R-r<\pi R/4$, i.e. $r>R(1-\pi/4)\approx 0.215R$. Since $1-\pi/4<1/4$ (because $\pi>3$), there is a window of radii that works — and it is narrow, which is what makes the puzzle cute.
SOLUTION: Circle, then dash
**Why the naive strategy fails.** Running straight from the centre takes $R/v$. The dog is at worst half the perimeter away, which takes it $\dfrac{\pi R}{4v}<\dfrac{R}{v}$ since $\pi<4$. It gets there first.

**Gaining an angular advantage.** Let $r$ be a radius with $r<R/4$. Running round the circle of radius $r$ (centred at the field's centre) at full speed $v$, your angular speed is $v/r>4v/R$, the maximum angular speed of the dog. So whatever the dog does, the angle between you and the dog (seen from the centre) can be changed by you at a strictly positive net rate. After finite time you are at the point of your circle diametrically opposite the dog's position (angle difference $\pi$).

**The dash.** At that moment run radially outwards, straight to the fence. You cover $R-r$ in time $\dfrac{R-r}{v}$. The dog must cover half the perimeter in either direction, $\pi R$, which takes $\dfrac{\pi R}{4v}$ at least. You escape if
$$\frac{R-r}{v}<\frac{\pi R}{4v}\iff r>R\left(1-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\approx 0.2146\,R.$$

**Choosing $r$.** We need $R(1-\pi/4)<r<R/4$. This interval is non-empty because $1-\pi/4<1/4\iff\pi>3$. Writing $r=R/4-\varepsilon$, the condition is $0<\varepsilon<\dfrac{(\pi-3)R}{4}$, the book's form.

So: move out to a circle of radius $r=R/4-\varepsilon$ with $0<\varepsilon<(\pi-3)R/4$ (e.g. $r=0.24R$), circle until the dog is directly opposite, then run straight for the fence on the side away from the dog.
===END===

TITLE: The Gaussian integral
QUESTION:
Prove that
$$\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx=\sqrt{\pi}.$$
HINT1:
There is no elementary antiderivative of $e^{-x^2}$, so attack the square of the integral instead.
HINT2:
Write $I^2=\int e^{-x^2}dx\int e^{-y^2}dy$ as a double integral over the plane and switch to polar coordinates; don't forget the Jacobian $r$.
ANSWER:
$I^2=\pi$, so $I=\sqrt{\pi}$.
REFRESH:
- Polar coordinates: $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, $dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta$ (Jacobian $r$), with $r\in[0,\infty)$, $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$.
- Fubini–Tonelli: for a non-negative function, a double integral equals the iterated integral in either order, and $\int f(x)dx\int g(y)dy=\iint f(x)g(y)\,dx\,dy$.
- Standard normal density: $\varphi(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^2/2}$ integrates to 1.
EXPLANATION:
The function $e^{-x^2}$ has no closed-form antiderivative, so one cannot integrate it directly. The trick is to square the integral: the product of two copies is $\iint e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy$, and $x^2+y^2=r^2$ is rotation-invariant. In polar coordinates the area element brings an extra factor $r$, and $re^{-r^2}$ *does* have an easy antiderivative, $-\tfrac12 e^{-r^2}$. The radial integral gives $\tfrac12$, the angular one gives $2\pi$, so $I^2=\pi$.

If you are allowed to take as known that the normal density integrates to one, the result is just a change of variables — but that fact is usually proved by exactly this polar-coordinates argument, so the second method is the real proof.
SOLUTION: Polar coordinates
Let $I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx$; it is finite since $e^{-x^2}\le e^{1-2|x|}$ (because $x^2\ge 2|x|-1$), and $I>0$. By Tonelli (the integrand is non-negative),
$$I^2=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-y^2}dy=\iint_{\mathbb R^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy.$$
Change to polar coordinates $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. The Jacobian is
$$\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}=\begin{vmatrix}\cos\theta & -r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta\end{vmatrix}=r\cos^2\theta+r\sin^2\theta=r,$$
so
$$I^2=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\infty}e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta=\int_0^{2\pi}\left[-\tfrac12e^{-r^2}\right]_{r=0}^{r=\infty}d\theta=\int_0^{2\pi}\tfrac12\,d\theta=\pi.$$
Since $I>0$, $I=\sqrt{\pi}$.
SOLUTION: From the normal density
Assume known that $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^2/2}\,du=1$. Substitute $u=\sqrt2\,x$, $du=\sqrt2\,dx$:
$$1=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2}\sqrt2\,dx=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx,$$
hence $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx=\sqrt\pi$. (This is circular if the normalisation of the normal density was itself proved via the Gaussian integral, which is the usual route.)
===END===

TITLE: Integral of sec
QUESTION:
What is $\displaystyle\int\sec\theta\,d\theta$?
HINT1:
Try to make the integrand look like $u'/u$ for some cleverly chosen $u$.
HINT2:
Multiply numerator and denominator by $\sec\theta+\tan\theta$. Then compute the derivative of $\sec\theta+\tan\theta$.
ANSWER:
$\displaystyle\int\sec\theta\,d\theta=\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|+C$.
REFRESH:
- $\sec\theta=\dfrac1{\cos\theta}$, $\tan\theta=\dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$, $\csc\theta=\dfrac1{\sin\theta}$, $\cot\theta=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}$.
- $(\tan\theta)'=\sec^2\theta$ and $(\sec\theta)'=\sec\theta\tan\theta$ (quotient rule).
- $\displaystyle\int\frac{u'(\theta)}{u(\theta)}\,d\theta=\ln|u(\theta)|+C$.
EXPLANATION:
The derivatives of $\sec$ and $\tan$ are "cross-linked": $(\sec)'=\sec\tan$ and $(\tan)'=\sec^2$. So the derivative of $\sec+\tan$ is $\sec\tan+\sec^2=\sec(\sec+\tan)$, i.e. $\sec$ times the function itself. That means $\sec\theta=\dfrac{(\sec\theta+\tan\theta)'}{\sec\theta+\tan\theta}$, a logarithmic derivative, and the integral is a log. It's a trick worth memorising; interviewers like it because it's the least obvious of the basic trig integrals.
SOLUTION: Logarithmic derivative
Let $u(\theta)=\sec\theta+\tan\theta$. Then
$$u'(\theta)=\sec\theta\tan\theta+\sec^2\theta=\sec\theta\,(\tan\theta+\sec\theta)=\sec\theta\,u(\theta).$$
Hence, wherever $u\neq0$,
$$\int\sec\theta\,d\theta=\int\frac{\sec\theta(\sec\theta+\tan\theta)}{\sec\theta+\tan\theta}\,d\theta=\int\frac{u'(\theta)}{u(\theta)}\,d\theta=\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|+C.$$
Check by differentiating: $\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\ln|u|=\dfrac{u'}{u}=\sec\theta$. ✓
SOLUTION: Substitution $s=\sin\theta$
Write $\sec\theta=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{\cos^2\theta}=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{1-\sin^2\theta}$ and put $s=\sin\theta$, $ds=\cos\theta\,d\theta$:
$$\int\frac{ds}{1-s^2}=\frac12\int\left(\frac1{1-s}+\frac1{1+s}\right)ds=\frac12\ln\left|\frac{1+s}{1-s}\right|+C.$$
Now $\dfrac{1+s}{1-s}=\dfrac{(1+s)^2}{1-s^2}=\dfrac{(1+\sin\theta)^2}{\cos^2\theta}=(\sec\theta+\tan\theta)^2$, so $\frac12\ln\left|\frac{1+s}{1-s}\right|=\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|$, the same answer.
FOLLOWUP: The other trig functions
Give the antiderivatives and derivatives of $\sin\theta$, $\cos\theta$, $\tan\theta$, $\cot\theta$, $\sec\theta$ and $\csc\theta$ (cosec).
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
Up to constants:

| $f(\theta)$ | $\int f$ | $f'$ |
|---|---|---|
| $\sin\theta$ | $-\cos\theta$ | $\cos\theta$ |
| $\cos\theta$ | $\sin\theta$ | $-\sin\theta$ |
| $\tan\theta$ | $\ln\lvert\sec\theta\rvert=-\ln\lvert\cos\theta\rvert$ | $\sec^2\theta$ |
| $\cot\theta$ | $\ln\lvert\sin\theta\rvert$ | $-\csc^2\theta$ |
| $\sec\theta$ | $\ln\lvert\sec\theta+\tan\theta\rvert$ | $\sec\theta\tan\theta$ |
| $\csc\theta$ | $\ln\lvert\tan\tfrac{\theta}{2}\rvert=-\ln\lvert\csc\theta+\cot\theta\rvert$ | $-\csc\theta\cot\theta$ |

Derivations: $\tan=\frac{\sin}{\cos}=-\frac{(\cos)'}{\cos}$, so $\int\tan=-\ln|\cos|$; $\cot=\frac{(\sin)'}{\sin}$, so $\int\cot=\ln|\sin|$. For $\csc$, use the same trick as for $\sec$: $(\csc+\cot)'=-\csc\cot-\csc^2=-\csc(\csc+\cot)$, so $\int\csc=-\ln|\csc+\cot|$; and $\csc\theta+\cot\theta=\frac{1+\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}=\frac{2\cos^2(\theta/2)}{2\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2)}=\cot\frac\theta2$, giving $\ln|\tan\frac\theta2|$. The derivatives come from the quotient rule, e.g. $(\sec)'=\left(\frac1{\cos}\right)'=\frac{\sin}{\cos^2}=\sec\tan$.

TITLE: Does $\sum e^{-\sqrt n}$ converge?
QUESTION:
Does the infinite series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-\sqrt n}$ converge?
HINT1:
The ratio and root tests are inconclusive here (both limits equal 1). Think of a test that compares the sum to something you can compute exactly, or to a series you know.
HINT2:
Either use the integral test with $\int_1^\infty e^{-\sqrt x}\,dx$ (substitute $u=\sqrt x$ and integrate by parts), or compare with $\sum 1/n^2$ by showing $e^{-\sqrt n}<1/n^2$ for $n$ large.
ANSWER:
Yes, it converges (its value is about $1.670$, between $4/e\approx1.47$ and $5/e\approx1.84$).
REFRESH:
- Integral test: if $f$ is positive, continuous and decreasing on $[1,\infty)$, then $\sum_{n\ge1}f(n)$ and $\int_1^\infty f(x)dx$ converge or diverge together; moreover $\int_1^\infty f\le\sum_{n\ge1}f(n)\le f(1)+\int_1^\infty f$.
- Comparison test: if $0\le a_n\le b_n$ for all $n\ge N$ and $\sum b_n<\infty$ then $\sum a_n<\infty$.
- $\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^2=\pi^2/6<\infty$ (more generally $\sum 1/n^p$ converges iff $p>1$).
- Exponentials beat polynomials: $u^k e^{-u}\to0$ as $u\to\infty$ for every $k$.
EXPLANATION:
The terms go to zero, but only "sub-exponentially": $e^{-\sqrt n}$ decays more slowly than any $e^{-cn}$, which is why the ratio test fails ($a_{n+1}/a_n=e^{-(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n)}\to1$). But it still decays much faster than any power of $n$: taking logs, $e^{-\sqrt n}<n^{-2}\iff\sqrt n>2\ln n$, which is true for large $n$ because $\sqrt n$ grows faster than $\ln n$. So the series is dominated by $\sum 1/n^2$ and converges. The integral test gives the same verdict and even a numerical bracket for the sum.
SOLUTION: Integral test
Let $f(x)=e^{-\sqrt x}$, positive, continuous and decreasing on $[1,\infty)$. Substitute $u=\sqrt x$, $x=u^2$, $dx=2u\,du$:
$$\int_1^{\infty}e^{-\sqrt x}\,dx=\int_1^\infty 2u\,e^{-u}\,du.$$
By parts, $\int 2ue^{-u}du=-2ue^{-u}+\int2e^{-u}du=-2(1+u)e^{-u}$. Therefore
$$\int_1^\infty 2ue^{-u}\,du=\Big[-2(1+u)e^{-u}\Big]_1^\infty=0+2\cdot2\cdot e^{-1}=\frac4e,$$
using $(1+u)e^{-u}\to0$. The integral is finite, so by the integral test the series converges.

The integral test also brackets the sum: $\frac4e\le\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-\sqrt n}\le e^{-1}+\frac4e=\frac5e$, i.e. $1.47\le S\le1.84$; numerically $S\approx1.670$.

Note: the book says the sum is "only slightly below $4/e$". That's wrong: for a decreasing positive $f$, $\sum_{n\ge1}f(n)\ge\int_1^\infty f$, so the sum is *above* $4/e$ (about $1.670$ vs $1.472$).
SOLUTION: Comparison with $\sum 1/n^2$
We have $e^{-\sqrt n}<\dfrac1{n^2}\iff\sqrt n>2\ln n$. The function $g(x)=\sqrt x-2\ln x$ has $g'(x)=\frac1{2\sqrt x}-\frac2x>0$ for $x>16$, so it is increasing there, and $g(75)=8.660-8.635>0$. So $e^{-\sqrt n}<1/n^2$ for all $n\ge75$ (it fails at $n=74$, where $\sqrt{74}\approx8.602<2\ln74\approx8.608$). Since $\sum1/n^2=\pi^2/6$ converges, the comparison test gives convergence of $\sum e^{-\sqrt n}$.

(Any exponent works: $e^{-\sqrt n}\le C_k n^{-k}$ for every $k$, because $u^{2k}e^{-u}$ is bounded. So the series converges "very comfortably".)

SLUG: sums-of-squares-and-cubes
IDS: H1B-13
SECTION: brainteaser
CATEGORY: Induction & recursion
DIFFICULTY: medium
TITLE: Sums of squares and cubes
QUESTION:
Find closed forms for $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^2$ and $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^3$.
HINT1:
You probably know $\sum k=\frac{n(n+1)}2$. Look for an identity that turns $k^2$ (or $k^3$) into something that telescopes.
HINT2:
Sum the identity $(k+1)^3-k^3=3k^2+3k+1$ from $k=1$ to $n$: the left side telescopes. Do the same with $(k+1)^4-k^4$ for cubes. (Or guess a polynomial of degree 3, resp. 4, and prove it by induction.)
ANSWER:
$\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n k^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$ and $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n k^3=\frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4}=\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2$.
REFRESH:
- Telescoping: $\sum_{k=1}^n\big(g(k+1)-g(k)\big)=g(n+1)-g(1)$.
- Binomial expansions: $(k+1)^3=k^3+3k^2+3k+1$, $(k+1)^4=k^4+4k^3+6k^2+4k+1$.
- Induction: prove $P(1)$ and $P(n)\Rightarrow P(n+1)$.
EXPLANATION:
The sum of $k^p$ over $k\le n$ is a polynomial in $n$ of degree $p+1$ (think of it as a discrete integral of $x^p$). There are two standard ways to find it. The telescoping trick expresses $k^p$ through the difference $(k+1)^{p+1}-k^{p+1}$, whose sum collapses to two terms; you then solve for the unknown sum using the lower-order sums you already know. The other route is to guess the polynomial (from a few values, or from the rising-factorial pattern $\sum k(k+1)=\frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}3$) and check it by induction. A memorable fact: the sum of the first $n$ cubes is the square of the sum of the first $n$ integers.
SOLUTION: Telescoping
**Squares.** Summing $(k+1)^3-k^3=3k^2+3k+1$ for $k=1,\dots,n$:
$$(n+1)^3-1=3\sum_{k=1}^n k^2+3\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}+n.$$
So
$$3\sum k^2=(n+1)^3-(n+1)-\frac{3n(n+1)}{2}=(n+1)\left[(n+1)^2-1-\frac{3n}{2}\right].$$
The bracket is $n^2+2n-\frac{3n}{2}=n^2+\frac n2=\frac{n(2n+1)}{2}$, hence $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n k^2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$.

**Cubes.** Summing $(k+1)^4-k^4=4k^3+6k^2+4k+1$:
$$(n+1)^4-1=4\sum k^3+6\cdot\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6+4\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}2+n.$$
So $4\sum k^3=(n+1)^4-(n+1)-n(n+1)(2n+1)-2n(n+1)=(n+1)\left[(n+1)^3-1-n(2n+1)-2n\right]$. The bracket is $n^3+3n^2+3n-2n^2-n-2n=n^3+n^2=n^2(n+1)$. Hence
$$\sum_{k=1}^n k^3=\frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4}.$$
SOLUTION: Guess and prove by induction
Guess that $\sum_{k=1}^n k^2=an^3+bn^2+cn+d$ and fit it on $n=1,2,3,4$ (values $1,5,14,30$): solving the linear system gives $a=\frac13$, $b=\frac12$, $c=\frac16$, $d=0$, i.e. $f(n)=\frac{2n^3+3n^2+n}{6}=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6$.

Induction: $f(1)=1$. If $f(n)=\sum_{k\le n}k^2$, then
$$f(n)+(n+1)^2=\frac{2n^3+3n^2+n+6n^2+12n+6}{6}=\frac{2n^3+9n^2+13n+6}{6}=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)(2n+3)}{6}=f(n+1).$$
For cubes, let $h(n)=\frac{n^2(n+1)^2}4$. Then $h(1)=1$ and
$$h(n)+(n+1)^3=\frac{(n+1)^2\left(n^2+4(n+1)\right)}{4}=\frac{(n+1)^2(n+2)^2}{4}=h(n+1).$$
SOLUTION: Rising-factorial sums

$$\sum k^2=\sum k(k+1)-\sum k=\frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}3-\frac{n(n+1)}2=\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6.$$

$$\sum k^3=\frac{n(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)}4-n(n+1)(n+2)+\frac{n(n+1)}2=\frac{n(n+1)}{4}\big[(n+2)(n+3)-4(n+2)+2\big]=\frac{n(n+1)}4\,(n^2+n)=\frac{n^2(n+1)^2}4.$$

- Point symmetry about $O$: the map $P\mapsto P'$ with $O$ the midpoint of $PP'$ (rotation by $180^\circ$). A region is centrally symmetric if this map sends it onto itself.

**Opening.** Go first and place your quarter with its centre at $O$ (possible on any convex symmetric table big enough to hold a coin).

**Invariant.** After each of your moves, the set of coins is symmetric about $O$. True after the first move. Suppose it holds and the opponent places a coin centred at $P$. Before their move $P$ was a legal spot, so by symmetry $P'$ was also legal. Could the new coin at $P$ overlap a coin at $P'$? Two coins of radius $\rho$ overlap iff their centres are less than $2\rho$ apart, and $|PP'|=2|OP|$. Colour square $(x,y)$ black if $x+y$ is even and white if $x+y$ is odd. Adjacent squares differ by 1 in exactly one coordinate, so they have opposite colours: each domino covers exactly one black and one white square. Hence $k$ non-overlapping dominoes cover $k$ black and $k$ white squares.

SOLUTION: Coordinate-sum parity
A domino covers $(x,y),(x+1,y)$ or $(x,y),(x,y+1)$; the sum of all four coordinates is $2x+2y+1$, which is odd. A tiling of 62 squares uses $31$ dominoes, so the total coordinate sum of the covered squares would be a sum of 31 odd numbers: odd.

FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:

One exists (writing squares as (column, row)): go up column 1 from $(1,1)$ to $(1,8)$; then snake through columns 2–8 row by row — row 8 from column 2 to 8, row 7 from column 8 back to 2, row 6 from 2 to 8, and so on — so that row 1 is traversed from column 8 to column 2; finally step from $(2,1)$ back to $(1,1)$. Along the cycle, colours alternate.

Remove the two marked squares. If they are adjacent on the cycle, what remains is a single path of $62$ squares; pair consecutive squares along it into dominoes. Otherwise the cycle breaks into two arcs. Since the marked squares have opposite colours and colours alternate along the cycle, the number of squares strictly between them along either arc is even (from a black square to a white square takes an odd number of steps, so an even number of squares lie between). Each arc is a path of even length whose consecutive squares are adjacent, so it can be tiled by dominoes laid along it. Hence the 62 squares can always be tiled.

REFRESH:
- For $S\sim U[0,1]$ and a function $g$, $E[g(S)]=\int_0^1 g(s)\,ds$.
- Conditioning a uniform: given $S\le B$ (with $B\le1$), $S\sim U[0,B]$, so $E[S\mid S\le B]=B/2$.
EXPLANATION:
The trap is to think "the firm is worth $2S$ to me and on average $S=1/2$, so bidding anything below 1 is profitable". But your bid is only accepted when $S\le B$, and in that event the firm is worth on average $E[2S\mid S\le B]=2\cdot\frac B2=B$ — exactly what you pay. Acceptance is bad news about the firm's value (the winner's curse). With a doubling, the conditional value exactly offsets the price for every $B\le 1$, so the expected profit is zero whatever you bid in $[0,1]$. Bidding above 1 is pure waste: every extra dollar is paid with certainty and buys nothing.

(With a general multiplier $k$ the expected payoff for $B\le1$ is $B^2\left(\frac k2-1\right)$: for $k<2$, e.g. the classic "+50%" version, the best bid is $0$; for $k>2$ you'd bid $1$. Doubling is the knife-edge case.)
SOLUTION: Direct expectation
Payoff: $P=(2S-B)\,\mathbf 1_{\{S\le B\}}$, for a bid $B\ge0$.

**Case $B\le1$:**
$$E[P]=\int_0^{B}(2s-B)\,ds=\Big[s^2-Bs\Big]_0^B=B^2-B^2=0.$$
**Case $B>1$:** the bid is always accepted:
$$E[P]=\int_0^{1}(2s-B)\,ds=1-B<0.$$
So the maximum expected payoff is $0$, attained by any $B\in[0,1]$ (including not bidding at all). Bid no more than 1 and expect to break even.
SOLUTION: Conditioning on acceptance
For $0<B\le1$: $P(\text{accept})=P(S\le B)=B$ and, given acceptance, $S\sim U[0,B]$, so $E[2S\mid\text{accept}]=B$. Hence
$$E[P]=P(\text{accept})\cdot\big(E[2S\mid\text{accept}]-B\big)=B\cdot(B-B)=0.$$
For $B>1$, acceptance is certain and $E[P]=E[2S]-B=1-B<0$.

- Arc length on a circle: length $=$ radius $\times$ angle in radians.

At the North Pole this is automatic: from the pole, go one mile south, walk east any amount, and walking north one mile brings you back to the pole along whichever meridian you are on. Near the South Pole the circles of latitude become arbitrarily small. SOLUTION: Classify the walks

**The North Pole.** From $A=$ North Pole, $B$ is one mile from the pole; the east leg keeps you on the circle of latitude at that distance; so $C$ is one mile from the pole and walking one mile north returns you to it. So $D=A$.

**Other points.** If $A$ is not the North Pole, then $A$ and $B$ lie on the same meridian, and $D$ lies on the meridian of $C$ at the latitude of $A$. So $D=A$ iff $C$ is on the meridian of $B$, i.e. iff the one-mile east leg is a whole number $n\ge1$ of laps around its circle of latitude. $$1+E\arcsin\!\left(\frac{1}{2\pi nE}\right)\ \text{miles from the South Pole},\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots$$

REFRESH:
- A plane mirror at $z=0$ maps $(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,y,-z)$: coordinates parallel to the mirror are unchanged, the perpendicular one changes sign.
- A reflection reverses handedness (orientation); a rotation preserves it.
EXPLANATION:
Physically, a flat mirror is a reflection in its own plane: every point keeps its position along the two directions parallel to the mirror and has its distance to the mirror reversed. Your watch hand, on the east side of your body, is reflected to an image on the east side; your head is reflected to an image that is still up. What changes is depth: you face the mirror (say north), your reflection faces south.

Why then do we say "left and right are swapped"? Because to compare yourself with your reflection you imagine walking round and turning to face you — a rotation of $180^\circ$ about a vertical axis (the natural way humans turn). After that rotation, your front matches its front and your head matches its head, but your watch hand is on the opposite side, so the mismatch shows up as left–right. Had you instead imagined flipping head over heels (rotation about a horizontal left–right axis) to face the mirror, fronts and hands would match but heads and feet would be swapped, and you'd say the mirror flips up and down. The reflection changes handedness, and no rotation can undo that; which axis "gets the blame" depends on how you mentally rotate.
SOLUTION: Coordinates
Put the mirror in the plane $z=0$ with $x$ pointing (say) east along the mirror, $y$ up, and $z$ perpendicular to the mirror, towards you. Reflection maps the point $(x,y,z)$ to $(x,y,-z)$.

- Your watch at $x=-a$ (on the west side, your left when facing the mirror) is imaged at $x=-a$: still on the west side. No left–right swap in space.
- Your head at height $y=h$ is imaged at $y=h$: no up–down swap.
- Your nose, closer to the mirror than the back of your head, is imaged closer to the mirror too, so the image's nose points towards you: front and back are reversed.

So the mirror reverses only the perpendicular axis. The image is a left-handed copy of a right-handed person: its orientation is reversed. A person is (roughly) left–right symmetric but not up–down or front–back symmetric, so we try to match the image to ourselves by a rotation that realigns head and front, i.e. a half-turn about the vertical axis. A half-turn about the vertical axis maps $(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,y,-z)$; composing it with the reflection gives $(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,y,z)$, a left–right reflection. That's the "left–right flip" we perceive. Rotating instead about the $x$-axis, $(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,-y,-z)$, would make the residual map $(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,-y,z)$, an up–down flip. A check: write a sentence on a transparent sheet so that you can read it, and hold it between you and the mirror; its image still reads normally from left to right, confirming that the mirror itself doesn't swap left and right.

Let $D$ be the number of dots, uniform on $\{1,\dots,6\}$. Then
$$E[D]=\frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6}=\frac{21}{6}=3.5.$$
So the break-even (fair) ticket price is \$3.50. To make money over many plays, charge \$3.50 plus a margin; for a one-off game add a risk premium on top. The price must stay below \$6 for anyone to want to play.
FOLLOWUP: An $n$-sided die
What is the expected score of a fair $n$-sided die with faces $1,2,\dots,n$?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$$E[D]=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^nk=\frac1n\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}2=\frac{n+1}2.$$
For $n=6$ this gives $3.5$, as before.
FOLLOWUP: Sum of two rolls
What is the expected sum of two rolls of a fair die?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
By linearity of expectation (independence isn't even needed), $E[D_1+D_2]=E[D_1]+E[D_2]=3.5+3.5=7$.
FOLLOWUP: Probability of a seven
What is the probability that the sum of two rolls of a fair die equals 7?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
The 36 ordered outcomes are equally likely. A sum of 7 occurs for $(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)$: 6 outcomes. So $P=\frac6{36}=\frac16$. (Equivalently: whatever the first roll is, exactly one value of the second roll makes 7, so the probability is $\frac16$. Seven is the most likely sum.)
===END===

TITLE: Five coins versus four coins
QUESTION:
I toss four fair coins and you toss five fair coins. You win if you get strictly more heads than I do. What is the probability that you win?
HINT1:
Brute force works but there's a symmetry argument that avoids all computation. Set aside your fifth coin for a moment.
HINT2:
Compare your first four coins with my four. Either you're ahead, I'm ahead, or it's a tie — how does your fifth coin settle each case? Alternatively, consider both heads *and* tails.
ANSWER:
$\dfrac12$.
REFRESH:
- Law of total probability: $P(A)=\sum_i P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)$ for a partition $\{B_i\}$.
- If $X$ and $Y$ are i.i.d., then $P(X>Y)=P(Y>X)$.
- For a fair coin, the number of heads $H$ and the number of tails $n-H$ have the same distribution.
EXPLANATION:
Split your five coins into the first four and a last one. Your first four and my four are identically distributed, so by symmetry "you ahead" and "I'm ahead" have the same probability $q$, and a tie has probability $1-2q$. If you're ahead after four, you win regardless of the fifth coin (you already have strictly more). If you're behind, you can at best tie, so you lose. If it's a tie, your fifth coin decides: you win with probability $\frac12$. Total: $q+\frac12(1-2q)=\frac12$. The unknown $q$ cancels, so no computation is needed.
SOLUTION: Set aside the fifth coin
Let $Y_4$ be your heads among your first four coins, $\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}$ your fifth coin, and $X$ my heads. $Y_4$ and $X$ are i.i.d. Binomial$(4,\frac12)$, so $P(Y_4>X)=P(X>Y_4)=:q$ and $P(Y_4=X)=1-2q$.

You win iff $Y_4+\varepsilon>X$:
- If $Y_4>X$: then $Y_4+\varepsilon\ge Y_4>X$, you win.
- If $Y_4<X$: then $Y_4+\varepsilon\le Y_4+1\le X$, you lose.
- If $Y_4=X$: you win iff $\varepsilon=1$, probability $\frac12$ (independent of the rest).

Hence $P(\text{win})=q+\frac12(1-2q)=\frac12$.
SOLUTION: Heads versus tails symmetry
Let $Y$ be your heads (out of 5) and $X$ mine (out of 4); your tails are $5-Y$, mine $4-X$. Since $Y$ and $X$ are integers, exactly one of $Y>X$ and $5-Y>4-X$ holds: indeed $5-Y>4-X\iff Y<X+1\iff Y\le X$. So "you have more heads" and "you have more tails" are complementary events. By fairness of the coins, flipping every coin's label (heads $\leftrightarrow$ tails) doesn't change the joint distribution, so the two events have the same probability. Thus each has probability $\frac12$.
SOLUTION: Direct computation
$P(X=x)=\binom4x/16$ and $P(Y>x)=\sum_{y>x}\binom5y/32$. The tail counts $\sum_{y>x}\binom5y$ for $x=0,\dots,4$ are $31,26,16,6,1$. So
$$P(Y>X)=\frac{1\cdot31+4\cdot26+6\cdot16+4\cdot6+1\cdot1}{16\cdot32}=\frac{31+104+96+24+1}{512}=\frac{256}{512}=\frac12.$$
===END===
