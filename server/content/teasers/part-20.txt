IDS: Q-431
FIRMS: WorldQuant
SECTION: trading
CATEGORY: Markets & risk
DIFFICULTY: hard
TITLE: Maximum-Sharpe mix of two assets
QUESTION:
Two assets $A$ and $B$ have expected excess returns $\mu_A = 7\%$ and $\mu_B = 4\%$, and the covariance matrix of their excess returns is
$$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
You hold $a$ units of $A$ and $b$ units of $B$ with $a + b = 1$ (short positions allowed). How many units $a$ of asset $A$ maximise the expected Sharpe ratio of the portfolio?
HINT1:
The Sharpe ratio does not change if you scale the whole portfolio by a positive constant. So first find the best *direction* of the weight vector, then rescale so the weights add up to 1.
HINT2:
Maximising $\dfrac{w^\top\mu}{\sqrt{w^\top\Sigma w}}$ over all $w$ gives $w \propto \Sigma^{-1}\mu$ (the tangency portfolio). Invert the $2\times 2$ matrix and normalise.
ANSWER:
$a = 10/7$ (and $b = -3/7$: short some $B$).
REFRESH:
**Inverse of a $2\times2$ matrix.** $\begin{pmatrix} p & q \\ r & s\end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{ps-qr}\begin{pmatrix} s & -q \\ -r & p\end{pmatrix}$.

**Portfolio moments.** With weights $w$, the expected excess return is $w^\top\mu$ and the variance is $w^\top \Sigma w$.

**Cauchy–Schwarz.** For a positive definite $\Sigma$, $(w^\top\mu)^2 \le (w^\top\Sigma w)(\mu^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu)$, with equality iff $w \propto \Sigma^{-1}\mu$.
EXPLANATION:
The Sharpe ratio $S(w) = w^\top\mu / \sqrt{w^\top\Sigma w}$ is homogeneous of degree 0: doubling every position doubles both the numerator and the standard deviation. So the constraint $a+b=1$ only fixes the scale; the real question is the direction of $w$, and the answer to that is the classic tangency portfolio $w \propto \Sigma^{-1}\mu$.

The interesting feature is that $B$ gets a negative weight even though its expected return is positive. $A$ and $B$ are strongly correlated ($\rho = 1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.71$), and $B$ pays relatively little per unit of risk, so the optimal portfolio uses $B$ as a hedge: shorting a bit of $B$ removes some of $A$'s risk while giving up only a small amount of return.
SOLUTION: Tangency portfolio $\Sigma^{-1}\mu$
Scale-invariance: $S(cw) = S(w)$ for $c>0$. By Cauchy–Schwarz in the inner product defined by $\Sigma$,
$$w^\top\mu = (\Sigma^{1/2}w)^\top(\Sigma^{-1/2}\mu) \le \sqrt{w^\top\Sigma w}\,\sqrt{\mu^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu},$$
with equality iff $w \propto \Sigma^{-1}\mu$. So the maximum Sharpe ratio is attained at $w \propto \Sigma^{-1}\mu$.

$\det\Sigma = 1\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 1 = 1$, so
$$\Sigma^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma^{-1}\mu = \begin{pmatrix} 2\cdot 7 - 4 \\ -7 + 4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ -3\end{pmatrix}$$
(working with returns in percent; the scale does not matter). Normalising so the weights sum to 1: the sum is $10 - 3 = 7$, hence
$$a = \frac{10}{7}, \qquad b = -\frac{3}{7}.$$
Check: expected return $\frac{70 - 12}{7} = \frac{58}{7}$, variance $a^2 + 2ab + 2b^2 = \frac{100 - 60 + 18}{49} = \frac{58}{49}$, so $S = \frac{58/7}{\sqrt{58}/7} = \sqrt{58} \approx 7.62$, which equals $\sqrt{\mu^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu} = \sqrt{7\cdot10 + 4\cdot(-3)} = \sqrt{58}$, as it must.
SOLUTION: One-variable calculus on the budget line
Set $b = 1-a$. Then
$$m(a) = 7a + 4(1-a) = 4 + 3a, \qquad v(a) = a^2 + 2a(1-a) + 2(1-a)^2 = a^2 - 2a + 2.$$
Maximise $S(a) = m/\sqrt v$, equivalently set the derivative of $\ln S = \ln m - \tfrac12\ln v$ to zero:
$$\frac{m'}{m} = \frac{v'}{2v} \iff 3(a^2 - 2a + 2) = (4 + 3a)(a - 1).$$
Left: $3a^2 - 6a + 6$. Right: $3a^2 + a - 4$. So $-6a + 6 = a - 4$, i.e. $7a = 10$, $a = 10/7$. At this point $m = 58/7 > 0$, and $S(a) \to \pm 3$ as $a\to\pm\infty$ while $S(10/7) = \sqrt{58} > 3$, so it is the maximum.

TITLE: Three marksmen taking turns
QUESTION:
Three marksmen $X$, $Y$ and $Z$ shoot at a target in the order $X, Y, Z, X, Y, Z, \dots$ until someone hits it. Each shot hits with probability $1/2$, independently of all other shots. What is the probability that $X$ is the first to hit the target?
HINT1:
Think about what happens if the first full round ($X$, $Y$, $Z$) produces no hit at all. What does the game look like afterwards?
HINT2:
Let $p$ be the answer. Either $X$ hits immediately, or all three miss and the game restarts from scratch: $p = \tfrac12 + \tfrac18\,p$.
ANSWER:
$4/7$
REFRESH:
**Geometric series.** For $|r|<1$, $\sum_{k\ge0} r^k = \frac{1}{1-r}$.
EXPLANATION:
The game is memoryless: after a round of three misses nothing has changed, so the situation is exactly the starting one. That gives a one-line equation. Equivalently, the three players' chances of being first are in the ratio $\tfrac12 : \tfrac14 : \tfrac18 = 4:2:1$ (Y needs X to miss first, Z needs both to miss), and they add up to 1, so X gets $4/7$, Y $2/7$, Z $1/7$. Going first is a big advantage when the hit probability is high.
SOLUTION: First-step (renewal) equation
Let $p = P(X \text{ hits first})$. On the first shot, $X$ hits with probability $1/2$ and wins. Otherwise $Y$ and $Z$ must both miss too (probability $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac18$ for the whole round), after which the game starts afresh. If $Y$ or $Z$ hits in the first round, $X$ loses. Hence
$$p = \frac12 + \frac18 p \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac78 p = \frac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = \frac47.$$
SOLUTION: Sum over rounds
$X$ is first to hit on his $k$-th shot ($k = 1,2,\dots$) iff the first $k-1$ rounds are all misses (probability $(1/8)^{k-1}$) and then $X$ hits ($1/2$):
$$p = \sum_{k\ge1}\frac12\Big(\frac18\Big)^{k-1} = \frac{1/2}{1 - 1/8} = \frac{1/2}{7/8} = \frac47.$$
SOLUTION: Ratio argument
Within any round that is reached, $X$ ends the game with probability $\tfrac12$, $Y$ with $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14$, $Z$ with $\tfrac18$. Since each round is a fresh copy, the probabilities of eventually being first are proportional to these: $4:2:1$. The game ends almost surely, so they sum to 1, giving $P(X) = \frac{4}{7}$.
===END===

SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Distributions

TITLE: One normal given the sum
QUESTION:
Let $X_1 \sim N(0, 9)$ and $X_2 \sim N(0, 16)$ be independent (the second parameter is the variance). Compute $\mathbb{E}[X_1 \mid X_1 + X_2 = 5]$.
HINT1:
The pair $(X_1, X_1+X_2)$ is jointly Gaussian. For jointly Gaussian variables, the conditional expectation is linear in the conditioning variable.
HINT2:
Write $\mathbb{E}[X_1\mid S] = \beta S$ with $S = X_1 + X_2$ and $\beta = \mathrm{Cov}(X_1,S)/\mathrm{Var}(S)$.
ANSWER:
$9/5 = 1.8$
REFRESH:
**Gaussian conditioning.** If $(U,V)$ is bivariate normal, then $\mathbb{E}[U\mid V=v] = \mathbb{E}U + \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(U,V)}{\mathrm{Var}(V)}(v - \mathbb{E}V)$.

**Covariance of independent sums.** If $X_1, X_2$ are independent, $\mathrm{Cov}(X_1, X_1+X_2) = \mathrm{Var}(X_1)$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X_1+X_2) = \mathrm{Var}X_1 + \mathrm{Var}X_2$.
EXPLANATION:
Knowing the total is 5, you have to decide how much of that 5 came from $X_1$ and how much from $X_2$. The natural answer is to split it in proportion to how much each one varies: $X_1$ carries variance 9 and $X_2$ variance 16, out of a total 25. So $X_1$ gets $9/25$ of the sum, i.e. $\frac{9}{25}\cdot 5 = \frac95$, and $X_2$ gets $\frac{16}{25}\cdot 5 = \frac{16}{5}$. The two conditional means add back up to 5, as they must. This is the same formula as a regression coefficient, and the same idea as the Kalman filter's gain.
SOLUTION: Regression coefficient
$S = X_1 + X_2 \sim N(0, 25)$ and $(X_1, S)$ is jointly Gaussian (a linear image of the Gaussian vector $(X_1,X_2)$). By independence,
$$\mathrm{Cov}(X_1, S) = \mathrm{Var}(X_1) + \mathrm{Cov}(X_1,X_2) = 9, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(S) = 9 + 16 = 25.$$
By the Gaussian conditioning formula (both means are 0),
$$\mathbb{E}[X_1\mid S = 5] = \frac{9}{25}\cdot 5 = \frac95.$$
SOLUTION: Decomposition into independent parts
Write $X_1 = \frac{9}{25} S + R$ where $R = X_1 - \frac{9}{25}S$. Then $\mathrm{Cov}(R, S) = 9 - \frac{9}{25}\cdot 25 = 0$; since $(R,S)$ is jointly Gaussian, zero covariance means $R$ is independent of $S$, and $\mathbb{E}R = 0$. Therefore
$$\mathbb{E}[X_1 \mid S = 5] = \frac{9}{25}\cdot 5 + \mathbb{E}[R] = \frac95.$$
SOLUTION: Direct density computation
The conditional density of $X_1$ given $S = 5$ is proportional to $f_{X_1}(x) f_{X_2}(5-x) \propto \exp\!\Big(-\frac{x^2}{18} - \frac{(5-x)^2}{32}\Big)$. The exponent is a concave quadratic in $x$, so this is a normal density whose mean is the maximiser. Setting the derivative to zero: $-\frac{x}{9} + \frac{5-x}{16} = 0 \iff 16x = 45 - 9x \iff x = \frac{45}{25} = \frac95$.

TITLE: Nine couples re-paired at random
QUESTION:
Nine married couples (18 people) are split into 9 pairs uniformly at random, among all possible ways of splitting 18 people into pairs (same-sex pairs are allowed). What is the expected number of married couples that end up paired together?
HINT1:
Don't try to find the distribution of the number of reunited couples. Write it as a sum of indicators, one per couple.
HINT2:
For one particular couple, look at the husband: his partner is equally likely to be any of the other 17 people.
ANSWER:
$9/17 \approx 0.53$
REFRESH:
**Linearity of expectation.** $\mathbb{E}[\sum_i I_i] = \sum_i \mathbb{E}[I_i] = \sum_i P(A_i)$ for indicators $I_i = \mathbf 1_{A_i}$, whether or not the events are independent.

**Number of perfect matchings.** $2n$ people can be split into pairs in $(2n-1)!! = (2n-1)(2n-3)\cdots 3\cdot 1$ ways.
EXPLANATION:
Each couple contributes 1 if reunited and 0 otherwise, so the expected number is just the sum of the nine reunion probabilities. By symmetry, a given person's partner is uniform over the 17 others, and exactly one of them is the spouse, so each couple is reunited with probability $1/17$. Nine couples give $9/17$. The events are dependent (if eight couples are reunited, the ninth is too), but linearity of expectation does not care.
SOLUTION: Indicators
Let $I_k = 1$ if couple $k$ is paired together, $k = 1,\dots,9$, and $N = \sum_k I_k$. In a uniformly random pairing, the partner of the husband of couple $k$ is uniform over the other 17 people, so $P(I_k = 1) = 1/17$. By linearity,
$$\mathbb{E}N = \sum_{k=1}^{9} P(I_k=1) = 9\cdot\frac1{17} = \frac{9}{17}.$$
SOLUTION: Counting matchings
There are $17!!$ pairings of 18 people. Those in which couple $k$ is together: fix that pair and pair the remaining 16 people in $15!!$ ways. So $P(I_k=1) = \frac{15!!}{17!!} = \frac{1}{17}$, and again $\mathbb{E}N = \frac{9}{17}$.
FOLLOWUP: General $n$
With $n$ couples, what is the expected number reunited, and what happens as $n\to\infty$?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
Each couple is together with probability $\frac{1}{2n-1}$, so $\mathbb{E}N = \frac{n}{2n-1} \to \frac12$. (In fact $N$ converges in distribution to a Poisson$(1/2)$ variable.)

TITLE: How long until the bottom card reaches the top?
QUESTION:
A standard 52-card deck is shuffled by repeating the following move: take the top card and reinsert it at a uniformly random one of the 52 possible positions in the deck (it may go back on top, or to the very bottom, or anywhere in between). A particular card $B$ starts at the bottom of the deck. What is the expected number of moves until $B$ is at the top of the deck for the first time?
HINT1:
Track only the position of $B$, counted from the top ($1$ = top, $52$ = bottom). $B$ is never the card being moved until it reaches the top. How can its position change in one move?
HINT2:
When the top card is reinserted, $B$ moves up by one exactly when the card lands below $B$; otherwise $B$ stays put. Work out that probability as a function of $B$'s position, and add up the expected waiting times at each level.
ANSWER:
$52\,H_{51} = 52\left(1 + \tfrac12 + \cdots + \tfrac1{51}\right) \approx 235$ moves (234.98).
REFRESH:
**Geometric waiting time.** If each trial succeeds independently with probability $q$, the expected number of trials until the first success is $1/q$.

**Harmonic numbers.** $H_n = \sum_{j=1}^n \frac1j \approx \ln n + 0.5772 + \frac{1}{2n}$.
EXPLANATION:
Card $B$ can only ever move up, by one place, and only when the card taken off the top is slipped in underneath it. When $B$ is near the bottom this is rare: at the very bottom there is only one slot below it out of 52, so you wait 52 moves on average for the first step up. As more cards pile up below $B$, the chance increases: with $j$ slots below $B$, the chance is $j/52$ and the wait is $52/j$. Adding the waits for $j = 1, 2, \dots, 51$ gives $52 H_{51}$. This is the same structure as the coupon collector problem, and it is the key step in showing that about $n\ln n$ top-to-random shuffles mix a deck.
SOLUTION: Sum of geometric waiting times
Let $i$ be $B$'s position from the top. A move removes the top card (not $B$, as long as $i\ge 2$): $B$ is now at position $i-1$ in a 51-card deck, with $51-(i-1) = 52-i$ cards below it. The removed card is inserted into one of 52 slots (above the first card, between two consecutive cards, or below the last). $B$ ends at position $i-1$ iff the card goes into one of the slots below $B$, of which there are $52 - i + 1 = 53 - i$; otherwise it ends at position $i$. So
$$P(i \to i-1) = \frac{53-i}{52}, \qquad P(i\to i) = \frac{i-1}{52}.$$
The time spent at level $i$ is geometric with mean $\frac{52}{53-i}$, and the levels are visited in order $52, 51, \dots, 2$. Hence
$$\mathbb{E}T = \sum_{i=2}^{52}\frac{52}{53-i} = 52\sum_{j=1}^{51}\frac1j = 52\,H_{51}.$$
Numerically $H_{51} \approx 4.51881$, so $\mathbb{E}T \approx 234.98 \approx 235$.

Note: the source quotes 234, which is this value truncated; the exact expectation is $52H_{51}\approx 234.98$.
SOLUTION: Recurrence on the expected time to go up
Let $e_i$ be the expected number of moves to reach the top from position $i$, with $e_1 = 0$. First-step analysis:
$$e_i = 1 + \frac{53-i}{52}e_{i-1} + \frac{i-1}{52}e_i \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{53-i}{52}e_i = 1 + \frac{53-i}{52}e_{i-1} \;\Longrightarrow\; e_i = e_{i-1} + \frac{52}{53-i}.$$
Telescoping from $e_1 = 0$: $e_{52} = \sum_{i=2}^{52}\frac{52}{53-i} = 52 H_{51}\approx 234.98$. A quick simulation (20 000 runs) gives about 235, confirming it.
FOLLOWUP: One more move
Once $B$ is on top, one further move puts it at a uniformly random position. Why does that make the whole deck exactly uniformly shuffled?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
By induction, the cards that have been inserted below $B$ are in a uniformly random order at all times: each new card inserted below $B$ goes to a uniformly random one of the slots among them. When $B$ reaches the top, all 51 other cards are below it in uniform random order; reinserting $B$ uniformly then gives a uniform permutation of all 52. So $T+1$ is a strong uniform time, with mean $52H_{51}+1 \approx 236$.
===END===

**Newton's method.** $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$; near a simple root the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step.

Try $x = 0.5$: $1 - 0.0208 = 0.979$, too small; the left side has derivative about $2 - x^2/2 \approx 1.87$, so add $0.021/1.87 \approx 0.011$: $x \approx 0.511$. Check $2(0.511) - 0.511^3/6 = 1.022 - 0.0222 = 0.9998$. So $x \approx 0.51$.

One hundred cars are given independent speeds, uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$, and lined up in a random order in a single lane. Call a *block* any place where a faster car sits directly behind a slower car. What is the expected number of blocks?
HINT1:
Count blocks one adjacent pair of cars at a time.
HINT2:
There are 99 adjacent pairs. For each one, what is the probability that the rear car is the faster of the two?
ANSWER:
$99/2 = 49.5$
REFRESH:
**Linearity of expectation.** The expected number of events that occur is the sum of their probabilities, with no independence needed.

**Ties have probability zero.** Two independent continuous random variables are equal with probability 0.
EXPLANATION:
Each of the 99 adjacent pairs is a block with probability exactly $1/2$: the two speeds are i.i.d. and continuous, so by symmetry either car is equally likely to be the faster one. Adding the indicators gives $99/2$. The distribution of the speeds (uniform or anything continuous) does not matter.
SOLUTION: Indicators over adjacent pairs
Number the positions $1,\dots,100$ from front to back and let $I_k = 1$ if the car in position $k+1$ is faster than the car in position $k$, for $k = 1,\dots,99$. The two speeds are i.i.d. continuous, so $P(I_k = 1) = \frac12$ by symmetry. Hence
$$\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{99} I_k\Big] = 99\cdot\frac12 = \frac{99}{2} = 49.5.$$
FOLLOWUP: Platoons on an open road
Now the cars drive for a long time on a one-lane road with no overtaking, so faster cars get stuck and the traffic forms platoons, each led by a car that is slower than every car ahead of it. What is the expected number of platoons?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
Number the cars from the front. Car $k$ leads a platoon iff it is the slowest of the first $k$ cars; by symmetry this has probability $1/k$. So the expected number of platoons is $H_{100} = \sum_{k=1}^{100}\frac1k \approx 5.19$.

TITLE: Does the random tetrahedron contain the centre?
QUESTION:
Four points are chosen independently and uniformly at random on the surface of a unit sphere. What is the probability that the tetrahedron with these four vertices contains the centre of the sphere?
HINT1:
Solve the 2D version first: three random points on a circle, when does the triangle contain the centre? Look for an argument that pairs each point with its antipode.
HINT2:
Choose the points in two steps: for each of $P_1, P_2, P_3$ first pick a random diameter, then pick which end of it to use. Given the diameters and $P_4$, how many of the $2^3$ choices of ends put the centre inside the tetrahedron?
ANSWER:
$1/8$
REFRESH:
**Uniform point via symmetric choice.** A uniform point on the sphere has the same law as: pick a uniform diameter, then pick one of its two ends with probability $1/2$ each.

**Coordinates in a basis.** For three linearly independent vectors $u_1,u_2,u_3$, every vector $v$ can be written uniquely as $v = \sum c_i u_i$; it lies in the cone they span iff all $c_i \ge 0$.
EXPLANATION:
The centre $O$ lies inside the tetrahedron iff $O$ is a convex combination of the four vertices with positive weights, which is the same as saying $-P_4$ is a positive combination of $P_1, P_2, P_3$. Now flip ends: replacing $P_i$ by $-P_i$ flips the sign of the coefficient $c_i$ in $-P_4 = \sum c_i P_i$. Among the 8 ways to choose the ends $\pm P_1, \pm P_2, \pm P_3$, exactly one makes all three coefficients positive. All 8 choices are equally likely, so the probability is $1/8$. In the plane, the same argument with two diameters gives $1/4$ for a triangle.
SOLUTION: Antipodal sign-flip argument
With probability 1 any three of the points are linearly independent and no coefficient below vanishes. The centre $O$ is inside the tetrahedron $P_1P_2P_3P_4$ iff there are weights $\lambda_i > 0$ with $\sum_{i=1}^4 \lambda_iP_i = 0$, i.e. iff
$$-P_4 = c_1P_1 + c_2P_2 + c_3P_3 \quad\text{with } c_1,c_2,c_3 > 0 \qquad (c_i = \lambda_i/\lambda_4).$$
Generate $P_i = \varepsilon_i D_i$ ($i=1,2,3$), where $D_i$ is uniform on the sphere and $\varepsilon_i = \pm1$ an independent fair sign; each $P_i$ is still uniform and all four points are independent. Write $-P_4 = \sum_i d_i D_i$ uniquely; then $c_i = \varepsilon_i d_i$. Conditional on $D_1,D_2,D_3,P_4$, the sign pattern of $(c_1,c_2,c_3)$ runs over all 8 patterns as $(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\varepsilon_3)$ runs over its 8 equally likely values, and exactly one of them is $(+,+,+)$. Therefore
$$P(O \in \text{tetrahedron}) = \frac18.$$
SOLUTION: Wendel's theorem
Wendel's theorem: for $n$ i.i.d. points with a distribution symmetric about the origin in $\mathbb{R}^d$ (in general position a.s.), the probability that they all lie in some half-space through $O$ is $2^{-(n-1)}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\binom{n-1}{k}$. The tetrahedron contains $O$ exactly when the four points are *not* all in one hemisphere. With $n=4$, $d=3$:
$$1 - \frac{1}{8}\left(\binom30+\binom31+\binom32\right) = 1 - \frac{1+3+3}{8} = \frac18.$$
FOLLOWUP: The planar version
Three points are chosen uniformly on a circle. What is the probability that the triangle contains the centre?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
Same argument in 2D: $-P_3 = c_1P_1 + c_2P_2$, and the 4 equally likely choices of ends for $P_1, P_2$ realise each sign pattern of $(c_1,c_2)$ once, so the answer is $1/4$. (Wendel: $1 - \frac14(1+2) = \frac14$.)

TITLE: Gaussian trick for a uniform point on the sphere
QUESTION:

HINT1:
Look at the joint density of $(X_1,X_2,X_3)$: what does it depend on? What happens to it under a rotation?
HINT2:
For the variance: the three squared coordinates always add up to 1, and they all have the same distribution.
ANSWER:
It is uniform because the Gaussian vector is rotation-invariant; each coordinate has mean $0$ and variance $1/3$.
REFRESH:
**Rotation invariance.** The density of $X = (X_1,X_2,X_3)$ is $(2\pi)^{-3/2}e^{-\|x\|^2/2}$; if $R$ is orthogonal then $RX$ has the same density since $\|Rx\| = \|x\|$ and $|\det R| = 1$.

**Uniqueness of the uniform law.** The uniform (surface-area) distribution is the only probability distribution on the sphere invariant under all rotations.
EXPLANATION:
The standard Gaussian in 3D looks the same from every direction: its density only depends on the distance to the origin. So the direction $X/r$ has a distribution that no rotation can change, and the only such distribution on the sphere is the uniform one.

The variance needs no integration. Each coordinate $U_i = X_i/r$ has mean 0 (flip the sign of $X_i$). And $U_1^2 + U_2^2 + U_3^2 = 1$ always, while the three $U_i^2$ have the same distribution, so each has mean $1/3$. Hence $\mathrm{Var}(U_i) = 1/3$. In $d$ dimensions it would be $1/d$.
SOLUTION: Symmetry
**Uniformity.** Let $U = X/\|X\|$ (defined with probability 1). For any rotation $R$, $RU = RX/\|RX\|$ and $RX \overset{d}{=} X$, so $RU \overset{d}{=} U$. The law of $U$ is a rotation-invariant probability on the sphere, hence the uniform law.

**Mean.** $(-X_1, X_2, X_3) \overset{d}{=} (X_1,X_2,X_3)$ and $r$ is unchanged, so $U_1 \overset{d}{=} -U_1$ and $\mathbb{E}U_1 = 0$ (it is bounded, so the mean exists).

**Second moment.** $U_1^2 + U_2^2 + U_3^2 = 1$ and, by permuting coordinates, $U_1^2, U_2^2, U_3^2$ are identically distributed. Taking expectations, $3\,\mathbb{E}[U_1^2] = 1$. Therefore
$$\mathrm{Var}(U_i) = \mathbb{E}[U_i^2] - 0 = \frac13.$$
SOLUTION: Archimedes' hat-box theorem
On the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the $z$-coordinate of a uniform point is uniform on $[-1,1]$ (the area of a band between two horizontal planes is proportional to its height). So $U_3 \sim U[-1,1]$ and $\mathrm{Var}(U_3) = \frac{(1-(-1))^2}{12} = \frac{4}{12} = \frac13$.

Let $D$ be the first roll and $T$ the total. If $D\ge3$, $T = D$; if $D\le2$, $T = D + T'$ where $T'$ is a fresh copy of the game. So
$$E = \mathbb{E}[D] + P(D\le2)\,E = \frac72 + \frac13E \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac23 E = \frac72 \;\Longrightarrow\; E = \frac{21}{4}.$$

$$\mathbb{E}T = \mathbb{E}N\cdot\mathbb{E}D_1 = \frac32\cdot\frac72 = \frac{21}{4}.$$

The number of 1s and 2s before the final roll has mean $\frac{1/3}{2/3} = \frac12$, and each is worth on average $\frac{1+2}{2} = 1.5$. The final roll is uniform on $\{3,4,5,6\}$, average $4.5$. Total: $\frac12\cdot 1.5 + 4.5 = 0.75 + 4.5 = 5.25$.

- residue 2: $42$,
- residue 3: $63$,
- residue 4: $84$.

TITLE: Handing out pieces of a stick
QUESTION:
You have a stick of length 1 and a line of $n$ people. At the first person you break the stick at a uniformly random point and give that person one of the two pieces, chosen at random (each with probability $1/2$); you keep the other piece. You walk to the next person and repeat with the piece you kept, and so on down the line. Pick a person at random who is not last in line. What is the probability that the piece they received is longer than the piece received by the person right behind them? Do it by hand.
HINT1:
Everything scales: if you hold a piece of length $L$, the piece you hand over is $L$ times a random fraction. What is the distribution of that fraction, given the break is uniform and the piece is chosen by a coin flip?
HINT2:
The given fraction $V$ is uniform on $[0,1]$ and you keep $1 - V$. So a person gets $LV$ and the next one gets $L(1-V)W$ with $W$ uniform and independent. Compute $P\big(V > (1-V)W\big)$ by conditioning on $V$.
ANSWER:
$\ln 2 \approx 0.693$
REFRESH:
**Random piece of a uniform break.** If $U\sim U[0,1]$ and you take $U$ or $1-U$ with probability $1/2$ each (independently), the result is again $U[0,1]$.

**Integral.** $\int_0^{1/2}\frac{v}{1-v}\,dv = \big[-v - \ln(1-v)\big]_0^{1/2} = -\frac12 + \ln 2$.
EXPLANATION:
The length you are holding when you reach a person is a common factor for that person and the next one, so it cancels: only two independent uniforms matter. The current person gets a uniform fraction $V$; the next person gets a uniform fraction $W$ of what is left, $1-V$. If $V \ge 1/2$ the current person automatically wins, because what is left is shorter than what they got. If $V < 1/2$ they win with probability $\frac{V}{1-V}$. Averaging gives $\frac12 + (\ln 2 - \frac12) = \ln 2$, the same for every position, so it does not matter which person you pick.
SOLUTION: Condition on the fraction given away
Suppose you arrive at person $k$ (not last) holding a piece of length $L > 0$. The break point is $U \sim U[0,1]$ (as a fraction of $L$) and you hand over $U$ or $1-U$ with probability $1/2$; the fraction handed over, $V$, is uniform on $[0,1]$, and you keep $L(1-V)$. Person $k+1$ then receives $L(1-V)W$, where $W \sim U[0,1]$ is independent of $V$. So
$$P(\text{person } k \text{ beats } k+1) = P\big(V > (1-V)W\big) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\min\Big(1, \tfrac{V}{1-V}\Big)\Big].$$
For $v \ge \frac12$, $\frac{v}{1-v} \ge 1$; for $v<\frac12$ it is below 1. Hence
$$P = \int_{1/2}^1 1\,dv + \int_0^{1/2}\frac{v}{1-v}\,dv = \frac12 + \Big[-v - \ln(1-v)\Big]_0^{1/2} = \frac12 - \frac12 + \ln 2 = \ln 2.$$
This does not depend on $k$ or $L$, so picking the person at random gives the same value, $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$.
SOLUTION: Integrate over the unit square
In the $(v,w)$ unit square, the event is $w < \frac{v}{1-v}$. Its complement $\{w \ge \frac{v}{1-v}\}$ is the region above the curve $w = \frac{v}{1-v}$, i.e. $v \le \frac{w}{1+w}$. Its area is
$$\int_0^1 \frac{w}{1+w}\,dw = \int_0^1\Big(1 - \frac{1}{1+w}\Big)dw = 1 - \ln 2,$$
so the event has probability $1 - (1-\ln 2) = \ln 2$.

Any particular pattern with three sixes and two non-sixes (e.g. 6,6,6,x,x) has probability $(1/6)^3(5/6)^2$, and there are $\binom53 = 10$ positions for the sixes.
SOLUTION: Binomial formula
$$P(X = 3) = \binom53\Big(\frac16\Big)^3\Big(\frac56\Big)^2 = 10\cdot\frac{1}{216}\cdot\frac{25}{36} = \frac{250}{7776} = \frac{125}{3888}\approx 0.0322.$$
SOLUTION: Direct count of outcomes
Of the $6^5 = 7776$ equally likely outcomes, those with exactly three sixes: choose the positions of the sixes ($\binom53 = 10$ ways) and a non-six value for each of the other two rolls ($5^2 = 25$ ways): $250$ outcomes. $\frac{250}{7776} = \frac{125}{3888}$.
===END===

IDS: Q-497
FIRMS: Citadel, Jane Street
SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Counting & combinatorics
DIFFICULTY: easy
TITLE: Rolling a total of 10 with three dice
QUESTION:
Three fair six-sided dice are rolled. What is the probability that their total is 10?
HINT1:
Count the ordered triples $(a,b,c)$ with $1\le a,b,c\le 6$ and $a+b+c = 10$, out of $216$.
HINT2:
Shift to $a' = a-1$ etc., so $a'+b'+c' = 7$ with each at most 5. Use stars and bars, then remove the solutions where some variable exceeds 5. Or use the symmetry of the distribution around 10.5.
ANSWER:
$27/216 = 1/8$
REFRESH:
**Stars and bars.** Non-negative solutions of $x_1+x_2+x_3 = m$: $\binom{m+2}{2}$.

**Symmetry of dice sums.** Replacing each die $a$ by $7-a$ maps a total $t$ to $21-t$, so $P(\text{sum}=t) = P(\text{sum}=21-t)$.
EXPLANATION:
10 and 11 are the two most likely totals of three dice, being the two middle values of the symmetric range 3–18, and each occurs in 27 of the 216 outcomes, i.e. exactly $1/8$ of the time.
SOLUTION: Stars and bars with a cap
Set $a' = a - 1$, etc.: $a'+b'+c' = 7$ with $0\le a',b',c'\le5$. Without the cap there are $\binom{9}{2} = 36$ solutions. If, say, $a'\ge 6$, write $a' = 6 + a''$: then $a''+b'+c' = 1$, which has $\binom32 = 3$ solutions; two variables cannot both be $\ge6$. So the capped count is $36 - 3\cdot3 = 27$ and
$$P = \frac{27}{216} = \frac18.$$
SOLUTION: List by unordered triples
The multisets of three faces summing to 10, with the number of orderings: $\{6,3,1\}$: 6, $\{6,2,2\}$: 3, $\{5,4,1\}$: 6, $\{5,3,2\}$: 6, $\{4,4,2\}$: 3, $\{4,3,3\}$: 3. Total $6+3+6+6+3+3 = 27$, so $P = \frac{27}{216} = \frac18$.
===END===
