TITLE: Endless square roots of 2
QUESTION:
Find the value of the infinitely nested radical
$$\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2+\cdots}}}.$$
HINT1:
The expression contains a copy of itself.
HINT2:
Call the value $x$. What is the expression under the outermost square root? Turn that into an equation for $x$, and remember that $x$ must be positive.
ANSWER:
$2$
REFRESH:
**Monotone convergence**: an increasing sequence of reals that is bounded above converges to a finite limit.
EXPLANATION:
Under the first root sits exactly the same infinite expression again, so the value $x$ satisfies $x=\sqrt{2+x}$. Squaring gives $x^2-x-2=0$, i.e. $(x-2)(x+1)=0$, and since a square root is positive, $x=2$. To be rigorous, the "infinite expression" means the limit of the finite truncations $a_1=\sqrt2$, $a_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+a_n}$, and we should check this limit exists before solving for it.
SOLUTION: Self-similarity plus a convergence check
Define $a_1=\sqrt2$ and $a_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+a_n}$; the nested radical is $\lim a_n$.

**Bounded by 2.** $a_1=\sqrt2<2$, and if $a_n<2$ then $a_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+a_n}<\sqrt4=2$.

**Increasing.** $a_{n+1}^2-a_n^2=2+a_n-a_n^2=(2-a_n)(1+a_n)>0$ since $0<a_n<2$, so $a_{n+1}>a_n$.

By monotone convergence $a_n\to x$ with $0<x\le2$. Passing to the limit in $a_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+a_n}$ (the square root is continuous):
$$x=\sqrt{2+x}\ \Longrightarrow\ x^2-x-2=0\ \Longrightarrow\ (x-2)(x+1)=0.$$
The root $-1$ is excluded because $x>0$. Hence the value is $2$.
SOLUTION: Trigonometric closed form
Use $2\cos\theta=\sqrt{2+2\cos2\theta}$ (from $\cos2\theta=2\cos^2\theta-1$, valid for $\theta\in[0,\pi/2]$). Then $a_1=\sqrt2=2\cos\frac{\pi}{4}$, and if $a_n=2\cos\frac{\pi}{2^{n+1}}$,
$$a_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+2\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2^{n+1}}}=2\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2^{n+2}}.$$
So $a_n=2\cos\frac{\pi}{2^{n+1}}\to2\cos0=2$.
===END===

TITLE: Circle growing: when do area and circumference grow equally fast?
QUESTION:
The radius of a circle starts at $0$ and increases at a constant positive rate. At what radius is the rate of change of the circumference equal to the rate of change of the area?
HINT1:
Write circumference and area as functions of $r$, and $r$ as a function of time.
HINT2:
Differentiate both with respect to time using the chain rule; the common factor $\frac{dr}{dt}$ is nonzero.
ANSWER:
$r=1$
REFRESH:
**Chain rule**: if $f$ depends on $r$ and $r$ on $t$, then $\frac{df}{dt}=f'(r)\,\frac{dr}{dt}$.
EXPLANATION:
Both quantities change only because $r$ changes, so each rate is (derivative with respect to $r$) $\times\,\frac{dr}{dt}$. The circumference $2\pi r$ always grows at $2\pi$ per unit of radius, while the area $\pi r^2$ grows at $2\pi r$ per unit of radius, which is the length of the boundary being pushed outward. The two coincide exactly when $r=1$. Before that the circumference grows faster, after that the area does.
SOLUTION: Differentiate in time
Let $v=\frac{dr}{dt}>0$ be the constant rate. With $C=2\pi r$ and $A=\pi r^2$:
$$\frac{dC}{dt}=2\pi v,\qquad \frac{dA}{dt}=2\pi r\,v.$$
Setting them equal: $2\pi v=2\pi r v$, and dividing by $2\pi v\neq0$ gives $r=1$ (in whatever unit of length is used).

SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Markov chains & random walks
DIFFICULTY: hard
TITLE: Toss until heads equal tails
QUESTION:
Jacob tosses a fair coin repeatedly and stops as soon as the number of heads equals the number of tails (for example, $HHTHTT$ stops after $6$ tosses). Let $T$ be the number of tosses. What is $1/\mathbb{E}[T]$?
HINT1:
Track the difference (heads minus tails). What kind of process is it, and what does stopping mean for it?
HINT2:
After the first toss the difference is $\pm1$. Let $h$ be the expected time to get from $1$ back to $0$. Getting from $2$ to $0$ means going from $2$ to $1$ and then from $1$ to $0$. Write a first-step equation for $h$.
ANSWER:
$0$, because $\mathbb{E}[T]=\infty$ (Jacob stops with probability 1, but the expected number of tosses is infinite).
REFRESH:
**Simple symmetric random walk**: $S_n=\sum_{i\le n}\xi_i$ with i.i.d. $\xi_i=\pm1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each. It is recurrent: it returns to $0$ with probability 1.
**First-step analysis**: condition on the first move and use the Markov property to write equations for expected hitting times.
EXPLANATION:
Heads minus tails is a simple symmetric random walk started at $0$, and $T$ is its first return time to $0$. The walk does come back with probability 1, but the return time has a very heavy tail: $\mathbb{P}(T>2n)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}$, which decays so slowly that $\sum\mathbb{P}(T>k)$ diverges. The slick way to see it: if $h$ = expected time from $1$ to $0$ were finite, then from $1$ you either reach $0$ in one step or go to $2$, and from $2$ you need on average $h$ to come down to $1$ and another $h$ to reach $0$. That gives $h=1+\tfrac12\cdot2h=1+h$, which is impossible for a finite number. So $\mathbb{E}[T]=\infty$ and its inverse is $0$.
SOLUTION: First-step analysis
Let $S_n$ = (heads $-$ tails) after $n$ tosses. $T=\min\{n\ge1:S_n=0\}$. After the first toss $|S_1|=1$; by symmetry $\mathbb{E}[T]=1+h$ where $h$ is the expected hitting time of $0$ from $1$.

Suppose $h<\infty$. To go from $2$ to $0$ the walk must first reach $1$ (expected time $h$, by translation invariance), then reach $0$ from $1$ (expected time $h$, Markov property). So the expected time from $2$ is $2h$. First-step from $1$:
$$h=1+\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12\cdot2h=1+h,$$
a contradiction. Hence $h=\infty$, $\mathbb{E}[T]=\infty$ and $1/\mathbb{E}[T]=0$.

(And $T<\infty$ almost surely: the probability $p$ of ever reaching $0$ from $1$ satisfies $p=\tfrac12+\tfrac12p^2$, i.e. $(p-1)^2=0$, so $p=1$.)
SOLUTION: Exact distribution of the return time
$T=2n$ requires $n$ heads and $n$ tails with the partial differences never $0$ before. Such a path stays strictly positive (or strictly negative) on times $1,\dots,2n-1$; a positive one is an up-step, then a path from $1$ to $1$ in $2n-2$ steps that never touches $0$, then a down-step. By the reflection principle the number of those middle paths is $\binom{2n-2}{n-1}-\binom{2n-2}{n}=\frac{1}{n}\binom{2n-2}{n-1}$ (a Catalan number). Doubling for the negative side,
$$\mathbb{P}(T=2n)=\frac{2}{n}\binom{2n-2}{n-1}2^{-2n}=\frac{1}{2n-1}\binom{2n}{n}2^{-2n}.$$
With Stirling, $\binom{2n}{n}2^{-2n}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi n}}$, so $\mathbb{P}(T=2n)\sim\frac{1}{2\sqrt\pi}\,n^{-3/2}$ and
$$\mathbb{E}[T]=\sum_{n\ge1}2n\,\mathbb{P}(T=2n)\sim\sum_n\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\,n^{-1/2}=\infty.$$
(Check the formula on small cases: $\mathbb{P}(T=2)=\frac11\cdot2\cdot\frac14=\frac12$ and $\mathbb{P}(T=4)=\frac13\cdot6\cdot\frac1{16}=\frac18$, matching $HT,TH$ and $HHTT,TTHH$.) So $1/\mathbb{E}[T]=0$.
===END===

TITLE: Locking in a profit on a football match
QUESTION:
A bookmaker offers the following odds on a football match:
- Team Alpha wins: $2:1$ (a winning \$1 stake returns \$3, i.e. a net gain of \$2),
- Team Beta wins: $3:1$,
- Draw: $10:1$.

You place whole-dollar stakes $A$ on Alpha, $B$ on Beta and $C$ on the draw, chosen as small as possible so that your profit is exactly the same whatever the result. What is that profit? (If no such bet is worth making, answer $0$.)

Turn each set of odds into the probability the bookmaker is implicitly charging you. Do they add up to 1?
HINT2:
If the total payout must be the same in all three outcomes, then $3A=4B=11C$. Find the smallest whole numbers that do this, and compare the payout with the total stake.
ANSWER:
\$43 (stakes $A=44$, $B=33$, $C=12$: you pay \$89 and receive \$132 whatever happens).
REFRESH:
**Odds and implied probability**: odds of $k:1$ return $k+1$ per unit staked, so the break-even probability is $\frac{1}{k+1}$. A fair (or bookmaker-favourable) book has implied probabilities summing to at least $1$; a sum below $1$ means a guaranteed profit (a "Dutch book").
EXPLANATION:
The implied probabilities are $\frac13$, $\frac14$ and $\frac1{11}$, and they sum to $\frac{89}{132}<1$: the bookmaker is effectively selling you a claim that pays \$132 for sure for only \$89. To collect the same amount in every outcome, bet on each result in proportion to its implied probability, i.e. make $3A=4B=11C$. The smallest integer solution uses $\operatorname{lcm}(3,4,11)=132$.
SOLUTION: Equalising the payout
If Alpha wins you receive $3A$, if Beta wins $4B$, if it's a draw $11C$. Your total stake is $A+B+C$ whichever happens, so the profit is the same in every outcome iff
$$3A=4B=11C=:L.$$
Since $3,4,11$ are pairwise coprime, $L$ must be a multiple of $3\cdot4\cdot11=132$. The smallest choice $L=132$ gives
$$A=44,\quad B=33,\quad C=12,\quad A+B+C=89.$$
Profit in every outcome: $132-89=43$ dollars. The bet is clearly worth making (it is riskless), so the answer is \$43.

Equivalently, profit per dollar paid out is $1-\left(\frac13+\frac14+\frac1{11}\right)=1-\frac{89}{132}=\frac{43}{132}$.

TITLE: Roll until a six, given all rolls were even
QUESTION:
You roll a fair six-sided die until you get a $6$. Given that every roll you made was even, what is the expected number of rolls (counting the final $6$)?
HINT1:
The tempting answer is to pretend the die only has faces $2,4,6$. Test that idea carefully: does conditioning on "all even" really leave the rolls uniform on $\{2,4,6\}$?
HINT2:
Look at the first roll that is not a $2$ or a $4$. When does it happen, and what is its value? Is the time independent of the value?
ANSWER:
$\dfrac32$
REFRESH:
**Conditional expectation given an event**: $\mathbb{E}[N\mid A]=\mathbb{E}[N\mathbf 1_A]/\mathbb{P}(A)$.
**Geometric distribution**: if each trial succeeds with probability $p$, the index of the first success has mean $1/p$.
EXPLANATION:
The event "all rolls even until the first 6" says the first roll outside $\{2,4\}$ is a $6$ rather than a $1,3,5$. Let $N$ be the time of the first roll in $\{1,3,5,6\}$: it is geometric with success probability $\frac46$, and the value shown at that time is uniform on $\{1,3,5,6\}$ **independently of $N$**. So conditioning on that value being $6$ does not change $N$ at all: $\mathbb{E}[N\mid\text{it's a }6]=\mathbb{E}[N]=\frac{1}{4/6}=\frac32$.

Conditioning on "all even" is much stronger than it looks: long sequences are very unlikely to stay even, so the condition favours short games. Pretending the die has faces $\{2,4,6\}$ gives $3$, which is wrong.
SOLUTION: Direct computation
The game ends at roll $n$ with all rolls even iff rolls $1,\dots,n-1$ are in $\{2,4\}$ and roll $n$ is $6$:
$$\mathbb{P}(N=n,\ A)=\left(\tfrac13\right)^{n-1}\tfrac16.$$
Summing, $\mathbb{P}(A)=\frac16\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/3}=\frac14$. Then, using $\sum_{n\ge1}n x^{n-1}=\frac1{(1-x)^2}$,
$$\mathbb{E}[N\mid A]=\frac{1}{1/4}\sum_{n\ge1}n\left(\tfrac13\right)^{n-1}\tfrac16=4\cdot\tfrac16\cdot\frac{1}{(2/3)^2}=\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac94=\tfrac32.$$
Note: the source states $3$; the correct value is $\frac32$, because the rolls conditioned on "all even" are not uniform on $\{2,4,6\}$ (a quick simulation also gives $\approx1.50$).
SOLUTION: Independence of the stopping time and the stopping value
Let $N$ be the first roll showing a value in $\{1,3,5,6\}$. Each roll lands there with probability $\frac46$ independently, so $N\sim\text{Geom}(2/3)$. Given $N=n$, the value of roll $n$ is uniform on $\{1,3,5,6\}$ whatever $n$ is, so $N$ and $V=$ (value at time $N$) are independent. The conditioning event is exactly $\{V=6\}$, and on it the game length is $N$. Hence
$$\mathbb{E}[N\mid V=6]=\mathbb{E}[N]=\frac{1}{2/3}=\frac32.$$
===END===

- $e_1+e_2\ge8$: would need $o\le-1$, impossible.

Total favourable: $1+6+6=13$. Hence
$$\mathbb{P}=\frac{13}{108}.$$

The likelihood is $L(N)=\binom{50}{10}\binom{N-50}{30}\big/\binom{N}{40}$. Its ratio
$$\frac{L(N)}{L(N-1)}=\frac{(N-50)(N-40)}{N(N-80)}\ \ge1\iff 2000\ge 10N\iff N\le200.$$
So $L$ increases up to $N=200$ and then decreases ($L(200)=L(199)$, a tie), and the MLE is $\hat N=199$ or $200$, i.e. about $200$ fish.

| 15 | 0.068 | 0.066 |

| $\ge17$ | | $\approx0.033$ in total |
