IDS: Q-501
FIRMS: SIG
SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Expected value
DIFFICULTY: medium
TITLE: Removing matching pairs from 20 cards
QUESTION:
A deck has $20$ cards: the numbers $1$ to $10$, each appearing on exactly two cards. On each turn you draw $2$ cards at random from the cards still in the deck. If the two cards show the same number, you throw them away; otherwise you put both back and reshuffle. The game ends when the deck is empty.

What is the expected number of turns until the game ends?
HINT1:
The deck always consists of complete pairs. Describe the state of the game by a single number and ask how long you spend in each state.
HINT2:
When $n$ pairs are left ($2n$ cards), compute the probability $p_n$ that a random 2-card draw is a matching pair. The number of turns spent with $n$ pairs left is geometric with parameter $p_n$.
ANSWER:
$100$ turns.
REFRESH:
**Geometric distribution**: if each trial succeeds independently with probability $p$, the number of trials up to and including the first success $T$ has $\mathbb{E}[T]=1/p$.

**Linearity of expectation**: $\mathbb{E}[T_1+\dots+T_k]=\mathbb{E}[T_1]+\dots+\mathbb{E}[T_k]$, with no independence needed.
EXPLANATION:
Nothing changes when you draw a non-matching pair: the deck goes back to exactly the same composition. So the game is a staircase: you sit on the step "$n$ pairs left" until you hit a match, then drop to "$n-1$ pairs left". The time on each step is geometric, and the total is the sum of the step times.

With $n$ pairs, whatever the first card is, exactly $1$ of the other $2n-1$ cards matches it, so a match happens with probability $1/(2n-1)$ and the expected time on that step is $2n-1$. Adding the odd numbers $1+3+\dots+19$ gives $10^2=100$.
SOLUTION: Sum of geometric waiting times
Let $T_n$ be the number of turns spent while exactly $n$ pairs remain ($n=10,9,\dots,1$). The total number of turns is $T=T_{10}+T_9+\dots+T_1$.

With $n$ pairs left there are $2n$ cards and $\binom{2n}{2}=n(2n-1)$ possible draws, of which exactly $n$ are matching pairs. So
$$p_n=\frac{n}{n(2n-1)}=\frac{1}{2n-1}.$$
(Equivalently: the first card drawn has exactly one partner among the other $2n-1$ cards.)

A failed draw leaves the deck unchanged, so each turn in this state is an independent trial with success probability $p_n$, and $T_n$ is geometric: $\mathbb{E}[T_n]=2n-1$.

By linearity,
$$\mathbb{E}[T]=\sum_{n=1}^{10}(2n-1)=1+3+5+\dots+19=10^2=100.$$
FOLLOWUP: General deck
Same game with $N$ pairs ($2N$ cards). What is the expected number of turns?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
Exactly the same argument gives $\mathbb{E}[T]=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(2n-1)=N^2$, since the sum of the first $N$ odd numbers is $N^2$.
===END===

TITLE: Correlation of $X+Y$ and $X-Y$
QUESTION:
$X$ and $Y$ are random variables with finite means, both with variance $16$ (they may be dependent, but assume $X-Y$ is not constant). What is $\operatorname{Corr}(X+Y,\;X-Y)$?
HINT1:
Correlation is zero exactly when the covariance is zero. Try to compute the covariance.
HINT2:
Expand $\operatorname{Cov}(X+Y,X-Y)$ using bilinearity; the cross terms $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(Y,X)$ appear with opposite signs.
ANSWER:
$0$
REFRESH:
**Bilinearity of covariance**: $\operatorname{Cov}(aX+bY,cU+dV)=ac\operatorname{Cov}(X,U)+ad\operatorname{Cov}(X,V)+bc\operatorname{Cov}(Y,U)+bd\operatorname{Cov}(Y,V)$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X,X)=\operatorname{Var}(X)$.

**Correlation**: $\operatorname{Corr}(U,V)=\dfrac{\operatorname{Cov}(U,V)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}U\operatorname{Var}V}}$, defined when both variances are positive.
EXPLANATION:
Covariance behaves like a product: $(X+Y)(X-Y)=X^2-Y^2$. In the same way $\operatorname{Cov}(X+Y,X-Y)=\operatorname{Var}X-\operatorname{Var}Y$, and the covariance between $X$ and $Y$ cancels out. Equal variances therefore give zero covariance, hence zero correlation, whatever the dependence between $X$ and $Y$. The value $16$ is irrelevant; only the equality matters.
SOLUTION: Bilinearity
$$\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Cov}(X+Y,X-Y)&=\operatorname{Cov}(X,X)-\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)+\operatorname{Cov}(Y,X)-\operatorname{Cov}(Y,Y)\\&=\operatorname{Var}X-\operatorname{Var}Y=16-16=0.\end{aligned}$$
We need the variances of $X+Y$ and $X-Y$ to be positive for the correlation to exist. $\operatorname{Var}(X-Y)>0$ by assumption, and $\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=32+2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$, which is $0$ only if $Y=-X+c$; in every other case
$$\operatorname{Corr}(X+Y,X-Y)=\frac{0}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)\operatorname{Var}(X-Y)}}=0.$$
FOLLOWUP: Unequal variances
Now $\operatorname{Var}X=9$, $\operatorname{Var}Y=4$ and $X,Y$ are independent. Find $\operatorname{Corr}(X+Y,X-Y)$.
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$\operatorname{Cov}=9-4=5$; $\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X-Y)=13$ by independence. So $\operatorname{Corr}=5/13\approx0.385$.

HINT2:
Runs alternate between E and N. So the number of runs is odd exactly when the first and the last letter are the same. Count words that start and end with the same letter.
ANSWER:
$6006$

**Lattice paths**: the number of words with $a$ letters E and $b$ letters N is $\binom{a+b}{a}$.
EXPLANATION:
Cut the path into maximal straight segments (runs). Consecutive runs have different directions, and each boundary between two runs is one turn, so turns $=$ runs $-\,1$. Because runs alternate E, N, E, N, …, an odd number of runs means the path ends in the same direction it started. Let $r$ be the number of runs; the number of turns is $r-1$. Runs alternate in direction, so the last run has the same direction as the first iff $r$ is odd, i.e. iff the number of turns is even.

**First and last step both E.** The remaining $14$ steps contain $6$ E and $8$ N: $\binom{14}{6}=3003$ paths.

**First and last step both N.** By symmetry, also $\binom{14}{6}=3003$.

Total: $3003+3003=6006$.

Check: all paths number $\binom{16}{8}=12870$, so $6864$ paths have an odd number of turns (those starting and ending differently: $2\binom{14}{7}=2\cdot3432=6864$), and $6006+6864=12870$.

FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$4$ turns means $5$ runs, so the pattern is E N E N E (3 E-runs, 2 N-runs) or N E N E N. Splitting 8 E's into 3 nonempty runs: $\binom{7}{2}=21$ ways; 8 N's into 2 runs: $\binom71=7$ ways. So $21\cdot7=147$ per pattern and $2\cdot147=294$ paths.

TITLE: $X-Y>1/2$ given $X+Y<1$

The conditioning event is the triangle below the anti-diagonal (area $1/2$). Inside it, find the region where $x>y+\tfrac12$: it is a small triangle; find its three vertices.

$\dfrac18$

**Uniform on a region**: if $(X,Y)$ is uniform on the unit square, $\mathbb{P}((X,Y)\in A)=\text{area}(A)$ and $\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=\text{area}(A\cap B)/\text{area}(B)$.

The line $x-y=\tfrac12$ cuts off a small triangle near the corner $(1,0)$, with vertices $(\tfrac12,0)$, $(1,0)$ and the point where the two lines meet, $(\tfrac34,\tfrac14)$. SOLUTION: Areas in the unit square
$\mathbb{P}(X+Y<1)=\tfrac12$ (triangle with vertices $(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)$).

So the region is the triangle with vertices $(\tfrac12,0),(1,0),(\tfrac34,\tfrac14)$: base $\tfrac12$ on the $x$-axis, height $\tfrac14$, area $\tfrac1{16}$.

$$\mathbb{P}\left(X-Y>\tfrac12\mid X+Y<1\right)=\frac{1/16}{1/2}=\frac18.$$
SOLUTION: Integration
For $y\in(0,\tfrac14)$, $x$ ranges over $(y+\tfrac12,\,1-y)$, of length $\tfrac12-2y$:
$$\int_0^{1/4}\left(\tfrac12-2y\right)dy=\tfrac18-\tfrac1{16}=\tfrac1{16},$$
and dividing by $\tfrac12$ gives $\tfrac18$.

TITLE: Waiting for the next bad day
QUESTION:
Each day is either good ($G$) or bad ($B$). If today is $G$, tomorrow is $G$ with probability $\tfrac35$. If today is $B$, tomorrow is $B$ with probability $\tfrac7{10}$. Today is a bad day. What is the expected number of days until the next bad day (tomorrow counts as $1$ day)?
HINT1:
Look only at tomorrow: either it is bad already, or you are in a good day and must wait from there.
HINT2:
Starting from a good day, each following day is bad with probability $\tfrac25$ independently of how long you have waited, so the wait from $G$ to the next $B$ is geometric.
ANSWER:
$\dfrac74$ days.
REFRESH:
**First-step analysis**: for a Markov chain, the expected hitting time from state $i$ satisfies $m_i=1+\sum_j P_{ij}m_j$ (with $m=0$ at the target).

**Kac's formula**: for an irreducible positive-recurrent chain with stationary distribution $\pi$, the expected return time to state $i$ is $1/\pi_i$.
EXPLANATION:
Tomorrow is bad with probability $0.7$ and then the wait is $1$ day. With probability $0.3$ tomorrow is good, and from a good day you escape to bad with probability $0.4$ per day, taking on average $1/0.4=2.5$ more days. So the mean wait is $1+0.3\cdot2.5=1.75$.

Equivalently, this is the mean return time to $B$, which equals $1/\pi_B$: in the long run bad days make up $4/7$ of all days, so they recur every $7/4$ days on average.
SOLUTION: First-step analysis
Let $m_B$ = expected days to the next $B$ from a $B$ day, $m_G$ = expected days to reach $B$ from a $G$ day.

From $G$: $m_G=1+\tfrac35 m_G$, so $\tfrac25m_G=1$ and $m_G=\tfrac52$.

From $B$: $m_B=1+\tfrac{3}{10}m_G+\tfrac{7}{10}\cdot0=1+\tfrac3{10}\cdot\tfrac52=1+\tfrac34=\tfrac74$.
SOLUTION: Stationary distribution and Kac's formula
Transition probabilities: $G\to B$ is $\tfrac25$, $B\to G$ is $\tfrac3{10}$. Balance of flows: $\pi_G\cdot\tfrac25=\pi_B\cdot\tfrac3{10}$, so $\pi_G/\pi_B=\tfrac34$ and $\pi_B=\tfrac{4}{7}$.

The expected return time to $B$ is $1/\pi_B=\tfrac74$.

HINT2:
Use Bayes to get $\mathbb{P}(\text{urn }1\mid\text{drew a }1)$. Then compute the expected value of the next chip for "same urn" (remember one $1$ has been removed) and for "other urn" (a full urn).

Stay with the same urn; expected payout $\dfrac{52}{7}\approx7.43$.

Two effects compete. The $1$ you drew makes urn 1 (more $1$s) more likely, which argues for switching to what is probably the richer urn 2. **Posterior.** $\mathbb{P}(1\mid U_1)=\tfrac4{10}$, $\mathbb{P}(1\mid U_2)=\tfrac3{10}$, prior $\tfrac12$ each:
$$\mathbb{P}(U_1\mid 1)=\frac{0.4}{0.4+0.3}=\frac47,\qquad \mathbb{P}(U_2\mid1)=\frac37.$$

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{stay}]=\tfrac47\cdot7+\tfrac37\cdot8=\tfrac{28+24}{7}=\tfrac{52}{7}\approx7.43.$$

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{switch}]=\tfrac47\cdot7.3+\tfrac37\cdot6.4=\tfrac{29.2+19.2}{7}=\tfrac{48.4}{7}\approx6.91.$$

Only one player can ever beat player 49. When exactly does that player meet 49?
HINT2:
After $10$ rounds exactly $11$ players have entered the game, and the order of entry is a uniformly random permutation. Show that 49 is out after 10 rounds if and only if both 49 and 50 are among these $11$.
ANSWER:
$\dfrac{234}{245}\approx0.955$
REFRESH:
**Hypergeometric counting**: the probability that two specific items both land in a uniformly random subset of size $k$ out of $n$ is $\binom{n-2}{k-2}/\binom nk=\dfrac{k(k-1)}{n(n-1)}$.
EXPLANATION:
The player on the table after any round is the best player seen so far. So once 49 and 50 have both entered, they must have met: whoever came first rose to the top (only the other one can beat either of them) and stayed until the other arrived. If 50 is not among the first 11 entrants, 49 cannot have lost; if 49 is not among them, it has not played and is still in. So 49 is eliminated within 10 rounds exactly when both 49 and 50 are among the first 11 entrants, which has probability $\frac{11\cdot10}{50\cdot49}=\frac{11}{245}$.
SOLUTION: Reduce to "both among the first 11 entrants"
The matches follow a random order of entry $\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\dots,\sigma_{50}$ (round 1 is $\sigma_1$ vs $\sigma_2$, round $k$ is the current winner vs $\sigma_{k+1}$), which is a uniformly random permutation. After round $k$ the survivor is $\max(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_{k+1})$.

- If player $50$ is not among $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_{11}$, nobody who played could beat 49, so 49 is not out.
- If player $49$ is not among $\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_{11}$, it has not played yet, so it is still in.
- If both are among them, say 49 enters first: from then on 49 is the maximum so far and stays on the table until 50 enters and beats it. If 50 enters first, it holds the table and beats 49 when 49 enters. Either way 49 is out.

So
$$\mathbb{P}(49\text{ out})=\mathbb{P}(49,50\in\{\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_{11}\})=\frac{\binom{48}{9}}{\binom{50}{11}}=\frac{11\cdot10}{50\cdot49}=\frac{110}{2450}=\frac{11}{245},$$
and
$$\mathbb{P}(49\text{ still in after 10 rounds})=1-\frac{11}{245}=\frac{234}{245}\approx0.955.$$
FOLLOWUP: Player 48
What is the probability that player $48$ is still in after the first $10$ rounds?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:

- $a=11$: $L=60$, B wins $60/110$.
- $a=12$: $L=66$, B wins $66/110$.
- $a\le8$: $U\ge62$, even worse for A.

REFRESH:

EXPLANATION:
The unconditional fair value is $50.5$, and a naive quote would be something like $48$ at $53$. The interview point is recognising adverse selection. SOLUTION: Expected P&L of a quote

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{P\&L}]=-\frac1{100}\left(\sum_{n<b}(b-n)+\sum_{n>a}(n-a)\right)\le0,$$
with equality iff $b\le1$ and $a\ge100$.

TITLE: Vega of a one-year ATM call
QUESTION:
You are long a 1-year at-the-money call on a stock trading at $100$ dollars. Implied volatility drops by $1$ vol point (say from $20\%$ to $19\%$). Roughly how much do you make or lose per share?
HINT1:
The Greek that measures sensitivity to implied volatility is vega. Is it positive or negative for a long call?
HINT2:
For an at-the-money option, vega per unit of $\sigma$ is $S\sqrt T\,\varphi(d_1)$ and $\varphi(d_1)\approx\varphi(0)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\approx0.4$. Remember that one vol point is $0.01$ in $\sigma$.
ANSWER:
A loss of about $0.40$ dollars per share ($\approx0.4\,S\sqrt T\times0.01$).
REFRESH:
**Black–Scholes vega**: $\mathcal V=\dfrac{\partial C}{\partial\sigma}=S\sqrt T\,\varphi(d_1)$, where $\varphi$ is the standard normal density and $d_1=\dfrac{\ln(S/K)+(r+\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}$. It is the same for calls and puts, always positive, and largest near the money.
EXPLANATION:
A long option is long volatility: lower implied vol means a lower option price. At the money, $d_1$ is close to $0$ (here $d_1=\sigma\sqrt T/2=0.1$), so $\varphi(d_1)\approx0.4$ and vega $\approx0.4\,S\sqrt T=0.4\times100\times1=40$ dollars per unit of $\sigma$, i.e. $0.40$ dollars per vol point. Losing one vol point costs about $40$ cents per share, which is about $5\%$ of the option's value (an ATM 1y 20%-vol call is worth about $8$ dollars, using $C\approx0.4\,S\sigma\sqrt T$).

Useful rule of thumb: ATM vega per vol point $\approx0.4\,S\sqrt T/100$, and ATM call price $\approx0.4\,S\sigma\sqrt T$, so price $\approx$ vega per point $\times$ vol in points.
SOLUTION: Black–Scholes vega at the money
With $S=K=100$, $T=1$, $\sigma=0.2$ and $r\approx0$: $d_1=\frac{\sigma\sqrt T}{2}=0.1$, $\varphi(0.1)=\frac{e^{-0.005}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\approx0.397$.

$$\mathcal V=S\sqrt T\,\varphi(d_1)\approx100\times1\times0.397=39.7\ \text{per unit of }\sigma.$$

A drop of $1$ vol point is $\Delta\sigma=-0.01$:
$$\Delta C\approx\mathcal V\,\Delta\sigma=39.7\times(-0.01)\approx-0.40\ \text{dollars per share}.$$
(Repricing exactly: $C(\sigma=20\%)-C(\sigma=19\%)\approx0.397$.)

Note: the source quotes vega as $\approx S\sqrt T/25$; that is $4$ dollars, which corresponds to a $10$-point move. Per single vol point the figure is $S\sqrt T/250\approx0.40$.
FOLLOWUP: Three-month option
Same stock and vol, but the call expires in $3$ months. What is the P&L for a 1-point drop in vol?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
Vega scales with $\sqrt T$: $\sqrt{0.25}=0.5$, so vega $\approx0.4\times100\times0.5=20$ per unit of $\sigma$, and a 1-point drop loses about $0.20$ dollars per share.

TITLE: ATM call when the stock rises one dollar
QUESTION:
You own an at-the-money call option. The stock rises by $1$ dollar. Roughly what is your P&L per share, and how does it behave if the stock keeps moving (up a further dollar, or down instead)?
HINT1:
Which Greek gives the first-order price change for a small stock move, and what is its value at the money?
HINT2:
Use a second-order Taylor expansion: $\Delta C\approx\Delta\cdot\delta S+\tfrac12\Gamma(\delta S)^2$. Gamma is positive for a long option.
ANSWER:
About $+0.50$ dollars (delta $\approx0.5$), slightly more because of gamma; the P&L is convex: gains accelerate on further rises and losses decelerate on falls.
REFRESH:
**Delta and gamma**: $\Delta=\partial C/\partial S$ ($=N(d_1)$ for a Black–Scholes call, about $0.5$ at the money), $\Gamma=\partial^2C/\partial S^2=\dfrac{\varphi(d_1)}{S\sigma\sqrt T}>0$.

**Taylor expansion**: $C(S+h)\approx C(S)+\Delta h+\tfrac12\Gamma h^2$.
EXPLANATION:
At the money the call behaves like half a share: delta is about $0.5$ (a bit above, since $d_1=\sigma\sqrt T/2>0$). So a $1$-dollar rise earns about $50$ cents. As the stock rises the call moves into the money and delta climbs toward $1$, so each additional dollar earns more than the last; if the stock falls, delta shrinks toward $0$, so each dollar lost costs less than the last. That curvature is gamma: long options have positive gamma, i.e. a convex P&L in the stock price.
SOLUTION: Delta–gamma approximation
Take $S=K=100$, $\sigma=20\%$, $T=1$, $r=0$. Then $d_1=0.1$, $\Delta=N(0.1)\approx0.54$, $\Gamma=\frac{\varphi(0.1)}{100\times0.2\times1}\approx\frac{0.397}{20}\approx0.020$.

- Stock $+1$: $\Delta C\approx0.54\times1+\tfrac12\times0.020\times1^2\approx0.55$.
- Stock $+2$: $\approx0.54\times2+\tfrac12\times0.020\times4\approx1.12$ (more than twice the first dollar).
- Stock $-1$: $\approx-0.54+0.01=-0.53$ (a smaller loss than the gain on $+1$).

Exact Black–Scholes repricing gives $+0.550$, $+1.119$ and $-0.530$. The rough interview answer is "about $+0.50$, and convex because I am long gamma".

TITLE: How a long-gamma hedger makes money
QUESTION:
A market maker is long options (long gamma) and keeps the position delta-hedged with the stock. How does she make money as the stock moves around, and what does she need to happen to be profitable overall?
HINT1:
After the stock rises, is her delta now positive or negative? What trade restores delta neutrality?
HINT2:
Write the one-step P&L of a delta-hedged option as $\tfrac12\Gamma(\delta S)^2+\Theta\,\delta t$. Which term is the gain and which is the cost?
ANSWER:
Re-hedging makes her sell stock after rises and buy after falls (sell high, buy low), earning $\tfrac12\Gamma(\delta S)^2$ per move; she pays theta for it, and nets a profit iff realised volatility exceeds the implied volatility she paid.
REFRESH:
**Delta hedge**: holding $-\Delta$ shares against the option removes the first-order exposure to the stock.

**Theta–gamma relation** (Black–Scholes, $r=0$): $\Theta+\tfrac12\sigma_{\text{imp}}^2S^2\Gamma=0$, i.e. the time decay paid exactly offsets gamma gains when the stock moves with the implied volatility.
EXPLANATION:
Long gamma means delta rises when the stock rises and falls when it falls. So after an up-move she is net long and sells stock to get flat; after a down-move she is net short and buys stock. She is systematically selling high and buying low, and each round trip locks in a small profit, about $\tfrac12\Gamma(\delta S)^2$ per move, whatever the direction.

This is not free: a long option loses value every day (negative theta). In Black–Scholes the daily theta is exactly the gamma gain you would earn if the stock moved with the implied volatility. So the hedged position's P&L is roughly
$$\text{P\&L per step}\approx\tfrac12\Gamma S^2\left(\left(\tfrac{\delta S}{S}\right)^2-\sigma_{\text{imp}}^2\,\delta t\right),$$
positive when the realised squared moves beat what implied vol priced in. Long gamma $=$ betting that realised volatility will exceed implied.
SOLUTION: One step of the hedged book
Hold one call and $-\Delta$ shares. Over a short step $\delta t$ with stock move $\delta S$:
$$\delta(\text{option})\approx\Delta\,\delta S+\tfrac12\Gamma(\delta S)^2+\Theta\,\delta t,\qquad \delta(\text{hedge})=-\Delta\,\delta S.$$
The linear terms cancel:
$$\delta\Pi\approx\tfrac12\Gamma(\delta S)^2+\Theta\,\delta t.$$
With $\Theta=-\tfrac12\sigma_{\text{imp}}^2S^2\Gamma$ (Black–Scholes, $r=0$):
$$\delta\Pi\approx\tfrac12\Gamma S^2\left[\left(\frac{\delta S}{S}\right)^2-\sigma_{\text{imp}}^2\delta t\right].$$
Summed over the life of the hedge, the bracket compares realised variance with implied variance. Mechanically, gamma gains come from re-hedging: after $\delta S>0$ the new delta is $\Delta+\Gamma\delta S$, so she sells $\Gamma\delta S$ shares at the higher price; after $\delta S<0$ she buys at the lower price.

Example: $\Gamma=0.02$, $S=100$, the stock moves $2$ dollars in a day: gamma gain $\tfrac12\times0.02\times4=0.04$ dollars per option. If implied vol is $20\%$, the daily theta is $\tfrac12\times0.04\times100^2\times0.02\times\tfrac1{252}\approx0.016$. A $2$-dollar daily move is about $32\%$ annualised vol, so she wins that day.
===END===

TITLE: What the bid-ask spread pays for
QUESTION:
Why do market makers quote a bid-ask spread instead of a single price, and what are the three classic components of the spread?
HINT1:
Think about what it costs a market maker to stand ready to trade with anyone, at any time.
HINT2:
One cost is operational, one comes from holding a position you did not choose, and one comes from trading with people who may know more than you.
ANSWER:
The spread compensates the market maker for (1) order-processing costs, (2) inventory risk and (3) adverse selection (trading against better-informed counterparties).
REFRESH:
**Round trip**: a market maker who buys at the bid and later sells at the ask earns the full spread; per trade, the expected edge versus fair value is about half the spread.
EXPLANATION:
A market maker provides immediacy: anyone can trade instantly against her quotes. The spread is the price of that service and must cover:
1. **Order-processing costs**: exchange and clearing fees, technology, staff, capital charges. Fixed per trade, so they matter most in small, low-price trades.
2. **Inventory risk**: after a customer trades, the market maker holds a position she did not choose and bears price risk until she can unwind it. More volatile or less liquid assets mean wider spreads.
3. **Adverse selection**: some counterparties trade because they know the price is about to move. Losses to them must be recovered from uninformed flow through a wider spread. This component explains why spreads widen around news and in stocks with more private information.

Without a spread she would lose money on every one of these fronts, so nobody would provide liquidity.
SOLUTION: The three components with an example
Suppose fair value is $100.00$ and the quote is $99.95$ / $100.05$ (spread $10$ cents, half-spread $5$ cents).
- Processing: fees and costs of about $1$ cent per share.
- Inventory: holding $10{,}000$ shares for a few minutes in a $2\%$-daily-vol stock is real risk; the market maker charges for it and skews her quotes to lean against her position (lower both quotes when long, raise them when short).
- Adverse selection: if $20\%$ of the flow is informed and moves the price $15$ cents against her, the expected loss is $0.2\times15=3$ cents per trade, which must come out of the half-spread.

Here $1+1+3=5$ cents per share, roughly the half-spread. (The inventory number is illustrative.)

Glosten–Milgrom and Kyle models formalise the adverse-selection part: the ask is the expected value **conditional on** someone buying, the bid is the expected value conditional on someone selling.

Why do limit orders tend to cluster at round-number prices (such as $50.00$ or $100$)?

Who decides where a limit order goes: a model, or a person?
HINT2:
Think about how people pick targets and stops, how prices are discussed, and what happens when everyone else does the same.
ANSWER:
Mostly behavioural anchoring: people choose round prices as targets, stops and limits; this is reinforced by convenience of negotiation and by self-fulfilling "support/resistance" beliefs.
REFRESH:
**Price clustering**: the empirical fact that transaction and quote prices land on integers, halves and multiples of 5 or 10 far more often than a uniform distribution of the last digits would predict.

Humans think in round numbers. When a trader decides "I'll buy if it drops to 50" or "take profit at 100", the price is anchored on a salient number, not on a precisely computed value. Several reasons stack up:

- **Negotiation convention**: coarser price grids reduce the back-and-forth needed to agree.
- **Stops and targets**: stop-losses and profit targets are commonly set at round levels, so large amounts of conditional orders gather there.
- **Self-fulfilling belief**: because traders expect round levels to act as support or resistance, they place orders there, which makes them act as support or resistance.
- **Options strikes** are at round prices, and hedging flows around big strikes concentrate activity there.

Trading implication: liquidity is deeper at round levels, and a strategy can step just in front of them (buy at $50.01$ rather than $50.00$) to get priority over the cluster, while stops just beyond a round level can trigger sharp moves when broken.
SOLUTION: Answer in an interview
State the behavioural reason (anchoring on salient prices by people with imprecise valuations), then add the reinforcing mechanisms: stops and targets at round levels, convention and ease of negotiation, self-fulfilling support/resistance, round option strikes. Finish with the consequence: queues are longer at round prices, so fills there are slower and it can pay to quote one tick inside; a break through a round level can release a cascade of stop orders.

EXPLANATION:

Both effects point the same way:
- **Update fair value upward**, and move both quotes up. The offer goes up so you stop selling cheap; the bid goes up so that you attract sellers and cover your short.
- **Widen** while you are unsure what is going on, and **cut size** so each trade costs you less.
- **Hedge** the short (buy the underlying or a correlated instrument), and look for news explaining the move.

What you must not do is keep the quote unchanged or lower it.
SOLUTION: Step by step, in a market-making game
Say you quoted $48$ at $52$ on a quantity whose fair value you estimated at $50$, and the interviewer buys $52$ three times in a row.
1. After the first lift, move to about $50$ at $54$: fair value nudged up, inventory short 1.
2. After the second, $52$ at $56$ and maybe widen to $51$ at $57$ with smaller size.
3. Keep moving until the buying stops: the level where the flow becomes two-sided tells you where the market thinks fair value is.

The size of each step depends on how informed you think the counterparty is: against a counterparty who knows the answer, move a lot; against noise traders, move a little and mainly skew for inventory.

TITLE: Why market makers care about speed
QUESTION:
Why do market makers care so much about how fast they can update (or cancel) their quotes?
HINT1:
What happens to your resting quote in the milliseconds after the true price moves?
HINT2:
Think of two effects: the risk of trading at a price that is no longer right, and the benefit of being first in the queue.
ANSWER:
Speed protects against stale quotes being picked off (adverse selection when prices move) and wins queue priority at the best price; together these let a fast market maker quote tighter and larger.
REFRESH:
**Price-time priority**: on most exchanges, orders at the same price are filled in the order they arrived.
EXPLANATION:
A resting quote is a free option given to the market: anyone can trade on it. When news arrives or a related market moves (the future ticks, an ETF moves), your quote is suddenly mispriced, and faster traders will hit it before you can cancel. That loss is pure adverse selection, and the slower you are, the more of it you suffer, so the wider you must quote to compensate.

Speed also matters on the profit side: with price-time priority, the first order at a new price level gets filled first, so being fast to rejoin the best price means capturing more of the uninformed flow that pays the spread.

A fast market maker therefore loses less to picking-off and trades more of the good flow, which lets her quote tighter and bigger, which attracts even more flow. That is why firms invest heavily in latency.
SOLUTION: Interview answer with an example
Suppose you quote $99.99$ / $100.01$ on a stock that also trades as a future. The future jumps by the equivalent of $5$ cents. If your update takes $1$ ms and an arbitrageur's order reaches the exchange in $0.3$ ms, your offer at $100.01$ is lifted while the new fair value is about $100.05$: a $4$-cent loss per share. Repeated across many events, these losses exceed the spread you earn unless you widen, so being slow means quoting wider and losing flow to faster competitors.

Second point: price-time priority. When the price moves to a new level, the first quotes posted there are filled first; a fast market maker sits at the front of the queue, gets the uninformed fills, and can cancel before being run over.

Summary: speed reduces adverse selection (stale quotes picked off), improves queue position, and allows tighter, larger quotes.
