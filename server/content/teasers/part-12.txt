IDS: Q-310, Q-440
FIRMS: Transmarket Group

TITLE: The sliding ladder
QUESTION:
A ladder $50$ ft long leans against a vertical wall. The ground is slippery, so the foot of the ladder slides away from the wall at a constant speed of $4$ ft per minute, and the top slides down the wall.

How fast (in ft per minute) is the top of the ladder moving down the wall at the moment the foot is $30$ ft from the wall?
HINT1:
The length of the ladder never changes. Write down the relation between the distance of the foot from the wall and the height of the top.
HINT2:
With $x(t)$ the foot's distance and $y(t)$ the height, $x^2+y^2=50^2$ for all $t$. Differentiate this identity with respect to time and plug in the values at the given instant.
ANSWER:
The top slides down at $3$ ft per minute.
REFRESH:
**Related rates**: if two quantities $x(t),y(t)$ are linked by an identity $F(x,y)=0$ holding at all times, differentiating with the chain rule gives $F_x\,\dot x+F_y\,\dot y=0$, a linear relation between the two speeds.
EXPLANATION:
The ladder, the wall and the ground form a right triangle whose hypotenuse is fixed at $50$. When the foot is $30$ ft out, Pythagoras gives the height: $\sqrt{50^2-30^2}=40$ ft (the 3-4-5 triangle scaled by 10).

Differentiating $x^2+y^2=2500$ gives $x\dot x+y\dot y=0$, so $\dot y=-\dfrac{x}{y}\dot x$. The ratio $x/y$ is the key: when the ladder is still steep ($x$ small, $y$ large) the top barely moves; as the ladder flattens, $x/y$ blows up and the top falls faster and faster. At our instant $x/y=30/40=3/4$, so the top moves at $3/4$ of the foot's speed.
SOLUTION: Differentiate Pythagoras
Let $x(t)$ be the distance from the wall to the foot and $y(t)$ the height of the top. For all $t$,
$$x(t)^2+y(t)^2=50^2 .$$
Differentiate with respect to $t$:
$$2x\dot x+2y\dot y=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot y=-\frac{x\,\dot x}{y}.$$
At the instant of interest $x=30$, so $y=\sqrt{2500-900}=\sqrt{1600}=40$, and $\dot x=4$. Hence
$$\dot y=-\frac{30\cdot 4}{40}=-3\ \text{ft/min}.$$
The minus sign says $y$ decreases: the top moves **down** at $3$ ft per minute.
SOLUTION: Explicit formula for the height
The foot is at $x(t)=x_0+4t$, so $y(t)=\sqrt{2500-x(t)^2}$. Then
$$\dot y=\frac{-2x\,\dot x}{2\sqrt{2500-x^2}}=\frac{-x\cdot 4}{\sqrt{2500-x^2}}.$$
At $x=30$: $\dot y=\dfrac{-120}{40}=-3$. The top descends at $3$ ft/min.
FOLLOWUP: How fast does the angle close?
Same ladder, same setup: the foot slides away at $4$ ft/min. How fast (in radians per minute) is the angle $\theta$ between the ladder and the ground decreasing when the foot is $30$ ft from the wall?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$\theta$ decreases at $\dfrac{1}{10}$ rad/min. Indeed $\cos\theta=x/50$. Differentiating, $-\sin\theta\,\dot\theta=\dot x/50$, so $\dot\theta=-\dfrac{\dot x}{50\sin\theta}$. At $x=30$ the height is $40$, so $\sin\theta=40/50=0.8$ and $\dot\theta=-\dfrac{4}{50\cdot 0.8}=-\dfrac{4}{40}=-0.1$ rad/min.
===END===

FIRMS: SIG
SECTION: trading
CATEGORY: Markets & risk

TITLE: Stock doubles or halves, plus an even-money bet
QUESTION:
A stock will, by tomorrow, either **double** in value or **lose half** of its value. Besides buying the stock, you can bet with a friend on its direction at even odds ($1:1$: if you win you gain your stake, if you lose you lose your stake).

You invest $A$ dollars in the stock and bet $B$ dollars with your friend that the stock goes **down**, choosing $A$ and $B$ so that your total profit is the same whichever way the stock moves. Take $A$ and $B$ to be the smallest positive whole-dollar amounts that achieve this. What is your (guaranteed) profit? If no positive profit can be locked in, answer $0$.
HINT1:
Write your total profit in the "up" world and in the "down" world as functions of $A$ and $B$.
HINT2:
Up: the stock earns $+A$, the bet loses $-B$. Down: the stock loses $-A/2$, the bet wins $+B$. Set the two totals equal and look for the smallest integers solving it.
ANSWER:
$A=4$, $B=3$, and the profit is \$1 in both scenarios.
REFRESH:
**Risk-neutral probability** (one period, zero rates): if an asset worth $S$ today is worth $uS$ or $dS$ tomorrow, the unique probability $q$ making it fair is $q=\dfrac{1-d}{u-d}$. Any bet whose odds disagree with $q$ can be combined with the asset to lock in a riskless profit.
EXPLANATION:
The stock market "prices" the up move as a $1/3$ event: with $u=2$, $d=1/2$, $q=\frac{1-1/2}{2-1/2}=\frac13$ is the probability that makes $2q+\frac12(1-q)=1$. Your friend, by offering even odds, treats up and down as $50/50$. Two prices for the same event that disagree means an arbitrage: you "buy" the up move cheaply through the stock and "sell" it at a better price to your friend by betting on down.

Algebraically, the hedge ratio is $B=\frac34A$ and the locked-in profit is $A/4$; the smallest integers are $A=4,B=3$, giving \$1.
SOLUTION: Equalise the two scenarios
Profit if the stock doubles: the stock position gains $A$, the bet on "down" loses $B$:
$$\Pi_{\text{up}}=A-B.$$
Profit if the stock halves: the stock position loses $A/2$, the bet wins $B$:
$$\Pi_{\text{down}}=-\frac A2+B.$$
Setting $\Pi_{\text{up}}=\Pi_{\text{down}}$:
$$A-B=B-\frac A2\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{3A}{2}=2B\ \Longrightarrow\ B=\frac{3A}{4}.$$
For $B$ to be a whole number, $A$ must be a multiple of $4$. The smallest choice is $A=4$, $B=3$. Check: up, $4-3=1$; down, $-2+3=1$. The profit is \$1 either way (in general $A/4$).

Note: one version of this question describes both positions as bets with the friend; two opposite even-money bets on the same event always net to $A-B$ and $B-A$, which can only be equal at $0$. The intended reading (stock plus one bet) gives the stated answer \$1.
SOLUTION: Price the bet with the stock
With zero interest, the stock is fair under the probability $q$ solving $2q+\frac12(1-q)=1$, i.e. $\frac32q=\frac12$, $q=\frac13$. Under $q$ a \$1 even-money bet on "down" has value $\frac23\cdot1+\frac13\cdot(-1)=\frac13$, while it costs nothing to enter. So each dollar bet on down is worth $1/3$ of a dollar for free. Hedge its risk with the stock: per dollar bet you need $A=\frac43$ dollars of stock (from $B=\frac34A$), and the locked-in profit is $B/3=A/4$. With $A=4$, $B=3$, the profit is $3\cdot\frac13=1$ dollar.
===END===

SLUG: marbles-in-a-box-equilibrium
IDS: Q-335, Q-481
FIRMS: Citadel, Optiver
SECTION: probability
CATEGORY: Expected value
DIFFICULTY: hard
TITLE: How many marbles to put in the box?
QUESTION:
Two players, $A$ and $B$, each have $100$ marbles. Simultaneously and secretly, $A$ puts $a$ of his marbles into a box and $B$ puts $b$ of hers, with $a,b$ integers between $1$ and $100$.

Then two marbles are drawn from the box **with replacement**. For each drawn marble: if it belongs to $A$, a neutral bank pays $A$ the amount $100-a$; if it belongs to $B$, the bank pays $B$ the amount $100-b$.

Both players play optimally (each maximises their own expected earnings; look for the equilibrium). What are $A$'s expected earnings?
HINT1:
There is a trade-off: more marbles in the box means more chances of being drawn, but a smaller prize each time. Write $A$'s expected earnings as a function of $a$ and $b$.
HINT2:
$A$'s expected earnings are $2\cdot\frac{a}{a+b}(100-a)$. Find $A$'s best response to a given $b$ by setting a derivative to zero, then look for a symmetric equilibrium $a=b$ (and check integers near it).
ANSWER:
Both players put in $33$ marbles, and $A$ expects to earn $67$.
REFRESH:
**Nash equilibrium**: a pair of strategies $(a^*,b^*)$ such that $a^*$ is a best response to $b^*$ and $b^*$ is a best response to $a^*$; no player gains by deviating alone.

**Linearity of expectation**: the expected total from two draws is the sum of the expected payoffs of each draw, even though the draws are with replacement from the same box.
EXPLANATION:
Each draw picks one of $A$'s marbles with probability $\frac{a}{a+b}$ and then pays $100-a$, so by linearity $A$ earns on average $2(100-a)\frac{a}{a+b}$. The game is not zero-sum (the bank pays), so each player simply maximises their own function.

For fixed $b$, maximising $\frac{a(100-a)}{a+b}$ gives the first-order condition $a^2+2ab-100b=0$. At a symmetric equilibrium $a=b$ this reads $3a^2=100a$, so $a=100/3$. The nearest integers are $33$ and $34$; direct checking shows $a=b=33$ is an equilibrium. Each player is drawn half the time, and A earns $2\cdot\frac12\cdot(100-33)=67$.
SOLUTION: Best response and symmetric equilibrium
**Payoff.** Each of the two draws is $A$'s marble with probability $\frac{a}{a+b}$, paying $100-a$. By linearity,
$$E_A(a,b)=2\,(100-a)\,\frac{a}{a+b}.$$
**Best response.** For fixed $b$, maximise $g(a)=\dfrac{a(100-a)}{a+b}$ over $a$ (treat $a$ as continuous first):
$$g'(a)=\frac{(100-2a)(a+b)-a(100-a)}{(a+b)^2}=\frac{-a^2-2ab+100b}{(a+b)^2}.$$
The numerator is decreasing in $a>0$, so $g$ is maximised at the positive root of $a^2+2ab-100b=0$:
$$a^*(b)=-b+\sqrt{b^2+100b}.$$
**Symmetric equilibrium.** Setting $a=b$ in $a^2+2ab-100b=0$: $3a^2=100a$, so $a=\frac{100}{3}\approx 33.3$.

**Integers.** Take $b=33$ and compare $g$ at nearby integers:
$$g(32)=\tfrac{32\cdot 68}{65}\approx 33.477,\quad g(33)=\tfrac{33\cdot 67}{66}=33.5,\quad g(34)=\tfrac{34\cdot 66}{67}\approx 33.493.$$
Since $g$ is unimodal, $a=33$ is $A$'s best response to $b=33$, and by symmetry $b=33$ is $B$'s best response to $a=33$. So $(33,33)$ is an equilibrium. (With $b=34$ the best response is again $33$, so $(34,34)$ is not.)

**Earnings.**
$$E_A(33,33)=2\cdot 67\cdot\frac{33}{66}=67.$$
FOLLOWUP: Zero-sum version: one token, loser pays
Two players $X$ and $Y$ each have $100$ tokens and secretly put $x$ and $y$ tokens ($1$ to $100$) into a pool. **One** token is drawn. If it is $X$'s, $Y$ pays $X$ the amount $100-x$; if it is $Y$'s, $X$ pays $Y$ the amount $100-y$. Both play optimally. How many tokens should $X$ contribute in equilibrium?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$x=50$. $X$'s expected gain is $U(x,y)=\dfrac{x(100-x)-y(100-y)}{x+y}$, and the game is zero-sum and symmetric. Against $y=50$:
$$U(x,50)=\frac{x(100-x)-2500}{x+50}=\frac{-(x-50)^2}{x+50}\le 0,$$
with equality only at $x=50$. So $x=50$ is the best response to $y=50$, and by symmetry $(50,50)$ is a saddle point: each player contributes $50$ and the value of the game is $0$.
===END===

TITLE: 1% a day
QUESTION:
You put $100$ dollars in an investment that pays $1\%$ interest per day, compounded daily. Without a calculator, estimate (to the nearest dollar) how much you have:

(a) after $15$ days;

(b) after $100$ days.
HINT1:
You need $1.01^{15}$ and $1.01^{100}$. For a small exponent, the first few terms of the binomial expansion are enough; for a large one, think logarithms.
HINT2:
$1.01^{n}=e^{n\ln 1.01}$ with $\ln 1.01\approx 0.01-0.00005$. For $n=100$ the exponent is almost exactly $1$.
ANSWER:
(a) about \$116 (exactly $100\cdot1.01^{15}\approx 116.10$); (b) about \$270 (exactly $100\cdot 1.01^{100}\approx 270.48$).
REFRESH:
**Binomial expansion**: $(1+x)^n=1+nx+\binom n2x^2+\binom n3x^3+\dots$; for small $x$ the terms shrink fast.

**Logarithm series**: $\ln(1+x)=x-\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^3}{3}-\dots$ for $|x|<1$.
EXPLANATION:
Compounding means the growth factor is multiplied, not added: after $n$ days you have $100\cdot1.01^n$. Simple interest would give $100(1+0.01n)$, i.e. \$115 and \$200; the difference is "interest on interest".

For $15$ days that difference is small, so expanding $(1+0.01)^{15}$ and keeping three or four terms is enough. For $100$ days it is huge, and the clean trick is $1.01^{100}=e^{100\ln 1.01}\approx e^{0.995}$, which is just below $e\approx 2.718$. This is the same limit $(1+1/n)^n\to e$ that defines continuous compounding.
SOLUTION: Binomial expansion for 15 days, logarithm for 100 days
**(a)**
$$1.01^{15}=1+15(0.01)+\binom{15}{2}(0.01)^2+\binom{15}{3}(0.01)^3+\dots=1+0.15+105\cdot 10^{-4}+455\cdot10^{-6}+\dots$$
$$=1+0.15+0.0105+0.000455+0.0000137+\dots\approx 1.16097.$$
So $100\cdot 1.01^{15}\approx 116.10$, i.e. **\$116**.

**(b)** $\ln 1.01=0.01-\frac{0.0001}{2}+\frac{0.000001}{3}-\dots\approx 0.0099503$, so
$$1.01^{100}=e^{100\ln1.01}=e^{0.99503}=e\cdot e^{-0.00497}\approx 2.71828\times(1-0.00497)\approx 2.7048.$$
So $100\cdot1.01^{100}\approx 270.48$, i.e. **\$270**.
SOLUTION: Logarithm for both
$15\ln 1.01\approx 15\times 0.00995=0.14925$, and $e^{0.14925}\approx 1+0.14925+\frac{0.14925^2}{2}+\frac{0.14925^3}{6}\approx 1+0.14925+0.01114+0.00055=1.16094$. Hence \$116. Part (b) is as above: $e^{0.995}\approx 2.705$, hence \$270.
===END===

TITLE: Stacking 100 pennies for the biggest product
QUESTION:
You have $100$ pennies. You split them into stacks, as many as you like and of any sizes (the sizes add up to $100$). Your payout is the **product** of the stack sizes; for example stacks of $10$, $20$ and $70$ pay $10\cdot 20\cdot 70=14000$.

The best possible payout can be written as $a\cdot b^c$ with $a$ and $b$ coprime integers ($a$ as small as possible, $b>1$). Find $abc$.
HINT1:
Ask what a single large stack is worth compared with splitting it in two. And what is a stack of $1$ worth?
HINT2:
Any stack of size $n\ge 5$ can be split into $2$ and $n-2$ with $2(n-2)>n$. So only stacks of $2$ and $3$ (and possibly $4=2+2$) survive. Then compare three $2$s with two $3$s.
ANSWER:
The best payout is $2^2\cdot3^{32}=4\cdot 3^{32}$, so $abc=4\cdot3\cdot32=384$.
REFRESH:
NONE
EXPLANATION:
A stack of $1$ multiplies the payout by $1$, so it wastes a penny: merging it into another stack multiplies that stack by $\frac{n+1}{n}>1$. A big stack is also wasteful: splitting $n\ge5$ into $2+(n-2)$ multiplies the payout by $\frac{2(n-2)}{n}>1$. So the optimum only uses $2$s and $3$s ($4$ equals $2\cdot2$, so it can be written as two $2$s).

Between $2$ and $3$, the $3$s are better: six pennies give $2^3=8$ as three $2$s but $3^2=9$ as two $3$s. So use as many $3$s as possible, with at most two $2$s. Since $100=3\cdot 32+4$, the optimum is thirty-two $3$s and two $2$s.

(Continuous intuition: splitting $N$ into $k$ equal parts gives $(N/k)^k$, maximised when each part is $e\approx2.718$; the integer closest in value is $3$.)
SOLUTION: Exchange argument
Take an optimal splitting.

1. **No stack of 1**: merge it with any other stack $n$; the product goes from $n$ to $n+1$.
2. **No stack of 5 or more**: replace $n\ge5$ by $2$ and $n-2$; since $2(n-2)-n=n-4>0$, the product increases.
3. **Stacks of 4** can be replaced by $2+2$ without changing the product, so assume only $2$s and $3$s.
4. **At most two 2s**: three $2$s ($6$ pennies, product $8$) can be replaced by two $3$s (product $9$).

So the optimum has $k$ threes and $j\le 2$ twos with $3k+2j=100$. Since $100\equiv 1\pmod 3$, we need $2j\equiv1\pmod3$, so $j=2$ and $k=32$. The payout is
$$2^2\cdot3^{32}=4\cdot 3^{32}.$$
Here $a=4$, $b=3$, $c=32$ (coprime), and $abc=4\cdot3\cdot32=384$.
FOLLOWUP: 20 stones
You have $20$ stones and split them into groups; your score is the product of the group sizes. What is the largest possible score?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$1458$. By the same argument only $2$s and $3$s are used, with at most two $2$s. $20=3\cdot6+2$, so six $3$s and one $2$: $3^6\cdot 2=729\cdot2=1458$.
===END===

TITLE: Remainder of 11 to the 2360
QUESTION:
Find the remainder when $11^{2360}$ is divided by $50$.

Compute the first few powers of $11$ modulo $50$ and look for a pattern. Alternatively, write $11=1+10$.

**Congruences**: $a\equiv b \pmod m$ means $m$ divides $a-b$; congruences can be added and multiplied, so powers can be reduced step by step.

**Euler's theorem**: if $\gcd(a,m)=1$ then $a^{\varphi(m)}\equiv1\pmod m$, where $\varphi(m)$ counts the integers in $\{1,\dots,m\}$ coprime to $m$. For a prime $p$ this is Fermat: $a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$.

A power of a number close to $1$ (here $11=1+10$) is easy to expand: everything beyond the linear term is divisible by $100$. So modulo $50$ only $1+10n$ matters, and $10\cdot 2360$ is a multiple of $50$.

$$11^{2360}=(1+10)^{2360}=1+2360\cdot10+\sum_{k\ge2}\binom{2360}{k}10^k.$$
Every term with $k\ge2$ contains $10^2=100$, which is divisible by $50$. And $2360\cdot10=23600=50\cdot472$. Hence
$$11^{2360}\equiv1\pmod{50}.$$
SOLUTION: Find the cycle
Modulo $50$: $11^2=121\equiv21$, $11^4\equiv21^2=441\equiv41$, $11^5\equiv41\cdot11=451\equiv1$. Since $2360=5\cdot472$, $11^{2360}=(11^5)^{472}\equiv1^{472}=1$.
SOLUTION: Euler's theorem
$\varphi(50)=50\cdot(1-\frac12)(1-\frac15)=20$ and $\gcd(11,50)=1$, so $11^{20}\equiv1\pmod{50}$. As $2360=20\cdot118$, $11^{2360}\equiv1$.
FOLLOWUP: 15 to the 2021 mod 17
Compute $15^{2021} \bmod 17$.
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
$2$. Since $15\equiv-2\pmod{17}$, $15^{2021}\equiv(-2)^{2021}=-2^{2021}$. Now $2^4=16\equiv-1$, so $2^8\equiv1\pmod{17}$. As $2021=8\cdot252+5$, $2^{2021}\equiv2^5=32\equiv15$. Hence $15^{2021}\equiv-15\equiv2\pmod{17}$.

NONE

Mathematically, the call payoff is convex and a mean-preserving spread raises the expectation of a convex function (Jensen).

For the put the same argument works, and parity confirms it: $C-P=S-Ke^{-rT}$ has no $\sigma$ in it, so if $C$ goes up with volatility, $P$ goes up by exactly the same amount. Vega is largest at the money and for long maturities (it is $S\varphi(d_1)\sqrt T$).

Caveats worth mentioning in an interview: this is for plain vanilla options with everything else held fixed. Some exotics can lose value when vol rises (a knock-out call is more likely to be knocked out; a deep in-the-money digital loses probability of staying in the money).
SOLUTION: Convexity argument
The call payoff $f(s)=\max(s-K,0)$ is convex. Under the pricing measure the forward $\mathbb E S_T=Se^{rT}$ is fixed; raising $\sigma$ spreads the distribution of $S_T$ around that mean (for lognormal $S_T$ the higher-$\sigma$ law is a mean-preserving spread of the lower-$\sigma$ one). For a convex $f$, a mean-preserving spread increases $\mathbb E f(S_T)$, so $C=e^{-rT}\mathbb E f(S_T)$ increases. The put payoff $\max(K-s,0)$ is also convex, so the same holds.
SOLUTION: Black–Scholes vega
$C=SN(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$ with $d_{1,2}=\dfrac{\ln(S/K)+(r\pm\frac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}$. Differentiating and using the identity $S\varphi(d_1)=Ke^{-rT}\varphi(d_2)$ gives
$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial\sigma}=S\varphi(d_1)\sqrt T>0.$$
By parity $P=C-S+Ke^{-rT}$, so $\partial P/\partial\sigma=\partial C/\partial\sigma>0$ as well.
===END===

REFRESH:
**Put-call parity** (European options, no dividends): $C-P=S_0-Ke^{-rT}$.

**No-arbitrage / law of one price**: two portfolios with the same payoff in every state must have the same price today.
EXPLANATION:
At expiry, $\max(S_T-K,0)-\max(K-S_T,0)=S_T-K$ whatever $S_T$ is: when $S_T>K$ the call pays and the put is worthless, and vice versa. So "long call, short put" is a synthetic forward, and it must cost what the forward costs: the stock today minus the present value of the strike. Hence $P=C-S+Ke^{-rT}$.

Here the call is \$10 and the forward is worth $100-100e^{-0.05}\approx4.88$, so the put is worth $10-4.88=5.12$. The call is more expensive than the put because the strike is paid a year later: at-the-money spot, the forward is in the money for the call.
SOLUTION: Apply put-call parity
$$P=C-S_0+Ke^{-rT}=10-100+100e^{-0.05}.$$
$e^{-0.05}=1-0.05+0.00125-0.0000208+\dots\approx0.951229$, so $100e^{-0.05}\approx95.12$ and
$$P\approx10-100+95.12=5.12.$$
SOLUTION: Arbitrage if the put is mispriced
Suppose the put traded at some price $P$. Today: sell the put ($+P$), buy the call ($-10$), short the stock ($+100$), and deposit the net cash $90+P$ at $5\%$.

At expiry: the long call and short put together pay $S_T-100$; buying back the stock costs $S_T$; the deposit is worth $(90+P)e^{0.05}$. Net:
$$(S_T-100)-S_T+(90+P)e^{0.05}=(90+P)e^{0.05}-100,$$
a riskless amount, positive exactly when $P>100e^{-0.05}-90\approx5.12$. If $P<5.12$, doing every trade in reverse locks in a riskless profit. Only $P\approx5.12$ leaves no free money.
FOLLOWUP: A 90-day example

FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:

With the $90/360=0.25$ convention: $100e^{-0.01}\approx99.005$ and $P\approx4.005$. Either way the put is about \$4.01, roughly \$1 below the call, i.e. about the interest on the strike: $K(1-e^{-rT})\approx100\cdot0.01$.
FOLLOWUP: Explain put-call parity
Explain put-call parity: what it says, why it holds, and when it can fail.
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
For European options on the same non-dividend stock with the same strike $K$ and maturity $T$: $C-P=S_0-Ke^{-rT}$. **Why**: the portfolio "long call, short put" pays $S_T-K$ in every state, the same as "long stock, borrow $Ke^{-rT}$"; by no-arbitrage they cost the same today. **Uses**: price a put from a call, build synthetic stock or forwards, back out the implied forward/interest rate or dividend, and check that call and put implied vols agree. **With dividends**: $C-P=S_0-\text{PV(div)}-Ke^{-rT}$ (or $S_0e^{-qT}$ for a continuous yield $q$). **Limits**: it is model-free but needs European exercise (for American options only inequalities hold), frictionless borrowing/shorting (hard-to-borrow stocks or funding costs break it in practice), and same underlying, strike and maturity.

TITLE: How much of your bankroll to bet
QUESTION:

Betting everything each time is ruin with probability one in the long run; betting nothing earns nothing. Look at the growth rate of the logarithm of wealth.
HINT2:
If you bet a fraction $f$, wealth is multiplied by $1+f$ or $1-f$. Maximise $g(f)=0.75\ln(1+f)+0.25\ln(1-f)$.
ANSWER:
$f^*=\frac12$ of the bankroll (Kelly: $f^*=p-q=0.75-0.25$).
REFRESH:
**Kelly criterion**: for a bet paying $b$ to $1$ won with probability $p$ ($q=1-p$), the fraction maximising $\mathbb E\ln(\text{wealth})$ is $f^*=p-\dfrac{q}{b}$.

**Law of large numbers**: after $n$ independent bets, $\frac1n\ln W_n\to\mathbb E\ln(1+fR)$ almost surely, so maximising the expected log maximises the long-run growth rate.
EXPLANATION:
Wealth compounds multiplicatively, so what matters in the long run is the average of the **log** return, not the average return. Betting a large fraction has a high expected return but a bad log return (a loss of fraction $f$ costs $-\ln(1-f)$, which explodes as $f\to1$). The Kelly fraction balances the two.

For an even-money bet, the optimum is simply the edge: $f^*=p-q=0.5$. At that fraction wealth grows by about $e^{0.131}\approx1.14$ per bet in the long run. Traders usually bet a fraction of Kelly (half Kelly keeps about $3/4$ of the growth rate with half the volatility), because $p$ is estimated with error and overbetting is much worse than underbetting.
SOLUTION: Maximise expected log wealth
Bet a fraction $f$ each round. Wealth is multiplied by $1+f$ (probability $0.75$) or $1-f$ (probability $0.25$). The long-run growth rate per bet is
$$g(f)=0.75\ln(1+f)+0.25\ln(1-f).$$
$$g'(f)=\frac{0.75}{1+f}-\frac{0.25}{1-f}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ 0.75(1-f)=0.25(1+f)\ \Longrightarrow\ 0.5=f.$$
$g''<0$, so this is the maximum: bet **half** your bankroll. Growth rate $g(0.5)=0.75\ln1.5+0.25\ln0.5\approx0.3041-0.1733=0.1308$ per bet.
SOLUTION: Kelly formula
$f^*=p-\frac qb$ with $p=0.75$, $q=0.25$, $b=1$: $f^*=0.75-0.25=0.5$. Equivalently, "edge over odds": the expected profit per \$1 is $0.75-0.25=0.5$, divided by the odds $b=1$.
FOLLOWUP: Kelly for a continuous strategy

FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
For small returns, the log-growth rate when allocating a fraction $f$ is $g(f)\approx f\mu-\frac12f^2\sigma^2$, maximised at $f^*=\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}=\frac{0.05}{0.0225}\approx2.22$: full Kelly means $2.2\times$ leverage, with growth rate $\frac{\mu^2}{2\sigma^2}\approx5.6\%$ a year. In practice one uses a fraction of Kelly (about $\frac14$ to $\frac12$, i.e. $0.55$ to $1.1\times$ capital), because $\mu$ is very uncertain, returns have fat tails, and overshooting Kelly quickly destroys growth (at $2f^*$ the growth rate is zero).

TITLE: Value at Risk and its limitations
QUESTION:
Explain what Value at Risk (VaR) is, how it is typically computed, and what its main limitations are.
HINT1:
VaR answers one question about the loss distribution over a horizon. Which quantile of it?
HINT2:
Think about what VaR does **not** tell you: how bad the losses beyond the threshold are, and whether the risk of two books combined is at most the sum of their risks.
ANSWER:
VaR at level $\alpha$ over horizon $h$ is the loss threshold that is exceeded with probability only $1-\alpha$ (e.g. 1-day 99% VaR); it ignores the size of tail losses, is not subadditive, and depends heavily on model assumptions and data.
REFRESH:
**Quantile**: $q_\alpha(L)=\inf\{x: P(L\le x)\ge\alpha\}$.

**Expected shortfall**: $\text{ES}_\alpha=\mathbb E[L\mid L\ge\text{VaR}_\alpha]$ (for continuous $L$), the average loss in the worst $1-\alpha$ of cases.
EXPLANATION:
If $L$ is the loss of a portfolio over a horizon (1 day, 10 days), $\text{VaR}_{99\%}=\$1\text{M}$ means: on 99% of days you lose less than \$1M, and on 1% of days (about 2–3 days a year) you lose more. It condenses risk into one number in dollars, which makes it easy to set limits and compare desks.

Computation: (1) **parametric**: assume normal P&L with volatility $\sigma_P$, then $\text{VaR}_\alpha=z_\alpha\sigma_P$ ($z_{95\%}=1.645$, $z_{99\%}=2.326$); (2) **historical simulation**: revalue today's portfolio under the last $N$ days of market moves and take the empirical quantile; (3) **Monte Carlo** from a fitted model.

Limitations: it says nothing about how big the loss is when the threshold is breached (two books with the same VaR can have wildly different tails); it is not subadditive, so splitting or merging portfolios can make VaR go down while risk goes up; normal assumptions understate fat tails and correlations that jump in a crisis; historical windows miss events not in the sample and are procyclical; liquidity (you can't exit at the marked price) and horizon scaling ($\sqrt h$ assumes i.i.d. returns) are ignored; and it can be gamed, e.g. by selling far out-of-the-money options, which earn premium almost always and lose hugely beyond the quantile. Expected shortfall and stress tests address some of these.
SOLUTION: Definition and a worked number
Let $L$ be the loss over horizon $h$. $\text{VaR}_\alpha=q_\alpha(L)$, the $\alpha$-quantile. Example: a \$10M equity position with daily volatility $1.5\%$, normal P&L. Then $\sigma_P=\$150{,}000$ and
$$\text{VaR}_{99\%,1d}=2.326\times150{,}000\approx\$349{,}000,\qquad \text{VaR}_{99\%,10d}\approx349{,}000\sqrt{10}\approx\$1.10\text{M}.$$
Non-subadditivity example: two independent bonds, each defaulting with probability $4\%$ and losing \$100 then. Each alone has $\text{VaR}_{95\%}=0$ (default is less likely than $5\%$). Together, $P(\text{at least one default})=1-0.96^2=7.84\%>5\%$, so the combined $\text{VaR}_{95\%}=\$100>0+0$: diversification appears to increase risk.
FOLLOWUP: Living with a VaR limit
Your daily VaR limit is \$100K and you may take any position. What does this constrain, in practice?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
It caps size relative to volatility: with a parametric VaR, notional $\le\frac{100{,}000}{z_\alpha\,\sigma_{\text{daily}}}$. For a stock with $2\%$ daily vol: at $99\%$ ($z=2.326$) about $\frac{100{,}000}{0.0465}\approx\$2.15$M; at $95\%$ ($z=1.645$) about \$3.04M. Consequences: when volatility rises you must cut positions even if your view is unchanged; hedged or diversified books allow more gross notional (VaR uses net correlated risk); the confidence level and the model matter a lot. And what it does **not** constrain: tail risk beyond the quantile (short options, illiquid or gap-risk positions can look small in VaR), so desks also get stress-loss, Greek and concentration limits.

TITLE: Overfitting in backtests
QUESTION:
Explain what overfitting means when backtesting a trading strategy, why it happens, and how you would guard against it.
HINT1:
A backtest is an estimate made on a finite, noisy sample. What happens if you try many variants and keep the best one?
HINT2:
Think "data snooping": the maximum of many noisy Sharpe ratios is biased upward even if every strategy has zero true edge. Then think of the defences: out-of-sample data, fewer parameters, economic rationale, adjusting for the number of trials.
ANSWER:
Overfitting is fitting the noise of the historical sample (via too many parameters or too many tried variants) so the backtest looks great but has no predictive power; guard with out-of-sample/walk-forward testing, few parameters with an economic rationale, realistic costs, and deflating the Sharpe for the number of trials.
REFRESH:
**Standard error of a Sharpe ratio**: with $T$ years of i.i.d. returns, the estimated annual Sharpe $\widehat{SR}$ has standard error about $\sqrt{(1+\frac12SR^2)/T}$.

**Maximum of many noise estimates**: the maximum of $N$ independent $N(0,s^2)$ variables is about $s\sqrt{2\ln N}$ for large $N$.
EXPLANATION:
A backtest Sharpe ratio is a noisy statistic. If you try $N$ strategies (or parameter settings) with zero true edge and keep the best, you'll still find something that looks good: its expected backtest Sharpe is roughly $\text{SE}\cdot\sqrt{2\ln N}$. With 5 years of data (SE $\approx0.45$) and 200 trials, that's about $0.45\times3.3\approx1.5$ from pure luck. Many parameters do the same thing implicitly: each free knob is a trial.

Symptoms: performance very sensitive to parameters, great in-sample and poor out-of-sample, a story invented after the fact. Defences: hold out data you never look at until the end; walk-forward (re-fit on a rolling window, test on the next one); keep models simple and grounded in an economic reason; count every trial and deflate the Sharpe accordingly; check stability across sub-periods, assets and parameter neighbourhoods; include realistic costs and fills; beware look-ahead and survivorship bias.
SOLUTION: A quick quantitative check
Suppose you test $N=100$ variants of a rule on $4$ years of daily data, and in truth none has any edge. Each backtest Sharpe is approximately $N(0,\frac14)$, i.e. standard error $\frac1{\sqrt4}=0.5$. The best of $100$ is around $0.5\sqrt{2\ln100}=0.5\sqrt{9.2}\approx1.5$. So a reported in-sample Sharpe of $1.5$ from such a search is fully consistent with no edge at all; out of sample it would regress to about $0$. The fix is to compare the best Sharpe with this "luck benchmark" (deflated Sharpe ratio) and to confirm on data not used in the search.
FOLLOWUP: Sharpe 3 in backtest, 0.5 live
A market-making strategy had a backtested Sharpe ratio of $3$, but live it delivers $0.5$. What could explain the gap?
FOLLOWUP_ANSWER:
(1) **Fill assumptions / adverse selection**: backtests often assume your quote fills whenever the price touches it; live, you get filled mostly when the market moves through you (informed flow), and you are behind others in the queue when it doesn't. This alone can erase most of a market maker's edge. (2) **Latency and competition**: faster players take the good trades; quotes are stale. (3) **Costs**: exchange fees and rebate tiers, spread paid when hedging, market impact of your own trades. (4) **Overfitting / data snooping** of the parameters, plus look-ahead or survivorship bias. (5) **Regime change**: lower volatility or tighter spreads than in the sample. (6) **Statistical noise**: with one year live, the standard error of the Sharpe is about $\sqrt{1+\frac12(0.5)^2}\approx1.06$, so $0.5$ vs a "true" value near $1.5$ is not even significant; but a gap to $3$ usually points to (1)–(4). Diagnose by comparing live and simulated fills trade by trade (markouts after fills).

HINT2:

By linearity, $\mathbb E\left[\sum_dI_d\right]=365\left(1-\left(\frac{364}{365}\right)^n\right)$.
