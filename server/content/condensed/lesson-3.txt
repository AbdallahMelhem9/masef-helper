===LESSON===
TITLE: Conditional expectation and martingales
DESCRIPTION: Forien §5–6 condensed: conditional expectation built by orthogonal projection, its properties (tower, taking out what is known, freezing), conditioning on a variable, martingales, stopping times, optional stopping, almost sure convergence, uniform integrability and convergence in L^1. 10 statements instead of 40.
INTRO:
Two ideas in this lesson, and both are about information. The first is conditional expectation: what is the best guess of a quantity \(X\) when you only know the answers to the questions in a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{G}\)? The answer, \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\), is a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable variable with the same averages as \(X\) on every event of \(\mathcal{G}\). We build it here by orthogonal projection in \(L^2\) (Theorem 4 of Lesson 2) and extend it to integrable variables by monotone limits; this avoids the Radon–Nikodym theorem entirely, and it makes the geometric picture, projection onto the information, the definition rather than a remark. Two of its properties are the ones you will use every day in stochastic calculus: "taking out what is known" and the freezing lemma, \(\mathbb{E}[f(X, Z) \mid \mathcal{G}] = g(Z)\) with \(g(z) = \mathbb{E}[f(X, z)]\) when \(X\) is independent of \(\mathcal{G}\) and \(Z\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable. Hoffmann's Markov property in Chapter 2 is this lemma applied to \(X = B_{t+s} - B_t\) and \(Z = B_t\).

The second idea is the martingale: a process whose future increments have zero conditional mean given the present. In discrete time the theory has three pillars, and they are all here. Optional stopping (Theorem 7): you cannot beat a fair game by choosing when to stop, provided the stopping rule is bounded or the game has bounded stakes. Almost sure convergence (Theorem 8): a martingale bounded in \(L^1\) settles down. Uniform integrability (Definition 9, Theorems 9 and 10): the exact condition under which the limit can be taken inside the expectation, under which the martingale is closed by its limit, and under which optional stopping holds for arbitrary stopping times. Hoffmann quotes only the uniform integrability part in Chapter 1 (to identify \(L^2\) limits of Gaussians), but the whole of stochastic integration is built on continuous-time versions of these three theorems.

Ten statements out of forty. Left out: the elementary discrete conditioning as a separate block (it is now a special case of the definition), the exercises, Kolmogorov's 0–1 law, and the proof of the upcrossing inequality (sketched in the deep dive of Theorem 8, admitted as in the prérentrée).

KIND: theorem
TITLE: Theorem 6 (Conditional expectation: definition and existence by projection — Forien Prop. 12 and the Remark after it)
IMPORTANCE: imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
Let \(\mathcal{G} \subset \mathcal{F}\) be a sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra and \(X\) an integrable random variable (or \(X \ge 0\), possibly non-integrable). There exists a random variable \(Y\), \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable and integrable (resp. with values in \([0, +\infty]\)), unique up to a.s. equality, such that
\[
\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A] \qquad \text{for every } A \in \mathcal{G},
\]
or equivalently \(\mathbb{E}[XZ] = \mathbb{E}[YZ]\) for every bounded \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable \(Z\). It is denoted \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\), the **conditional expectation of \(X\) given \(\mathcal{G}\)**; one writes \(\mathbb{P}(A \mid \mathcal{G}) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_A \mid \mathcal{G}]\).

For \(X \in L^2\), \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\) is the orthogonal projection of \(X\) on the closed subspace \(L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, \mathbb{P})\): the best approximation of \(X\) in mean square by a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable variable.

Elementary cases: if \(\mathcal{G}\) is generated by a countable partition \((A_k)\) of \(\Omega\) into events of positive probability, then \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \sum_k \frac{\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_{A_k}]}{\mathbb{P}(A_k)}\mathbf{1}_{A_k}\); if \(\mathcal{G} = \{\varnothing, \Omega\}\), \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \mathbb{E}[X]\); if \(X\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = X\).
PROOF:
**Uniqueness.** Let \(Y, Y'\) both satisfy the property. For \(n \in \mathbb{N}\), \(A_n = \{Y > Y'\} \cap \{Y' \le n\} \in \mathcal{G}\) and \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_{A_n}] = \mathbb{E}[Y'\mathbf{1}_{A_n}] \le n\), so \(\mathbb{E}[(Y - Y')\mathbf{1}_{A_n}] = 0\) with a non-negative integrand: \(\mathbb{P}(A_n) = 0\). Hence \(Y \le Y'\) a.s. on \(\{Y' < \infty\}\), trivially on \(\{Y' = \infty\}\); by symmetry \(Y = Y'\) a.s.

**Existence, \(X \in L^2\).** Let \(Y\) be a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable version of the projection of \(X\) on \(H = L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, \mathbb{P})\) (Theorem 4). For \(A \in \mathcal{G}\), \(\mathbf{1}_A \in H\), so \(\mathbb{E}[(X - Y)\mathbf{1}_A] = 0\). Positivity: if \(X \ge 0\), then with \(A = \{Y < 0\} \in \mathcal{G}\), \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A] \ge 0\) while \(Y\mathbf{1}_A \le 0\), so \(\mathbb{P}(A) = 0\). Linearity of the projection then gives monotonicity.

**Existence, \(X \ge 0\).** Let \(X_n = \min(X, n) \in L^2\) and \(Y_n = \mathbb{E}[X_n \mid \mathcal{G}]\); by monotonicity \(Y_n \le Y_{n+1}\) a.s., so (after modification on a null set) \(Y = \lim \uparrow Y_n\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable with values in \([0, \infty]\). For \(A \in \mathcal{G}\), monotone convergence twice: \(\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A] = \lim \mathbb{E}[X_n\mathbf{1}_A] = \lim \mathbb{E}[Y_n\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A]\). If \(X\) is integrable, \(A = \Omega\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] < \infty\).

**Existence, \(X \in L^1\).** \(Y = \mathbb{E}[X^+ \mid \mathcal{G}] - \mathbb{E}[X^- \mid \mathcal{G}]\), both terms integrable.

**Equivalence of the two conditions.** Indicators, then simple \(Z\) by linearity, then bounded \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable \(Z\) as a uniform limit of simple ones, with dominated convergence (\(|XZ_n| \le |X|\sup|Z|\)).

**Elementary cases.** For the partition, every \(A \in \mathcal{G}\) is a union of cells, and \(\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A] = \sum_{k : A_k \subset A}\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_{A_k}] = \sum_{k : A_k \subset A}c_k\mathbb{P}(A_k) = \mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A]\) for \(Y = \sum_k c_k\mathbf{1}_{A_k}\), \(c_k = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_{A_k}]/\mathbb{P}(A_k)\). The other two are immediate from the definition. \(\square\)
REFRESH:
**Orthogonal projection** (Theorem 4): for a closed subspace \(H \subset L^2\), \(\pi_H(X)\) is the unique element of \(H\) with \(\mathbb{E}[(X - \pi_H(X))Z] = 0\) for all \(Z \in H\); it is linear.
EXPLANATION:
We are defining the expectation of \(X\) given partial information. The information is a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{G}\); the answer must be a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable variable, since it can only depend on what \(\mathcal{G}\) knows, and it must have the same averages as \(X\) on every event of \(\mathcal{G}\). That property determines it up to a null set. Existence is built by geometry, without Radon–Nikodym: for \(X \in L^2\), the conditional expectation is the orthogonal projection of \(X\) on the subspace of \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable square-integrable variables (Theorem 4), and the general case follows by truncating \(X\) and passing to the monotone limit. When \(\mathcal{G}\) is generated by a partition, the formula reduces to the elementary conditional averages \(\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_{A_k}]/\mathbb{P}(A_k)\) on each cell.
DEEP:
We want to construct \(Y = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\) and prove it is unique. Plan: uniqueness from the defining identity; existence first in \(L^2\) by projection, then for \(X \ge 0\) by monotone limits, then for \(X \in L^1\) by splitting.

**Step 1: uniqueness.** Suppose \(Y\) and \(Y'\) are both \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable with \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[Y'\mathbf{1}_A]\) for all \(A \in \mathcal{G}\). The event \(A = \{Y > Y'\}\) belongs to \(\mathcal{G}\) because both variables are \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable; on it \(Y - Y' > 0\), and \(\mathbb{E}[(Y - Y')\mathbf{1}_A] = 0\), so \(\mathbb{P}(A) = 0\) (a non-negative variable with zero expectation vanishes a.s.). Thus \(Y \le Y'\) a.s., and by symmetry \(Y = Y'\) a.s. (When \(Y'\) may take the value \(+\infty\), intersect \(A\) with \(\{Y' \le n\}\) first so that the expectations are finite, then let \(n \to \infty\).)

**Step 2: existence in \(L^2\).** Let \(H = L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, \mathbb{P})\), a closed subspace of \(L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\), and let \(Y = \pi_H(X)\), a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable variable. Theorem 4 says \(\mathbb{E}[(X - Y)Z] = 0\) for every \(Z \in H\); taking \(Z = \mathbf{1}_A\) with \(A \in \mathcal{G}\) gives exactly the defining property. So on \(L^2\) the conditional expectation is the orthogonal projection: the \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable variable closest to \(X\) in mean square, and the residual \(X - \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\) is orthogonal to every \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable variable. Two consequences we need: positivity, since if \(X \ge 0\) then \(A = \{Y < 0\} \in \mathcal{G}\) has \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A] \ge 0\) while \(Y\mathbf{1}_A \le 0\), forcing \(\mathbb{P}(A) = 0\); and monotonicity, by linearity of the projection.

**Step 3: existence for \(X \ge 0\).** Truncate: \(X_n = \min(X, n)\) is bounded, hence in \(L^2\), and \(X_n \uparrow X\). Let \(Y_n = \mathbb{E}[X_n \mid \mathcal{G}]\). By monotonicity \(Y_n \le Y_{n+1}\) a.s., so \(Y = \lim_n Y_n\) exists in \([0, +\infty]\) and is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable. For \(A \in \mathcal{G}\), monotone convergence on both sides gives \(\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A] = \lim \mathbb{E}[X_n\mathbf{1}_A] = \lim \mathbb{E}[Y_n\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A]\). If \(X\) is integrable, \(A = \Omega\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] < \infty\), so \(Y\) is finite a.s. and integrable.

**Step 4: existence for \(X \in L^1\), and the second form of the condition.** Set \(Y = \mathbb{E}[X^+ \mid \mathcal{G}] - \mathbb{E}[X^- \mid \mathcal{G}]\). The identity with \(\mathbf{1}_A\) extends to bounded \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable \(Z\) by the standard machine: simple \(Z\) by linearity, then a uniform limit of simple functions with dominated convergence.

**Step 5: the elementary cases.** For a partition, \(Y = \sum_k c_k\mathbf{1}_{A_k}\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, and it suffices to check the identity on the cells (they generate \(\mathcal{G}\) and both sides are \(\sigma\)-additive in \(A\)): \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_{A_k}] = c_k\mathbb{P}(A_k) = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_{A_k}]\). This recovers \(\mathbb{P}(B \mid A_k) = \mathbb{P}(B \cap A_k)/\mathbb{P}(A_k)\) as the value of \(\mathbb{P}(B \mid \mathcal{G})\) on \(A_k\).

Used next: all of Proposition 11; Hoffmann's Corollary 8 and Remark 9 compare this projection with the projection onto a Gaussian subspace.
EXAMPLE:
Let \(X_1, X_2\) be independent fair coin flips with values in \(\{0, 1\}\), \(S = X_1 + X_2\), and \(\mathcal{G} = \sigma(X_1)\), generated by the partition \(\{X_1 = 0\}, \{X_1 = 1\}\). The elementary formula gives \(\mathbb{E}[S \mid \mathcal{G}] = \frac12\mathbf{1}_{\{X_1 = 0\}} + \frac32\mathbf{1}_{\{X_1 = 1\}} = X_1 + \frac12\). Check the defining property on \(A = \{X_1 = 1\}\): \(\mathbb{E}[S\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{P}(X_1 = 1) + \mathbb{P}(X_1 = 1, X_2 = 1) = \frac12 + \frac14 = \frac34\), and \(\mathbb{E}[(X_1 + \frac12)\mathbf{1}_A] = \frac32\cdot\frac12 = \frac34\). Geometrically, \(X_1 + \frac12\) is the function of \(X_1\) closest to \(S\) in mean square; the residual \(S - X_1 - \frac12 = X_2 - \frac12\) is orthogonal to every function of \(X_1\).

TITLE: Proposition 11 (Properties of conditional expectation — Forien Prop. 13)
IMPORTANCE: imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
Let \(\mathcal{G} \subset \mathcal{F}\) be a sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra and \(X, Y\) integrable (or non-negative) random variables. All identities hold almost surely.

1. **(Linearity)** \(\mathbb{E}[aX + bY \mid \mathcal{G}] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]\).
2. **(Monotonicity)** If \(X \le Y\), then \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \le \mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{G}]\); in particular \(|\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]| \le \mathbb{E}[|X| \mid \mathcal{G}]\).
3. **(Tower property)** \(\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\big] = \mathbb{E}[X]\), and if \(\mathcal{H} \subset \mathcal{G}\), then \(\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \,\big|\, \mathcal{H}\big] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]\).
4. **(Taking out what is known)** If \(Z\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable and \(XZ\) is integrable (or \(X, Z \ge 0\)), then \(\mathbb{E}[XZ \mid \mathcal{G}] = Z\,\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\); in particular \(\mathbb{E}[Z \mid \mathcal{G}] = Z\).
5. **(Independence)** If \(X\) is independent of \(\mathcal{G}\), then \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \mathbb{E}[X]\).
6. **(Freezing)** If \(X\) is independent of \(\mathcal{G}\), \(Z\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, and \(f\) is measurable with \(f \ge 0\) or \(f(X, Z)\) integrable, then \(\mathbb{E}[f(X, Z) \mid \mathcal{G}] = g(Z)\), where \(g(z) = \mathbb{E}[f(X, z)]\).
7. **(Contraction, conditional Jensen)** \(\mathbb{E}\big|\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\big| \le \mathbb{E}|X|\); for \(\phi\) convex with \(\phi(X)\) integrable, \(\phi(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]) \le \mathbb{E}[\phi(X) \mid \mathcal{G}]\); hence \(\|\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\|_{L^p} \le \|X\|_{L^p}\) for \(p \ge 1\).
8. **(Conditional limit theorems)** Monotone convergence, Fatou's lemma and dominated convergence hold with \(\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot \mid \mathcal{G}]\) in place of \(\mathbb{E}\), almost surely.
PROOF:
Write \(\hat X = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\). Each identity is proved by checking that the right-hand side is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable and satisfies the defining property; uniqueness does the rest.

**1, 2.** Linearity: \(a\hat X + b\hat Y\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable and \(\mathbb{E}[(a\hat X + b\hat Y)\mathbf{1}_A] = a\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A] + b\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A]\). Monotonicity: \(\mathbb{E}[(\hat Y - \hat X)\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[(Y - X)\mathbf{1}_A] \ge 0\) for \(A = \{\hat Y < \hat X\} \in \mathcal{G}\), so \(\mathbb{P}(A) = 0\). Then \(-|X| \le X \le |X|\).

**3.** \(A = \Omega\) in the definition. For \(\mathcal{H} \subset \mathcal{G}\) and \(A \in \mathcal{H} \subset \mathcal{G}\): \(\mathbb{E}[\hat X\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A]\), and \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]\) is \(\mathcal{H}\)-measurable with the same integrals on \(\mathcal{H}\).

**4.** For \(Z = \mathbf{1}_B\), \(B \in \mathcal{G}\): \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_B\hat X\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_{A \cap B}]\) since \(A \cap B \in \mathcal{G}\). Simple \(Z\) by linearity, \(Z \ge 0\) by monotone convergence (with \(X \ge 0\)), then \(Z = Z^+ - Z^-\), \(X = X^+ - X^-\).

**5.** For \(A \in \mathcal{G}\), \(\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{P}(A) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbf{1}_A]\) by independence.

**6.** \(g\) is measurable and \(g(Z)\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable. For \(f = \mathbf{1}_{C \times D}\): \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_C(X)\mathbf{1}_D(Z)\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{P}(X \in C)\,\mathbb{P}(\{Z \in D\} \cap A) = \mathbb{E}[g(Z)\mathbf{1}_A]\), using independence of \(X\) and \(\mathcal{G}\) (\(\{Z \in D\} \cap A \in \mathcal{G}\)). The set of bounded \(f\) for which the identity holds is a vector space stable under bounded increasing limits (monotone convergence on both sides), so by Theorem 1 it contains all bounded measurable \(f\); then \(f \ge 0\) by monotone convergence and \(f = f^+ - f^-\).

**7.** From 2 and 3: \(\mathbb{E}|\hat X| \le \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[|X| \mid \mathcal{G}]] = \mathbb{E}|X|\). A convex \(\phi\) is the supremum of a countable family of affine functions \(a_nx + b_n\); by 1 and 2, \(\mathbb{E}[\phi(X) \mid \mathcal{G}] \ge a_n\hat X + b_n\) for every \(n\) a.s., and the supremum over the countable family is \(\phi(\hat X)\). With \(\phi = |\cdot|^p\) and 3: \(\mathbb{E}|\hat X|^p \le \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[|X|^p \mid \mathcal{G}]] = \mathbb{E}|X|^p\).

**8.** Monotone: if \(0 \le X_n \uparrow X\), then \(\hat X_n \uparrow\) some \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable \(Y\), and \(\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A] = \lim \mathbb{E}[\hat X_n\mathbf{1}_A] = \lim \mathbb{E}[X_n\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A]\). Fatou and dominated convergence follow as in Theorem 2. \(\square\)
REFRESH:
(none)
EXPLANATION:
These are the rules of computation with conditional expectations, and they are all consequences of the defining identity plus uniqueness: to prove \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = W\), check that \(W\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable and that \(\mathbb{E}[W\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A]\) for \(A \in \mathcal{G}\). Three rules carry the whole course: the tower property (conditioning in two steps is conditioning on the smaller information), taking out what is known (a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable factor behaves like a constant), and the freezing lemma (to condition \(f(X, Z)\) on \(\mathcal{G}\) when \(X\) is independent of \(\mathcal{G}\) and \(Z\) is known, freeze \(Z = z\), average over \(X\), then plug \(Z\) back in). The last one is the Markov property of Brownian motion in Hoffmann's Chapter 2.
DEEP:
We want to prove the eight rules. Plan: for each identity, verify the two conditions of Theorem 6 for the proposed right-hand side; uniqueness concludes. Write \(\hat X = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]\).

**Step 1: linearity and monotonicity.** \(a\hat X + b\hat Y\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, and for \(A \in \mathcal{G}\), \(\mathbb{E}[(a\hat X + b\hat Y)\mathbf{1}_A] = a\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A] + b\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[(aX + bY)\mathbf{1}_A]\). For monotonicity, \(A = \{\hat Y < \hat X\} \in \mathcal{G}\) and \(\mathbb{E}[(\hat Y - \hat X)\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[(Y - X)\mathbf{1}_A] \ge 0\) while the integrand is \(< 0\) on \(A\): \(\mathbb{P}(A) = 0\). Applying this to \(-|X| \le X \le |X|\) gives \(|\hat X| \le \mathbb{E}[|X| \mid \mathcal{G}]\).

**Step 2: tower property.** \(A = \Omega\) in the definition gives \(\mathbb{E}[\hat X] = \mathbb{E}[X]\). If \(\mathcal{H} \subset \mathcal{G}\), then for \(A \in \mathcal{H}\) we also have \(A \in \mathcal{G}\), so \(\mathbb{E}[\hat X\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A]\): the variable \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]\), which is \(\mathcal{H}\)-measurable with these same integrals, is therefore also \(\mathbb{E}[\hat X \mid \mathcal{H}]\). The smaller information wins.

**Step 3: taking out what is known.** Start with \(Z = \mathbf{1}_B\), \(B \in \mathcal{G}\): the candidate \(\mathbf{1}_B\hat X\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable, and \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_B\hat X\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[\hat X\mathbf{1}_{A \cap B}] = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_{A \cap B}] = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_B\mathbf{1}_A]\), because \(A \cap B \in \mathcal{G}\). Simple \(Z\) follow by linearity; \(Z \ge 0\) with \(X \ge 0\) by monotone convergence along simple \(Z_n \uparrow Z\); the general case by splitting \(Z = Z^+ - Z^-\) and \(X = X^+ - X^-\) (the integrability of \(XZ\) keeps everything finite).

**Step 4: independence.** For \(A \in \mathcal{G}\), \(\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{P}(A) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbf{1}_A]\) by the factorisation property (Definition 5): the constant \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) does the job.

**Step 5: freezing.** First, \(g(z) = \int f(x, z)\, \mathcal{L}_X(dx)\) is measurable in \(z\) by Tonelli, so \(g(Z)\) is \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable. For a rectangle indicator \(f(x, z) = \mathbf{1}_C(x)\mathbf{1}_D(z)\) and \(A \in \mathcal{G}\):
\[
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_C(X)\mathbf{1}_D(Z)\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{P}(X \in C)\,\mathbb{P}(\{Z \in D\} \cap A) = \mathbb{E}[g(Z)\mathbf{1}_A],
\]
since \(\{Z \in D\} \cap A \in \mathcal{G}\) is independent of \(X\) and \(g(z) = \mathbb{P}(X \in C)\mathbf{1}_D(z)\). The bounded measurable \(f\) satisfying the identity form a vector space containing these indicators and stable under bounded increasing limits (monotone convergence on both sides), and the rectangles form a \(\pi\)-system generating the product \(\sigma\)-algebra; the function version of Theorem 1 gives all bounded measurable \(f\). Then \(f \ge 0\) by monotone convergence, and integrable \(f(X, Z)\) by \(f = f^+ - f^-\).

**Step 6: contraction and conditional Jensen.** \(\mathbb{E}|\hat X| \le \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[|X| \mid \mathcal{G}]] = \mathbb{E}|X|\) by Steps 1 and 2. For Jensen, use that a convex \(\phi : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) is the supremum of the countable family of affine functions \(a_nx + b_n\) lying below it (take the supporting lines at rational points); by linearity and monotonicity, \(\mathbb{E}[\phi(X) \mid \mathcal{G}] \ge a_n\hat X + b_n\) a.s. for every \(n\), and the countable supremum gives \(\phi(\hat X)\).

**Step 7: conditional limit theorems.** If \(0 \le X_n \uparrow X\), then \(\hat X_n\) increases a.s. to some \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable \(Y\), and monotone convergence on both sides of \(\mathbb{E}[\hat X_n\mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X_n\mathbf{1}_A]\) identifies \(Y = \hat X\). Fatou and dominated convergence are deduced exactly as in Theorem 2.

Used next: martingales are defined with rule 3 in mind (\(\mathbb{E}[M_m \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n\)); rule 7 shows closed martingales are UI (Theorem 10); in Hoffmann's Chapter 2, the Markov property is rule 6 with \(X = B_{t+s} - B_t\), \(Z = B_t\), \(\mathcal{G} = \mathcal{F}_t\), and Chapman–Kolmogorov is rule 3.
EXAMPLE:
Let \(B\) be a Brownian motion, \(\mathcal{F}_s = \sigma(B_u, u \le s)\), and \(s < t\). Write \(B_t = B_s + \Delta\) with \(\Delta = B_t - B_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t - s)\) independent of \(\mathcal{F}_s\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[B_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = B_s + \mathbb{E}[\Delta] = B_s\) (rules 4 and 5): Brownian motion is a martingale. Freezing with \(f(x, z) = (x + z)^2\): \(g(z) = \mathbb{E}[(\Delta + z)^2] = (t - s) + z^2\), so \(\mathbb{E}[B_t^2 \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = B_s^2 + (t - s)\), i.e. \(B_t^2 - t\) is a martingale. Freezing with \(f(x, z) = h(x + z)\) for bounded \(h\): \(\mathbb{E}[h(B_t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = g(B_s)\) with \(g(z) = \mathbb{E}[h(z + \Delta)] = \int h(y)\frac{e^{-(y - z)^2/2(t-s)}}{\sqrt{2\pi(t - s)}}\, dy\): this is the Markov property, Proposition 1 of Hoffmann's Chapter 2, with \(g = P_{t-s}h\).

KIND: proposition
TITLE: Proposition 12 (Conditioning on a random variable; the density formula — Forien §5, Remark after Prop. 13, Prop. 14)
IMPORTANCE: imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
For a random variable \(Y\) with values in \((E, \mathcal{E})\), define \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] := \mathbb{E}[X \mid \sigma(Y)]\). By Proposition 5 there is a measurable \(h : E \to \mathbb{R}\), unique \(\mathcal{L}_Y\)-a.e., with \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = h(Y)\); one writes \(h(y) = \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y]\). To prove that \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = h(Y)\), it suffices to check
\[
\mathbb{E}[X\,g(Y)] = \mathbb{E}[h(Y)\,g(Y)] \qquad \text{for every bounded measurable } g,
\]
or only for \(g = \mathbf{1}_B\) with \(B\) in a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{E}\) (and \(B = E\)).

**(Density formula)** If \((X, Y)\) is a random vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) with density \(p(x, y)\), and \(\varphi\) is measurable with \(\varphi(X)\) integrable, then \(\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X) \mid Y] = g(Y)\) with
\[
g(y) = \frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}} \varphi(x)\,p(x, y)\, dx}{p_Y(y)}, \qquad p_Y(y) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} p(x, y)\, dx,
\]
when \(p_Y(y) > 0\), and \(g(y) = 0\) otherwise. In words: given \(Y = y\), \(X\) has the **conditional density** \(x \mapsto p(x, y)/p_Y(y)\).
PROOF:
**Reduction to functions of \(Y\).** The bounded \(\sigma(Y)\)-measurable variables are exactly the \(g(Y)\) with \(g\) bounded measurable (Proposition 5), so the identity for all such \(g\) is the second form of the defining property. For the \(\pi\)-system version: the two finite measures \(B \mapsto \mathbb{E}[X^+\mathbf{1}_B(Y)] + \mathbb{E}[h(Y)^-\mathbf{1}_B(Y)]\) and \(B \mapsto \mathbb{E}[h(Y)^+\mathbf{1}_B(Y)] + \mathbb{E}[X^-\mathbf{1}_B(Y)]\) agree on the \(\pi\)-system and on \(E\), hence on \(\mathcal{E}\) by Proposition 1. Uniqueness of \(h\): if \(h(Y) = h'(Y)\) a.s., then \(\mathcal{L}_Y(h \neq h') = \mathbb{P}(h(Y) \neq h'(Y)) = 0\).

**Density formula.** \(p_Y\) is the density of \(Y\) (Tonelli). Let \(N = \{p_Y = 0\}\); for \(y \in N\), \(p(\cdot, y) = 0\) a.e., so \(\int \varphi(x)p(x, y)\, dx = 0\). Measurability of \(g\) is Fubini; integrability: \(\mathbb{E}|g(Y)| = \int_{N^c}|g|p_Y\, dy \le \int\!\!\int |\varphi(x)|p(x, y)\, dx\, dy = \mathbb{E}|\varphi(X)| < \infty\). For bounded measurable \(G\), by the transfer formula and Fubini (justified by \(\mathbb{E}|\varphi(X)| < \infty\)),
\[
\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)G(Y)] = \int G(y)\Big(\int \varphi(x)p(x, y)\, dx\Big)dy = \int_{N^c}G(y)g(y)p_Y(y)\, dy = \mathbb{E}[g(Y)G(Y)] . \qquad \square
\]
REFRESH:
**Functions of \(Y\)** (Proposition 5): a variable is \(\sigma(Y)\)-measurable if and only if it is of the form \(h(Y)\) with \(h\) measurable.
EXPLANATION:
Conditioning on a random variable \(Y\) means conditioning on the information \(\sigma(Y)\), and the result is a function of \(Y\): this is the meaning of the familiar notation \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y]\), which is defined only up to a set of values \(y\) that \(Y\) never takes. The practical content is the recipe for identifying \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\): guess a function \(h\), then verify \(\mathbb{E}[Xg(Y)] = \mathbb{E}[h(Y)g(Y)]\) for bounded \(g\). When the pair has a density, the recipe produces the explicit formula: average \(\varphi(x)\) against the slice \(p(\cdot, y)\) of the joint density, normalised by the density of \(Y\). This is how one computes with transition densities, such as the Gaussian kernel \(p_t(x, y)\) of Brownian motion in Hoffmann's Chapter 2.
DEEP:
We want to make \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\) explicit as a function of \(Y\), and to prove the density formula. Plan: reduce the defining property to test functions of \(Y\); then verify the formula by Fubini.

**Step 1: why a function of \(Y\), and why "\(\mathcal{L}_Y\)-a.e." uniqueness.** \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid \sigma(Y)]\) is \(\sigma(Y)\)-measurable, hence equal to \(h(Y)\) for some measurable \(h\) (Proposition 5). If \(h'\) is another such function, then \(h(Y) = h'(Y)\) a.s., i.e. \(\mathcal{L}_Y(\{h \neq h'\}) = 0\): \(h\) is determined only on the support of the law of \(Y\), which is why \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y]\) has no meaning for a single \(y\) with \(\mathbb{P}(Y = y) = 0\), only as a function.

**Step 2: the verification recipe.** The bounded \(\sigma(Y)\)-measurable variables are the \(g(Y)\), \(g\) bounded measurable (Proposition 5 again), so the identity \(\mathbb{E}[Xg(Y)] = \mathbb{E}[h(Y)g(Y)]\) for all such \(g\) is the second form of the defining property of Theorem 6. It even suffices to test \(g = \mathbf{1}_B\) for \(B\) in a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{E}\), together with \(B = E\): both sides define finite (signed) measures in \(B\), and Proposition 1, applied after separating positive and negative parts, extends the equality from the \(\pi\)-system to \(\mathcal{E}\). For \(Y\) real, testing \(B = (-\infty, a]\) is enough.

**Step 3: the density formula, well-posedness.** \(p_Y(y) = \int p(x, y)\, dx\) is the density of \(Y\) by Tonelli: \(\mathbb{P}(Y \in B) = \int_B\int p(x, y)\, dx\, dy\). On \(N = \{p_Y = 0\}\), the non-negative function \(p(\cdot, y)\) has zero integral, so it vanishes a.e. and \(\int \varphi(x)p(x, y)\, dx = 0\); the value \(g = 0\) on \(N\) is harmless since \(\mathbb{P}(Y \in N) = \int_N p_Y = 0\). Fubini gives measurability of \(y \mapsto \int \varphi(x)p(x, y)\, dx\), and \(\mathbb{E}|g(Y)| = \int_{N^c}|g(y)|p_Y(y)\, dy \le \int\!\!\int |\varphi(x)|p(x, y)\, dx\, dy = \mathbb{E}|\varphi(X)| < \infty\).

**Step 4: the density formula, verification.** For bounded measurable \(G\), the transfer formula for the vector \((X, Y)\) and Fubini (legitimate because \(\int\!\!\int |\varphi(x)G(y)|p(x, y)\, dx\, dy \le \sup|G|\,\mathbb{E}|\varphi(X)| < \infty\)) give
\[
\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)G(Y)] = \int_{\mathbb{R}}G(y)\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x)p(x, y)\, dx\Big)dy = \int_{N^c}G(y)\,g(y)\,p_Y(y)\, dy = \mathbb{E}[g(Y)G(Y)],
\]
where the middle equality uses \(\int \varphi(x)p(x, y)\, dx = g(y)p_Y(y)\) on \(N^c\) and \(= 0\) on \(N\), and the last one is the transfer formula for \(Y\). By Step 2, \(\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X) \mid Y] = g(Y)\).

**Reading the formula.** \(\mathbb{P}(X \in C \mid Y) = \int_C \frac{p(x, Y)}{p_Y(Y)}\, dx\): conditionally on \(Y = y\), \(X\) is distributed with density \(p(x, y)/p_Y(y)\), the normalised slice of the joint density. In the elementary case of a discrete \(Y\), the same recipe gives back \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y_k] = \mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_{\{Y = y_k\}}]/\mathbb{P}(Y = y_k)\).

Used next: the Gaussian conditioning formula (Lesson 4) can be derived this way or, faster, by orthogonality; in Hoffmann's Chapter 2, the transition kernel \(p_t(x, y) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}e^{-(y - x)^2/2t}\) is the conditional density of \(B_{s+t}\) given \(B_s = x\).
EXAMPLE:
Let \((X, Y)\) be uniform on the triangle \(\{0 < x < y < 1\}\), with density \(p(x, y) = 2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{0 < x < y < 1}\). Then \(p_Y(y) = \int_0^y 2\, dx = 2y\) on \((0, 1)\), and for \(y \in (0,1)\) the conditional density of \(X\) given \(Y = y\) is \(p(x, y)/p_Y(y) = \frac1y\mathbf{1}_{0 < x < y}\): uniform on \((0, y)\). Hence \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y] = Y/2\) and \(\mathbb{E}[X^2 \mid Y] = Y^2/3\). Check the recipe with \(g(y) = y\): \(\mathbb{E}[XY] = \int_0^1\int_0^y 2xy\, dx\, dy = \int_0^1 y^3\, dy = \frac14\), and \(\mathbb{E}[\frac{Y}{2}Y] = \frac12\int_0^1 y^2\cdot 2y\, dy = \frac14\).
