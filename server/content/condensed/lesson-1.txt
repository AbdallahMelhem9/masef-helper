===LESSON===
TITLE: Measure and integration: the toolbox
DESCRIPTION: Forien §1 condensed: σ-algebras and measures, the monotone class lemma and uniqueness of measures, the integral and its three limit theorems, parameters under the integral, Fubini. 8 statements instead of 30.
INTRO:
Why does a course on Brownian motion start with \(\sigma\)-algebras? Because the objects of stochastic calculus are random functions of time. To say "the probability that the path is continuous" or "the expectation of \(\int_0^T H_s\, dB_s\)" you need a notion of probability on spaces of functions, and the only way anyone has found to build one is measure theory: decide which sets are allowed (a \(\sigma\)-algebra), assign them a mass (a measure), and integrate against it (the expectation). The price is that only countable operations are permitted, and the reward is that limits, which are countable operations, behave.

This lesson keeps eight statements out of the thirty in Forien's first chapter. They are the ones that come back on every page of Hoffmann's course:

- \(\sigma\)-algebras, measurable maps and measures (Definitions 1 and 2) are the vocabulary; the continuity properties of measures are the form in which \(\sigma\)-additivity is actually used.
- The monotone class lemma (Theorem 1) and its consequence, the uniqueness of measures agreeing on a \(\pi\)-system (Proposition 1), are the tools for proving that two laws coincide, or that an identity holds for all measurable functions, by checking it on a small family. Hoffmann uses the function version to close the proof of the Markov property in Chapter 2.
- The integral (Definition/Proposition 2) is the expectation, and the three limit theorems (Theorem 2) are the only licences you will ever have to exchange a limit and an expectation.
- Parameters under the integral (Proposition 3) give the derivatives of characteristic functions and the heat equation; Fubini (Theorem 3) is behind every computation with independent variables.

Left out on purpose: the Radon–Nikodym theorem (conditional expectation will be built by orthogonal projection in \(L^2\) instead), the exercises, and the small remarks. They are all still in the full prérentrée course.

How to read a block: the statement first; then the short explanation, which tells you what is being done and why; then the deep dive, which explains the proof step by step when there is one, or unpacks a definition clause by clause; then the worked example. The compact proof at the bottom is the reference version, to reread once the deep dive has made it obvious. Every block has a chat if something is unclear.

KIND: definition
TITLE: Definition 1 (σ-algebras, generated σ-algebras, Borel sets — Forien Def. 1–3)
IMPORTANCE: not imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
Let \(E\) be a set. A family \(\mathcal{E} \subset \mathcal{P}(E)\) of subsets of \(E\) is a **\(\sigma\)-algebra** (or \(\sigma\)-field) on \(E\) if

- \(E \in \mathcal{E}\);
- \(A \in \mathcal{E}\) implies \(E \setminus A \in \mathcal{E}\);
- for every sequence \((A_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}\) of elements of \(\mathcal{E}\), \(\bigcup_n A_n \in \mathcal{E}\).

The sets of \(\mathcal{E}\) are the **measurable sets** and \((E, \mathcal{E})\) is a **measurable space**. An intersection of \(\sigma\)-algebras on \(E\) is again a \(\sigma\)-algebra, so every family \(\mathcal{F} \subset \mathcal{P}(E)\) is contained in a smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra, the **\(\sigma\)-algebra generated by** \(\mathcal{F}\):
\[
\sigma(\mathcal{F}) \;=\; \bigcap \big\{ \mathcal{E} : \mathcal{E} \text{ is a } \sigma\text{-algebra on } E,\ \mathcal{E} \supset \mathcal{F} \big\}.
\]
If \(E\) is a topological space with open sets \(\mathcal{O}\), the **Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra** is \(\mathcal{B}(E) = \sigma(\mathcal{O})\). The spaces \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(\mathbb{R}^d\) are always equipped with their Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra. On \(\mathbb{R}\), \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) is also generated by the half-lines \((-\infty, a]\), \(a \in \mathbb{R}\).
PROOF:
**Intersection of \(\sigma\)-algebras.** Let \(\mathcal{E} = \bigcap_{i \in I} \mathcal{E}_i\) with \(I \neq \varnothing\). \(E \in \mathcal{E}_i\) for all \(i\), so \(E \in \mathcal{E}\). If \(A \in \mathcal{E}\), then \(E \setminus A \in \mathcal{E}_i\) for every \(i\), so \(E \setminus A \in \mathcal{E}\). If \(A_n \in \mathcal{E}\) for all \(n\), then \(\bigcup_n A_n \in \mathcal{E}_i\) for every \(i\), so \(\bigcup_n A_n \in \mathcal{E}\). Since \(\mathcal{P}(E)\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{F}\), the intersection defining \(\sigma(\mathcal{F})\) is over a non-empty family; it is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{F}\) and contained in every other one.

**Half-lines generate \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\).** Let \(\mathcal{H} = \sigma(\{(-\infty, a] : a \in \mathbb{R}\})\). Each \((-\infty, a]\) is closed, hence Borel, so \(\mathcal{H} \subset \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\). Conversely \((a, b] = (-\infty, b] \setminus (-\infty, a] \in \mathcal{H}\), \((a,b) = \bigcup_n (a, b - 1/n] \in \mathcal{H}\), and every open set \(O\) is the union of the intervals \((p,q) \subset O\) with \(p, q \in \mathbb{Q}\), a countable union; so \(O \in \mathcal{H}\) and \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}) \subset \mathcal{H}\). \(\square\)
REFRESH:
(none)
EXPLANATION:
This block fixes the objects on which probabilities will be defined: a \(\sigma\)-algebra is the list of sets to which we will be allowed to assign a mass. The three rules are the minimum needed to make the questions asked in probability legitimate: "\(A\) happens", "\(A\) does not happen", "at least one of the \(A_n\) happens", for countably many \(A_n\). We almost never describe a \(\sigma\)-algebra explicitly; we describe a small generating family (intervals, open sets, rectangles) and take the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra containing it. Everything then reduces to checking properties on the generating family, which is the job of the monotone class lemma below.
DEEP:
The goal is to understand why these three rules and not others, and why generated \(\sigma\)-algebras make sense.

**Why countable unions.** Finite unions would not be enough: to write "\(X_n \to X\)" as a set, \(\{\omega : \forall k\ \exists N\ \forall n \ge N,\ |X_n(\omega) - X(\omega)| < 1/k\}\), you need countable intersections and unions. Uncountable unions would be too much: with them every subset of \([0,1]\) would be measurable (a union of singletons), and no measure with \(\lambda([a,b]) = b - a\) exists on all subsets of \([0,1]\) (Vitali's construction, outside these notes). Countable is exactly the scale at which limits live and measures survive. The rules also give countable intersections, since \(\bigcap_n A_n = E \setminus \bigcup_n (E \setminus A_n)\), and the empty set \(\varnothing = E \setminus E\).

**Why the intersection of \(\sigma\)-algebras is a \(\sigma\)-algebra.** Let \((\mathcal{E}_i)_{i \in I}\) be \(\sigma\)-algebras on \(E\) and \(\mathcal{E} = \bigcap_i \mathcal{E}_i\). Each rule is checked index by index: \(E\) belongs to every \(\mathcal{E}_i\), hence to \(\mathcal{E}\); if \(A \in \mathcal{E}\) then \(A \in \mathcal{E}_i\) for all \(i\), so \(E \setminus A \in \mathcal{E}_i\) for all \(i\), so \(E \setminus A \in \mathcal{E}\); the same argument works for a countable union. This is what makes \(\sigma(\mathcal{F})\) well defined: the family of \(\sigma\)-algebras containing \(\mathcal{F}\) is non-empty (\(\mathcal{P}(E)\) is one) and their intersection is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{F}\), contained in every other one. Warning: \(\sigma(\mathcal{F})\) is defined from the outside, as an intersection; there is no formula listing its elements, which is why one always works with generating families rather than with the \(\sigma\)-algebra itself.

**Why the half-lines generate \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\).** Every open set of \(\mathbb{R}\) is a countable union of intervals \((p,q)\) with rational endpoints, and \((p,q) = \bigcup_n (-\infty, q - 1/n] \setminus (-\infty, p]\), so open sets belong to \(\sigma(\{(-\infty, a]\})\); conversely \((-\infty, a]\) is closed, hence Borel. The half-lines are stable under finite intersections, \((-\infty, a] \cap (-\infty, b] = (-\infty, a \wedge b]\), and this is the reason a law on \(\mathbb{R}\) is determined by its distribution function \(x \mapsto \mathbb{P}(X \le x)\) (Proposition 1 below).

The interpretation to keep for the rest of the course: a \(\sigma\)-algebra is an amount of information, the list of questions that can be answered. In Hoffmann's Chapter 2 the \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(B_s, s \le t)\) is "what has been observed up to time \(t\)".
EXAMPLE:
On \(E = \{1,2,3\}\) the family \(\mathcal{F} = \{\{1\}\}\) generates \(\sigma(\mathcal{F}) = \{\varnothing, \{1\}, \{2,3\}, E\}\): one must add \(E\), the complement \(\{2,3\}\) and \(\varnothing\), and then nothing more is forced, since unions of these sets stay in the list. This \(\sigma\)-algebra answers exactly one question, "is the outcome \(1\)?"; it cannot distinguish \(2\) from \(3\). On \(\mathbb{R}\), a singleton \(\{x\} = \bigcap_n (x - 1/n, x + 1/n)\) is Borel, so every countable set is Borel, and so is the set of irrationals, its complement.

KIND: definition
TITLE: Definition 2 (Measurable maps, measures, null sets — Forien Def. 4–6, Ex. 2)
IMPORTANCE: half imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
Let \((E, \mathcal{E})\) and \((F, \mathcal{F})\) be measurable spaces.

- A map \(f : E \to F\) is **measurable** if \(f^{-1}(A) \in \mathcal{E}\) for every \(A \in \mathcal{F}\). It is enough to check this for \(A\) in a family generating \(\mathcal{F}\); for \(F = \mathbb{R}\), it is enough that \(\{f \le a\} \in \mathcal{E}\) for every \(a \in \mathbb{R}\).
- A **measure** on \((E, \mathcal{E})\) is a map \(\mu : \mathcal{E} \to [0, +\infty]\) with \(\mu(\varnothing) = 0\) which is **\(\sigma\)-additive**: for pairwise disjoint \(A_n \in \mathcal{E}\), \(\mu\big(\bigcup_n A_n\big) = \sum_n \mu(A_n)\). The triple \((E, \mathcal{E}, \mu)\) is a **measured space**. The measure is **finite** if \(\mu(E) < \infty\), a **probability measure** if \(\mu(E) = 1\), and **\(\sigma\)-finite** if \(E = \bigcup_n E_n\) with \(E_n \in \mathcal{E}\) and \(\mu(E_n) < \infty\).
- A property holds **\(\mu\)-almost everywhere** (a.e.) if it holds outside a set \(N \in \mathcal{E}\) with \(\mu(N) = 0\); such an \(N\) is a **null set**.

Examples: the **Dirac mass** \(\delta_x(A) = \mathbf{1}_{x \in A}\); the **counting measure** \(A \mapsto \mathrm{card}(A)\); the **Lebesgue measure** \(\lambda\) on \((\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))\), the unique measure with \(\lambda([a,b]) = b - a\) for all \(a \le b\) (existence is admitted, uniqueness follows from Proposition 1); given a measure \(\mu\) and a measurable \(f \ge 0\), the measure **with density \(f\) with respect to \(\mu\)**, \(\nu(A) = \int_A f\, d\mu\) (the integral is defined in Definition/Proposition 2).

Every measure satisfies: **monotonicity** (\(A \subset B \Rightarrow \mu(A) \le \mu(B)\)); **continuity from below** (\(A_n \uparrow A \Rightarrow \mu(A_n) \uparrow \mu(A)\)); **continuity from above** (\(A_n \downarrow A\) and \(\mu(A_1) < \infty \Rightarrow \mu(A_n) \downarrow \mu(A)\)); **countable subadditivity** (\(\mu(\bigcup_n A_n) \le \sum_n \mu(A_n)\)). In particular a countable union of null sets is null.
PROOF:
**Monotonicity.** For \(A \subset B\), \(\sigma\)-additivity with \(A_1 = A\), \(A_2 = B \setminus A\), \(A_n = \varnothing\) (\(n \ge 3\)) gives \(\mu(B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B \setminus A) \ge \mu(A)\).

**Continuity from below.** If \(A_n \uparrow A\), put \(D_1 = A_1\), \(D_n = A_n \setminus A_{n-1}\); the \(D_n\) are disjoint, \(A_N = \bigsqcup_{n \le N} D_n\) and \(A = \bigsqcup_n D_n\), so \(\mu(A) = \sum_n \mu(D_n) = \lim_N \mu(A_N)\).

**Continuity from above.** If \(A_n \downarrow A\) and \(\mu(A_1) < \infty\), then \(A_1 \setminus A_n \uparrow A_1 \setminus A\), so \(\mu(A_1) - \mu(A_n) \to \mu(A_1) - \mu(A)\), all terms being finite.

**Subadditivity.** \(B_n = A_n \setminus (A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_{n-1})\) are disjoint with \(B_n \subset A_n\) and \(\bigcup_n B_n = \bigcup_n A_n\), hence \(\mu(\bigcup_n A_n) = \sum_n \mu(B_n) \le \sum_n \mu(A_n)\).

**Measurability on generators.** If \(\mathcal{C}\) generates \(\mathcal{F}\) and \(f^{-1}(C) \in \mathcal{E}\) for all \(C \in \mathcal{C}\), the family \(\mathcal{G} = \{A \subset F : f^{-1}(A) \in \mathcal{E}\}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra (preimages commute with complements and countable unions) containing \(\mathcal{C}\), hence \(\mathcal{F}\). For \(F = \mathbb{R}\), the half-lines generate \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) (Definition 1). \(\square\)
REFRESH:
(none)
EXPLANATION:
Here we say what a measure is and what "measurable" means for a map, the two words used in every statement of the course. A measure assigns a mass to each measurable set, and the only rule is that mass adds up over countable disjoint unions; length, counting, point masses, densities and probabilities are all instances. Measurability of \(f\) means that any question about \(f(x)\), such as "is \(f(x) \le a\)?", translates into a measurable question about \(x\), so that \(\mu(f \le a)\) makes sense; that it suffices to test generating sets is what makes measurability checkable. The four properties at the end are the form in which \(\sigma\)-additivity is used in practice: a measure passes to monotone limits of sets.
DEEP:
The goal is to derive the working properties from \(\sigma\)-additivity alone, and to see why measurability only needs to be tested on generators.

**Step 1: monotonicity and finite additivity.** Taking \(A_1 = A\), \(A_2 = B \setminus A\) and \(A_n = \varnothing\) for \(n \ge 3\) in \(\sigma\)-additivity gives \(\mu(B) = \mu(A) + \mu(B \setminus A) \ge \mu(A)\) when \(A \subset B\). This is where \(\mu(\varnothing) = 0\) is needed: otherwise the infinitely many terms \(\mu(\varnothing)\) would add up to \(+\infty\).

**Step 2: continuity from below.** Let \(A_n \uparrow A\). Write \(A\) as a disjoint union of the rings \(D_1 = A_1\), \(D_n = A_n \setminus A_{n-1}\): then \(A_N = D_1 \sqcup \dots \sqcup D_N\) and \(A = \bigsqcup_n D_n\). By \(\sigma\)-additivity, \(\mu(A) = \sum_n \mu(D_n) = \lim_N \sum_{n \le N} \mu(D_n) = \lim_N \mu(A_N)\). No finiteness is needed.

**Step 3: continuity from above.** Let \(A_n \downarrow A\) with \(\mu(A_1) < \infty\). Then \(A_1 \setminus A_n \uparrow A_1 \setminus A\), and Step 2 gives \(\mu(A_1) - \mu(A_n) \to \mu(A_1) - \mu(A)\); subtracting is allowed because all quantities are bounded by \(\mu(A_1) < \infty\). The finiteness hypothesis cannot be dropped: for the counting measure on \(\mathbb{N}\), \(A_n = \{n, n+1, \dots\}\) decreases to \(\varnothing\) while \(\mu(A_n) = \infty\) for every \(n\).

**Step 4: subadditivity.** Put \(B_n = A_n \setminus (A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_{n-1})\); the \(B_n\) are disjoint, \(B_n \subset A_n\) and \(\bigcup_n B_n = \bigcup_n A_n\), so \(\mu(\bigcup_n A_n) = \sum_n \mu(B_n) \le \sum_n \mu(A_n)\). Consequence: a countable union of null sets is null, which is used every time we say "almost surely, for all \(n\)".

**Step 5: measurability on generators.** Let \(\mathcal{C}\) generate \(\mathcal{F}\) and suppose \(f^{-1}(C) \in \mathcal{E}\) for all \(C \in \mathcal{C}\). The family \(\mathcal{G} = \{A \subset F : f^{-1}(A) \in \mathcal{E}\}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(F\), because preimages commute with set operations: \(f^{-1}(F \setminus A) = E \setminus f^{-1}(A)\) and \(f^{-1}(\bigcup_n A_n) = \bigcup_n f^{-1}(A_n)\). It contains \(\mathcal{C}\), hence \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}) = \mathcal{F}\). For a real function, measurability therefore means \(\{f \le a\} \in \mathcal{E}\) for every \(a\); this is why sums, products, \(\sup_n\), \(\liminf_n\) and limits of measurable functions are measurable (for instance \(\{\sup_n f_n \le a\} = \bigcap_n \{f_n \le a\}\)), and why continuous maps between topological spaces are Borel measurable: preimages of open sets are open.

Used next: continuity from below is the engine of Proposition 1 and of the monotone convergence theorem; "a.e." is the probabilistic "almost surely". In Hoffmann's Chapter 1, continuity of Brownian paths is a statement outside a null set, and Kolmogorov's criterion builds precisely that null set.
EXAMPLE:
Take \(\lambda\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(A_n = (0, 1/n]\). The sets decrease to \(\varnothing\) and \(\lambda(A_n) = 1/n \to 0 = \lambda(\varnothing)\), as continuity from above predicts since \(\lambda(A_1) = 1 < \infty\). The set \(\mathbb{Q}\) is a countable union of singletons, each of Lebesgue measure \(\lambda(\{x\}) = \lim_n \lambda([x, x + 1/n]) = 0\), so \(\lambda(\mathbb{Q}) = 0\): almost every real number is irrational. Measurability: \(f = \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}\) is Borel since \(\{f \le a\}\) is \(\varnothing\), \(\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}\) or \(\mathbb{R}\) according to \(a < 0\), \(0 \le a < 1\), \(a \ge 1\); and \(f = 0\) Lebesgue-a.e.

KIND: theorem
TITLE: Theorem 1 (Monotone class lemma, set and function versions — Forien Def. 7–8, Thm 1)
IMPORTANCE: half imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
A family \(\mathcal{M} \subset \mathcal{P}(E)\) is a **monotone class** if \(E \in \mathcal{M}\); if \(A, B \in \mathcal{M}\) with \(A \subset B\) implies \(B \setminus A \in \mathcal{M}\); and if \(A_n \in \mathcal{M}\), \(A_n \uparrow A\) implies \(A \in \mathcal{M}\). Intersections of monotone classes are monotone classes, so every \(\mathcal{F} \subset \mathcal{P}(E)\) is contained in a smallest monotone class \(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{F})\). A family \(\mathcal{C}\) is a **\(\pi\)-system** if it is stable under finite intersections.

**(Set version.)** If \(\mathcal{C}\) is a \(\pi\)-system, then \(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}) = \sigma(\mathcal{C})\). Equivalently: a monotone class containing a \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal{C}\) contains \(\sigma(\mathcal{C})\).

**(Function version.)** Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be a \(\pi\)-system on \(E\) and \(\mathcal{H}\) a vector space of bounded real functions on \(E\) such that \(\mathbf{1}_E \in \mathcal{H}\), \(\mathbf{1}_C \in \mathcal{H}\) for every \(C \in \mathcal{C}\), and \(\mathcal{H}\) is stable under bounded increasing limits (if \(0 \le h_n \in \mathcal{H}\) and \(h_n \uparrow h\) with \(h\) bounded, then \(h \in \mathcal{H}\)). Then \(\mathcal{H}\) contains every bounded \(\sigma(\mathcal{C})\)-measurable function.
PROOF:
Write \(\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})\). A \(\sigma\)-algebra is a monotone class, so \(\mathcal{M} \subset \sigma(\mathcal{C})\). A monotone class stable under finite intersections is a \(\sigma\)-algebra: it contains \(E\) and complements \(E \setminus A\), hence finite unions \(A \cup B = E \setminus ((E \setminus A) \cap (E \setminus B))\), hence countable unions \(\bigcup_n A_n = \bigcup_n (A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_n)\) as increasing unions. It remains to show \(\mathcal{M}\) is stable under finite intersections.

**Step 1.** \(\mathcal{M}_1 = \{A \in \mathcal{M} : A \cap C \in \mathcal{M}\ \forall C \in \mathcal{C}\}\) is a monotone class (\(E \cap C = C\); \((B \setminus A) \cap C = (B \cap C) \setminus (A \cap C)\); \(A_n \cap C \uparrow A \cap C\)) containing \(\mathcal{C}\) because \(\mathcal{C}\) is a \(\pi\)-system. By minimality \(\mathcal{M}_1 = \mathcal{M}\).

**Step 2.** \(\mathcal{M}_2 = \{A \in \mathcal{M} : A \cap B \in \mathcal{M}\ \forall B \in \mathcal{M}\}\) is a monotone class by the same checks, and contains \(\mathcal{C}\) by Step 1. By minimality \(\mathcal{M}_2 = \mathcal{M}\), so \(\mathcal{M}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing \(\mathcal{C}\), and \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}) \subset \mathcal{M}\).

**Function version.** \(\mathcal{D} = \{A \in \sigma(\mathcal{C}) : \mathbf{1}_A \in \mathcal{H}\}\) is a monotone class (\(\mathbf{1}_{B \setminus A} = \mathbf{1}_B - \mathbf{1}_A\), \(\mathbf{1}_{A_n} \uparrow \mathbf{1}_A\)) containing \(\mathcal{C}\), so \(\mathcal{D} = \sigma(\mathcal{C})\). A bounded \(\sigma(\mathcal{C})\)-measurable \(h \ge 0\) is the bounded increasing limit of the simple functions \(2^{-n}\lfloor 2^n h \rfloor\), finite linear combinations of indicators of sets of \(\mathcal{D}\); so \(h \in \mathcal{H}\), and \(h = h^+ - h^-\) in general. \(\square\)
REFRESH:
(none)
EXPLANATION:
This is the tool that turns "true on a small generating family" into "true on the whole \(\sigma\)-algebra". A \(\sigma\)-algebra has no explicit description, so one cannot check a property set by set; instead one shows that the sets having the property form a monotone class (three easy rules, typically verified with the continuity of measures) and that it contains a generating \(\pi\)-system. The theorem then says the property holds on all of \(\sigma(\mathcal{C})\). The function version does the same for identities between integrals: check them on indicators of a \(\pi\)-system, get them for all bounded measurable functions. The proof is a double bootstrap: one shows that \(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})\) is stable under intersection, first with elements of \(\mathcal{C}\), then with itself.
DEEP:
We want to show \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}) \subset \mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})\) when \(\mathcal{C}\) is a \(\pi\)-system; the other inclusion is free. Plan: prove that \(\mathcal{M} := \mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C})\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra; since it contains \(\mathcal{C}\), it then contains \(\sigma(\mathcal{C})\).

**Step 0: the easy inclusion and the reduction.** A \(\sigma\)-algebra is a monotone class (it contains \(E\), differences \(B \setminus A = B \cap (E \setminus A)\), and increasing unions), so \(\sigma(\mathcal{C})\) is a monotone class containing \(\mathcal{C}\) and \(\mathcal{M} \subset \sigma(\mathcal{C})\) by minimality. For the converse, note what a monotone class already has: \(E\), complements (\(E \setminus A\) is a difference), increasing unions. If it is also stable under finite intersections, then it has finite unions, \(A \cup B = E \setminus ((E \setminus A) \cap (E \setminus B))\), and countable unions, \(\bigcup_n A_n = \bigcup_n (A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_n)\), an increasing union. So everything reduces to one thing: \(\mathcal{M}\) is stable under finite intersections.

**Step 1: intersecting with elements of \(\mathcal{C}\).** Let \(\mathcal{M}_1 = \{A \in \mathcal{M} : A \cap C \in \mathcal{M} \text{ for all } C \in \mathcal{C}\}\). It is a monotone class: \(E \cap C = C \in \mathcal{M}\); if \(A \subset B\) are in \(\mathcal{M}_1\), then \((B \setminus A) \cap C = (B \cap C) \setminus (A \cap C)\) is a difference of two nested elements of \(\mathcal{M}\); if \(A_n \uparrow A\) in \(\mathcal{M}_1\), then \(A_n \cap C \uparrow A \cap C\). It contains \(\mathcal{C}\) precisely because \(\mathcal{C}\) is a \(\pi\)-system. By minimality \(\mathcal{M} \subset \mathcal{M}_1\): \(A \cap C \in \mathcal{M}\) for all \(A \in \mathcal{M}\), \(C \in \mathcal{C}\).

**Step 2: intersecting with elements of \(\mathcal{M}\).** Let \(\mathcal{M}_2 = \{A \in \mathcal{M} : A \cap B \in \mathcal{M} \text{ for all } B \in \mathcal{M}\}\). The same three verifications show it is a monotone class, and Step 1 says exactly that \(\mathcal{C} \subset \mathcal{M}_2\). By minimality \(\mathcal{M}_2 = \mathcal{M}\): \(\mathcal{M}\) is stable under finite intersections, hence a \(\sigma\)-algebra by Step 0, and \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}) \subset \mathcal{M}\).

**Step 3: the function version.** Let \(\mathcal{D} = \{A \in \sigma(\mathcal{C}) : \mathbf{1}_A \in \mathcal{H}\}\). It is a monotone class: \(\mathbf{1}_E \in \mathcal{H}\); for \(A \subset B\), \(\mathbf{1}_{B \setminus A} = \mathbf{1}_B - \mathbf{1}_A\) and \(\mathcal{H}\) is a vector space; for \(A_n \uparrow A\), \(\mathbf{1}_{A_n} \uparrow \mathbf{1}_A\), bounded by \(1\). It contains \(\mathcal{C}\), so \(\mathcal{D} = \sigma(\mathcal{C})\) by the set version. Next, a bounded \(\sigma(\mathcal{C})\)-measurable \(h \ge 0\) is the increasing limit of the simple functions \(h_n = 2^{-n}\lfloor 2^n h \rfloor\), each a finite linear combination of indicators of sets of \(\mathcal{D}\), hence in \(\mathcal{H}\); by stability under bounded increasing limits, \(h \in \mathcal{H}\). A general bounded \(h\) is \(h^+ - h^-\).

**Where the hypothesis bites.** Without the \(\pi\)-system property the conclusion fails: on \(E = \{1,2,3,4\}\), \(\mathcal{C} = \{\{1,2\}, \{2,3\}\}\) is not a \(\pi\)-system, \(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}) = \{\varnothing, E, \{1,2\}, \{3,4\}, \{2,3\}, \{1,4\}\}\) is not a \(\sigma\)-algebra (it misses \(\{2\}\)), while \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}) = \mathcal{P}(E)\).

Used next: Proposition 1 is the set version applied to \(\{A : \mu(A) = \nu(A)\}\). In Hoffmann's Chapter 2 the function version, applied to \(\mathcal{H} = \{Z \text{ bounded} : \mathbb{E}[Z \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = \mathbb{E}[Z \mid \sigma(B_t)]\}\) and to the \(\pi\)-system of sets \(\{B_{t_1} \in A_1, \dots, B_{t_n} \in A_n\}\), is what closes the proof of the Markov property.
EXAMPLE:
Let \(\mu, \nu\) be probability measures on \(\mathbb{R}\) with \(\int f\, d\mu = \int f\, d\nu\) for every bounded continuous \(f\). Do they coincide? Let \(\mathcal{H}\) be the set of bounded Borel \(h\) with \(\int h\, d\mu = \int h\, d\nu\): a vector space containing the constants, stable under bounded increasing limits by monotone convergence on both sides. It contains \(\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty, a]}\): the continuous functions \(f_n(x) = \min(1, \max(0, n(a + 1/n - x)))\) decrease to it, so \(1 - f_n \uparrow 1 - \mathbf{1}_{(-\infty, a]}\) and \(\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,a]} \in \mathcal{H}\). Half-lines form a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), so \(\mathcal{H}\) contains all bounded Borel functions, in particular all indicators: \(\mu = \nu\). This is why convergence in law can be tested on bounded continuous functions only.

KIND: proposition
TITLE: Proposition 1 (Two measures agreeing on a π-system are equal — Forien Prop. 2)
IMPORTANCE: imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
Let \(\mu, \nu\) be measures on \((E, \mathcal{E})\) and let \(\mathcal{C} \subset \mathcal{E}\) be a \(\pi\)-system with \(\sigma(\mathcal{C}) = \mathcal{E}\) and \(\mu(A) = \nu(A)\) for all \(A \in \mathcal{C}\). Assume that either \(\mu(E) = \nu(E) < \infty\) (automatic for two probability measures), or that there is a sequence \(E_n \in \mathcal{C}\) with \(\bigcup_n E_n = E\) and \(\mu(E_n) = \nu(E_n) < \infty\). Then \(\mu = \nu\) on \(\mathcal{E}\).

Consequences: a probability measure on \(\mathbb{R}\) is determined by its values on the half-lines \((-\infty, a]\); a probability measure on \(\mathbb{R}^d\) by its values on the products \(\prod_{i=1}^d (-\infty, a_i]\); the Lebesgue measure is unique.
PROOF:
**Finite case.** Let \(\mathcal{M} = \{A \in \mathcal{E} : \mu(A) = \nu(A)\}\). \(E \in \mathcal{M}\) by hypothesis. If \(A \subset B\) are in \(\mathcal{M}\), then \(\mu(B \setminus A) = \mu(B) - \mu(A) = \nu(B) - \nu(A) = \nu(B \setminus A)\), the subtraction being legitimate since \(\mu(A) \le \mu(E) < \infty\). If \(A_n \uparrow A\) with \(A_n \in \mathcal{M}\), continuity from below gives \(\mu(A) = \lim \mu(A_n) = \lim \nu(A_n) = \nu(A)\). So \(\mathcal{M}\) is a monotone class containing the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal{C}\), and \(\mathcal{M} \supset \sigma(\mathcal{C}) = \mathcal{E}\) by Theorem 1.

**Localised case.** For \(G \in \mathcal{C}\) with \(\mu(G) = \nu(G) < \infty\), the finite measures \(A \mapsto \mu(A \cap G)\) and \(A \mapsto \nu(A \cap G)\) have equal total mass and agree on \(\mathcal{C}\) (as \(A \cap G \in \mathcal{C}\)), so by the finite case \(\mu(A \cap G) = \nu(A \cap G)\) for all \(A \in \mathcal{E}\). With \(F_n = E_1 \cup \dots \cup E_n \uparrow E\), inclusion–exclusion writes \(\mu(A \cap F_n)\) as a finite signed sum of terms \(\mu(A \cap E_{i_1} \cap \dots \cap E_{i_k})\), each \(E_{i_1} \cap \dots \cap E_{i_k} \in \mathcal{C}\) having finite equal masses; so \(\mu(A \cap F_n) = \nu(A \cap F_n)\), and continuity from below along \(A \cap F_n \uparrow A\) gives \(\mu(A) = \nu(A)\).

**Consequences.** Half-lines (resp. products of half-lines) form a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) (resp. \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d)\)), and probability measures have equal total mass. For Lebesgue measure, take \(\mathcal{C}\) the bounded intervals \([a,b]\) and \(E_n = [-n, n]\). \(\square\)
REFRESH:
**Monotone class** (Theorem 1): a family containing \(E\), stable under nested differences \(B \setminus A\) and increasing unions; if it contains a \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal{C}\), it contains \(\sigma(\mathcal{C})\).
EXPLANATION:
This proposition tells you when two measures are the same without checking every measurable set: it is enough that they agree on a family stable under intersection that generates the \(\sigma\)-algebra, plus a finiteness condition. Laws of random variables are probability measures, so this is the statement behind "the law of \(X\) is determined by its distribution function", "the law of a random vector is determined by rectangles", and, in Hoffmann's Chapter 1, "the law of a process is determined by its finite-dimensional marginals". The proof is one application of Theorem 1: the sets on which \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) agree form a monotone class containing \(\mathcal{C}\). Finiteness is needed to subtract the measures of nested sets.
DEEP:
We want \(\mu(A) = \nu(A)\) for every \(A \in \mathcal{E}\). Plan: the finite case by the monotone class lemma, then the localised case.

**Step 1: the finite case.** Assume \(\mu(E) = \nu(E) < \infty\) and let \(\mathcal{M} = \{A \in \mathcal{E} : \mu(A) = \nu(A)\}\). Three checks. \(E \in \mathcal{M}\) by hypothesis. If \(A \subset B\) are in \(\mathcal{M}\), then \(\mu(B \setminus A) = \mu(B) - \mu(A) = \nu(B) - \nu(A) = \nu(B \setminus A)\); the subtraction is legitimate because \(\mu(A) \le \mu(E) < \infty\), and this is the only place where finiteness is used. If \(A_n \uparrow A\) with \(A_n \in \mathcal{M}\), continuity from below gives \(\mu(A) = \lim \mu(A_n) = \lim \nu(A_n) = \nu(A)\). So \(\mathcal{M}\) is a monotone class containing the \(\pi\)-system \(\mathcal{C}\), and Theorem 1 gives \(\mathcal{M} \supset \sigma(\mathcal{C}) = \mathcal{E}\).

**Step 2: localising on a set of finite mass.** In the second situation, fix \(G \in \mathcal{C}\) with \(\mu(G) = \nu(G) < \infty\). The maps \(A \mapsto \mu(A \cap G)\) and \(A \mapsto \nu(A \cap G)\) are finite measures with the same total mass \(\mu(G)\); they agree on \(\mathcal{C}\) because \(A \cap G \in \mathcal{C}\) for \(A \in \mathcal{C}\), which is where the \(\pi\)-system property is used a second time. Step 1 applied to them gives \(\mu(A \cap G) = \nu(A \cap G)\) for all \(A \in \mathcal{E}\).

**Step 3: exhausting \(E\).** With \(F_n = E_1 \cup \dots \cup E_n \uparrow E\), inclusion–exclusion expresses \(\mu(A \cap F_n)\) as a finite signed sum of terms \(\mu(A \cap E_{i_1} \cap \dots \cap E_{i_k})\); each \(E_{i_1} \cap \dots \cap E_{i_k}\) is in \(\mathcal{C}\) with finite equal masses, so Step 2 applies to each term and \(\mu(A \cap F_n) = \nu(A \cap F_n)\). Continuity from below along \(A \cap F_n \uparrow A\) concludes. If the \(E_n\) are increasing, the usual case, Step 3 is just Step 2 with \(G = E_n\) and \(n \to \infty\).

**The consequences.** The half-lines form a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\) (Definition 1), and two probability measures have the same total mass: this gives the distribution function statement. The same holds for products of half-lines in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), whose intersections are again such products. For Lebesgue measure, take \(\mathcal{C}\) the bounded intervals \([a,b]\), a \(\pi\)-system, and \(E_n = [-n, n]\).

**Where the hypotheses bite.** \(\sigma\)-finiteness of \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) is not enough: the sets of finite mass must lie in \(\mathcal{C}\). On \(E = \mathbb{R}\) with \(\mathcal{C} = \{(a, +\infty) : a \in \mathbb{R}\}\), a \(\pi\)-system generating \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\), the counting measure and twice the counting measure agree on \(\mathcal{C}\) (both are \(+\infty\) there) but differ.

Used next: uniqueness of the product measure (Theorem 3); the characterisation of laws by characteristic functions (Lesson 2); the statement that the law of a Gaussian process is determined by its mean and covariance (Lesson 5, and Hoffmann's Example 6).
EXAMPLE:
Let \(X\) be a random variable with \(\mathbb{P}(X \le a) = 1 - e^{-a}\) for \(a \ge 0\) and \(0\) for \(a < 0\). Then the law of \(X\) is the exponential law of parameter \(1\): \(\mathbb{P}(X \in A) = \int_A e^{-x}\mathbf{1}_{x \ge 0}\, dx\) for every Borel \(A\). Indeed both sides are probability measures on \(\mathbb{R}\) (the right-hand side has total mass \(\int_0^\infty e^{-x}dx = 1\)) and they agree on every half-line, since \(\int_{-\infty}^a e^{-x}\mathbf{1}_{x \ge 0}\, dx = 1 - e^{-a}\) for \(a \ge 0\). No other set needs checking.

KIND: proposition
TITLE: Definition/Proposition 2 (The integral — Forien Prop./Def. 1)
IMPORTANCE: half imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
Let \((E, \mathcal{E}, \mu)\) be a measured space. There is a unique way to assign to every measurable \(f : E \to [0, +\infty]\) a number \(\int_E f\, d\mu \in [0, +\infty]\) (also written \(\int f(x)\, \mu(dx)\)) such that

- \(\int \mathbf{1}_A\, d\mu = \mu(A)\) for \(A \in \mathcal{E}\);
- (linearity) \(\int (af + bg)\, d\mu = a\int f\, d\mu + b\int g\, d\mu\) for \(a, b \ge 0\);
- (monotone convergence) if \(0 \le f_n \uparrow f\) pointwise, then \(\int f_n\, d\mu \uparrow \int f\, d\mu\).

It is monotone (\(f \le g \Rightarrow \int f\, d\mu \le \int g\, d\mu\)), and \(\int f\, d\mu = 0\) if and only if \(f = 0\) \(\mu\)-a.e. A measurable \(f : E \to \mathbb{R}\) (or \(\mathbb{C}\)) is **integrable** if \(\int |f|\, d\mu < \infty\); one then sets \(\int f\, d\mu := \int f^+\, d\mu - \int f^-\, d\mu\) with \(f^\pm = \max(\pm f, 0)\) (real and imaginary parts separately in the complex case), the integral is linear on integrable functions and \(\big|\int f\, d\mu\big| \le \int |f|\, d\mu\). For \(A \in \mathcal{E}\), \(\int_A f\, d\mu := \int f\mathbf{1}_A\, d\mu\). Two functions equal \(\mu\)-a.e. have the same integral.
PROOF:
**Simple functions.** For \(s = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i}\) with \(a_i \ge 0\) and \((A_i)\) a measurable partition of \(E\), the first two bullets force \(\int s\, d\mu = \sum_i a_i\mu(A_i)\); this is independent of the representation (pass to the common refinement of two partitions and use finite additivity), linear, and monotone.

**Definition.** For measurable \(f \ge 0\), the simple functions \(f_n = \min(2^{-n}\lfloor 2^n f\rfloor, n)\) increase pointwise to \(f\); set \(\int f\, d\mu = \lim_n \int f_n\, d\mu\).

**Independence of the approximation.** If \(s_n\) simple increase to \(f\) and \(t = \sum_i a_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i} \le f\) is simple, then for \(\varepsilon \in (0,1)\) the sets \(B_n = \{s_n \ge (1-\varepsilon)t\}\) increase to \(E\), and \(\int s_n\, d\mu \ge (1-\varepsilon)\sum_i a_i\mu(A_i \cap B_n) \to (1-\varepsilon)\int t\, d\mu\) by continuity from below; so \(\lim_n \int s_n\, d\mu \ge \int t\, d\mu\). Applied in both directions to two approximating sequences, this shows the limits agree and that \(\int f\, d\mu = \sup\{\int s\, d\mu : s \text{ simple}, 0 \le s \le f\}\).

**Properties.** Linearity: \(af_n + bg_n\) are simple and increase to \(af + bg\). Monotone convergence: if \(0 \le g_n \uparrow g\), then \(\lim \int g_n \le \int g\) by monotonicity, and for simple \(t \le g\) the previous argument with \(s_n\) replaced by \(g_n\) gives \(\lim \int g_n \ge \int t\); take the supremum over \(t\). Monotonicity follows from the supremum formula. If \(\int f = 0\), then \(\mu(f > 1/k) \le k\int f = 0\) for all \(k\), so \(f = 0\) a.e.; conversely if \(f = 0\) a.e., every simple \(s \le f\) has integral \(0\). Uniqueness: the three bullets determine the integral on simple functions, then on all \(f \ge 0\) through \(f_n \uparrow f\).

**Integrable functions.** \(\int f^\pm \le \int |f| < \infty\), so the difference is defined; linearity follows from \((f+g)^+ - (f+g)^- = f^+ - f^- + g^+ - g^-\), rearranged with all terms finite; and \(|\int f| \le \int f^+ + \int f^- = \int |f|\). \(\square\)
REFRESH:
(none)
EXPLANATION:
We are building the integral against an arbitrary measure, the object that will become the expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_\Omega X\, d\mathbb{P}\). The three bullets are the whole user manual: indicators integrate to measures, the integral is linear, and it passes to increasing limits. Every proof of an identity valid "for all measurable \(f \ge 0\)" runs the same machine in this order: indicators, then simple functions by linearity, then non-negative functions by monotone convergence, then integrable functions by \(f = f^+ - f^-\). The construction itself follows this route: define the integral of \(\sum_i a_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i}\) as \(\sum_i a_i\mu(A_i)\), take the limit along increasing simple approximations of \(f\), and check that the limit does not depend on the approximation.
DEEP:
The goal is to see how the three bullets force a definition and why it is consistent. Plan: simple functions, then approximation, then the supremum formula that makes everything independent of choices.

**Step 1: simple functions.** A simple function is \(s = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i}\) with \(a_i \ge 0\) and \(A_i \in \mathcal{E}\) forming a partition of \(E\). The first two bullets leave no choice: \(\int s\, d\mu = \sum_i a_i\mu(A_i)\). This is well defined (two representations have a common refinement \(\{A_i \cap B_j\}\), and finite additivity of \(\mu\) gives the same number from both), linear, and monotone: if \(s \le t\), then on each cell of the common refinement of positive measure the coefficients compare.

**Step 2: approximation from below.** For measurable \(f \ge 0\) set \(f_n = \min(2^{-n}\lfloor 2^n f\rfloor, n)\). Each \(f_n\) is simple (the sets \(\{k2^{-n} \le f < (k+1)2^{-n}\}\) are measurable), \(f_n \le f_{n+1}\) (refining the grid can only raise the floor), and \(f_n(x) \uparrow f(x)\) for every \(x\), with \(0 \le f(x) - f_n(x) \le 2^{-n}\) once \(n > f(x)\). Define \(\int f\, d\mu = \lim_n \int f_n\, d\mu\), an increasing limit in \([0, +\infty]\).

**Step 3: the limit does not depend on the approximation.** Key lemma: if \(s_n\) are simple with \(s_n \uparrow f\) and \(t \le f\) is simple, then \(\lim_n \int s_n \ge \int t\). Write \(t = \sum_i a_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i}\), fix \(\varepsilon \in (0,1)\) and let \(B_n = \{s_n \ge (1-\varepsilon)t\}\). The \(B_n\) increase to \(E\): where \(t(x) > 0\), \(s_n(x) \to f(x) \ge t(x) > (1-\varepsilon)t(x)\) eventually. Then \(\int s_n \ge \int s_n\mathbf{1}_{B_n} \ge (1-\varepsilon)\sum_i a_i\mu(A_i \cap B_n)\), and continuity from below gives \(\mu(A_i \cap B_n) \uparrow \mu(A_i)\). Let \(n \to \infty\), then \(\varepsilon \to 0\). Applying the lemma in both directions to two approximating sequences shows they give the same limit, and yields the formula
\[
\int f\, d\mu = \sup\Big\{ \int s\, d\mu : s \text{ simple},\ 0 \le s \le f \Big\}.
\]

**Step 4: the properties.** Linearity: if \(f_n \uparrow f\) and \(g_n \uparrow g\) are the canonical approximations, \(af_n + bg_n\) are simple and increase to \(af + bg\), so by Step 3 the integral of the limit is the limit of the integrals. Monotone convergence: if \(0 \le g_n \uparrow g\), monotonicity gives \(\lim \int g_n \le \int g\); for the converse, fix a simple \(t \le g\) and run the key-lemma argument with \(s_n\) replaced by \(g_n\) (the sets \(B_n = \{g_n \ge (1-\varepsilon)t\}\) still increase to \(E\)) to get \(\lim \int g_n \ge \int t\); take the supremum over \(t\). Monotonicity comes from the supremum formula. Finally \(\int f = 0\) with \(f \ge 0\) forces \(\mu(f > 1/k) = 0\) for every \(k\), since \(\tfrac1k\mathbf{1}_{\{f > 1/k\}} \le f\), hence \(\mu(f > 0) = 0\).

**Step 5: integrable functions.** For real \(f\) with \(\int |f| < \infty\), both \(\int f^\pm \le \int |f|\) are finite, so \(\int f^+ - \int f^-\) makes sense, and \(|\int f| \le \int f^+ + \int f^- = \int |f|\). Note that the integral of a signed function is never defined by a limit of signed simple functions, which could oscillate; it is always the difference of two positive integrals.

Used next: Theorem 2, and the transfer formula \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)] = \int h\, d\mathcal{L}_X\) in Lesson 2, whose proof is exactly the four-step machine.
EXAMPLE:
Against the counting measure on \(\mathbb{N}\), a function is a sequence \((u_n)\) and \(\int u\, d\mu = \sum_n u_n\): integrable means absolutely summable. Against \(\delta_x\), \(\int f\, d\delta_x = f(x)\). Against Lebesgue measure, \(f = \mathbf{1}_{[0,1] \setminus \mathbb{Q}}\) has no Riemann integral, but \(\int f\, d\lambda = \lambda([0,1]) - \lambda(\mathbb{Q} \cap [0,1]) = 1\). The machine in action: for \(f(x) = x\) on \([0,1]\), the canonical approximations \(f_n = 2^{-n}\lfloor 2^n x\rfloor\) have integrals \(\sum_{k=0}^{2^n - 1} k2^{-n}\cdot 2^{-n} = \frac{2^n(2^n - 1)}{2\cdot 4^n} \to \frac12\).

KIND: theorem
TITLE: Theorem 2 (Monotone convergence, Fatou, dominated convergence — Forien Thm 2)
IMPORTANCE: imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
Let \((E, \mathcal{E}, \mu)\) be a measured space and \(f_n, f\) measurable functions.

1. **(Monotone convergence)** If \(f_n \ge 0\), \(f_n \le f_{n+1}\) and \(f_n \to f\) \(\mu\)-a.e., then \(\int f_n\, d\mu \to \int f\, d\mu\). In particular \(\int \sum_n g_n\, d\mu = \sum_n \int g_n\, d\mu\) for measurable \(g_n \ge 0\).
2. **(Fatou's lemma)** If \(f_n \ge 0\), then \(\int \liminf_n f_n\, d\mu \le \liminf_n \int f_n\, d\mu\).
3. **(Dominated convergence)** If \(f_n \to f\) \(\mu\)-a.e. and there is an integrable \(g\) with \(|f_n| \le g\) \(\mu\)-a.e. for every \(n\), then \(f\) is integrable, \(\int |f_n - f|\, d\mu \to 0\) and \(\int f_n\, d\mu \to \int f\, d\mu\).
PROOF:
Modifying the functions on the null set where the hypotheses fail changes no integral, so assume everything holds everywhere.

**1.** Established with the construction of the integral (Definition/Proposition 2). For the series, apply it to the partial sums \(S_N = \sum_{n \le N} g_n \uparrow \sum_n g_n\) and use linearity.

**2.** Let \(h_n = \inf_{k \ge n} f_k\). Then \(0 \le h_n \uparrow \liminf_k f_k\), so \(\int \liminf f_k = \lim_n \int h_n\) by 1. Since \(h_n \le f_k\) for all \(k \ge n\), \(\int h_n \le \inf_{k \ge n} \int f_k\), whose limit is \(\liminf_k \int f_k\).

**3.** From \(|f_n| \le g\) and \(f_n \to f\), \(|f| \le g\), so \(f\) and the \(f_n\) are integrable. The functions \(2g - |f_n - f| \ge 0\) converge to \(2g\); Fatou gives \(\int 2g \le \liminf_n \int (2g - |f_n - f|) = \int 2g - \limsup_n \int |f_n - f|\). As \(\int 2g < \infty\), cancel it: \(\limsup_n \int |f_n - f| \le 0\). Then \(|\int f_n - \int f| \le \int |f_n - f| \to 0\). \(\square\)
REFRESH:
**liminf** of a real sequence: \(\liminf_n u_n = \lim_n \inf_{k \ge n} u_k\), the smallest limit of a subsequence; \(\liminf_n f_n\) is taken pointwise.
EXPLANATION:
These three theorems answer one question: when can a limit be moved inside an integral, \(\lim_n \int f_n = \int \lim_n f_n\)? Monotone convergence says always, for non-decreasing non-negative sequences, even with infinite values. Dominated convergence says yes as soon as the whole sequence stays under one fixed integrable function. Fatou is the one-sided statement that survives with no hypothesis beyond positivity: mass can be lost in the limit but never created (a bump \(\mathbf{1}_{[n, n+1]}\) escaping to infinity has limit \(0\) while all its integrals equal \(1\)). The proof derives Fatou from monotone convergence by looking at \(\inf_{k \ge n} f_k\), then dominated convergence from Fatou applied to \(2g - |f_n - f|\).
DEEP:
We want to prove 2 and 3 from 1, which was established with the construction of the integral. A preliminary remark on "a.e.": modifying \(f_n\) and \(f\) on the null set where the hypotheses fail changes no integral, so we may assume everything holds everywhere (a countable union of null sets is null).

**Step 1: series of non-negative functions.** Apply 1 to the partial sums \(S_N = \sum_{n \le N} g_n\), which increase to \(\sum_n g_n\), and use linearity: \(\int S_N = \sum_{n \le N}\int g_n\).

**Step 2: Fatou from monotone convergence.** Set \(h_n = \inf_{k \ge n} f_k\). Each \(h_n\) is measurable, non-negative, and \(h_n \le h_{n+1}\) (an infimum over a smaller set is larger); by definition of the \(\liminf\), \(h_n \uparrow \liminf_k f_k\). Monotone convergence gives \(\int \liminf_k f_k = \lim_n \int h_n\). On the other hand \(h_n \le f_k\) for every \(k \ge n\), so \(\int h_n \le \inf_{k \ge n}\int f_k\), and as \(n \to \infty\) the right-hand side tends to \(\liminf_k \int f_k\). Hence \(\int \liminf f_k \le \liminf \int f_k\).

**Step 3: dominated convergence from Fatou.** From \(|f_n| \le g\) and \(f_n \to f\) we get \(|f| \le g\), so \(f\) and the \(f_n\) are integrable. The functions \(2g - |f_n - f|\) are non-negative, since \(|f_n - f| \le |f_n| + |f| \le 2g\), and converge to \(2g\). Fatou gives
\[
\int 2g \;\le\; \liminf_n \int \big(2g - |f_n - f|\big) \;=\; \int 2g \;-\; \limsup_n \int |f_n - f| .
\]
Because \(\int 2g < \infty\) we may cancel it, and this is exactly where integrability of the dominating function is used; we obtain \(\limsup_n \int |f_n - f| \le 0\). Then \(|\int f_n - \int f| \le \int |f_n - f| \to 0\).

**Where the hypotheses bite.** Monotonicity cannot be dropped in 1: \(f_n = \mathbf{1}_{[n, n+1]}\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) tends to \(0\) with \(\int f_n = 1\); the same example shows that Fatou can be strict and that dominated convergence fails without a dominating function, the smallest candidate \(\sup_n f_n = \mathbf{1}_{[0, \infty)}\) not being integrable. Positivity cannot be dropped in Fatou: \(f_n = -\mathbf{1}_{[n, n+1]}\) gives \(\int \liminf f_n = 0 > -1 = \liminf \int f_n\). Concentration is the other way to lose mass: \(f_n = n\mathbf{1}_{(0, 1/n)}\) tends to \(0\) pointwise with \(\int f_n = 1\).

**Two corollaries used constantly.** Bounded convergence: on a probability space, if \(X_n \to X\) a.s. and \(|X_n| \le C\), then \(\mathbb{E}[X_n] \to \mathbb{E}[X]\) (take \(g = C\), integrable because \(\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1\)). Fatou in probabilistic form: \(\mathbb{E}[\liminf X_n] \le \liminf \mathbb{E}[X_n]\) for \(X_n \ge 0\); this is how one shows that an a.s. limit of variables with bounded expectations is integrable (martingale convergence, Lesson 3).

Used next: Proposition 3 is dominated convergence applied to a continuous parameter; the modes-of-convergence proposition of Lesson 2 and Hoffmann's Proposition 3 on the semigroup (Chapter 2) invoke dominated convergence in every item.
EXAMPLE:
Let \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(X_n = \min(|Z|, n)\). Then \(0 \le X_n \uparrow |Z|\), so \(\mathbb{E}[X_n] \uparrow \mathbb{E}|Z| = \sqrt{2/\pi}\) by monotone convergence, with no need to know anything about the speed. Let \(Y_n = \sin(Z/n)\): \(Y_n \to 0\) pointwise and \(|Y_n| \le 1\), an integrable constant on a probability space, so \(\mathbb{E}[Y_n] \to 0\) by dominated convergence. Finally, \(\mathbb{E}[e^{tZ}] = \sum_k \frac{t^k}{k!}\mathbb{E}[Z^k]\): exchanging sum and expectation is legitimate because \(\sum_k \frac{|t|^k}{k!}\mathbb{E}|Z|^k = \mathbb{E}[e^{|t||Z|}] < \infty\) (monotone convergence for the series of absolute values, then dominated convergence for the signed partial sums).

KIND: proposition
TITLE: Proposition 3 (Continuity and differentiation under the integral sign — Forien Prop. 3–4)
IMPORTANCE: imp
HIGHLIGHT: 0
STATEMENT:
Let \((E, \mathcal{E}, \mu)\) be a measured space, \(F\) a metric space, and \(f : E \times F \to \mathbb{R}\) such that for every \(y \in F\) the map \(x \mapsto f(x,y)\) is measurable and integrable. Set \(h(y) = \int_E f(x,y)\, \mu(dx)\).

1. **(Continuity)** If for \(\mu\)-a.e. \(x\) the map \(y \mapsto f(x,y)\) is continuous, and if there is an integrable \(g\) with \(|f(x,y)| \le g(x)\) for all \(y \in F\) and \(\mu\)-a.e. \(x\), then \(h\) is continuous on \(F\).
2. **(Differentiation)** If \(F = I\) is an interval of \(\mathbb{R}\), if for \(\mu\)-a.e. \(x\) the map \(y \mapsto f(x,y)\) is differentiable on \(I\), and if there is an integrable \(g\) with \(|\partial_y f(x,y)| \le g(x)\) for all \(y \in I\) and \(\mu\)-a.e. \(x\), then \(h\) is differentiable on \(I\) and
\[
h'(y) = \int_E \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x,y)\, \mu(dx).
\]
PROOF:
**1.** Fix \(y\) and \(y_n \to y\); in a metric space it suffices to show \(h(y_n) \to h(y)\). Put \(f_n(x) = f(x, y_n)\). Outside a null set, \(f_n(x) \to f(x,y)\) by continuity in \(y\), and \(|f_n(x)| \le g(x)\). Dominated convergence gives \(\int f_n\, d\mu \to \int f(\cdot, y)\, d\mu\).

**2.** Fix \(y \in I\) and \(y_n \to y\), \(y_n \neq y\). The quotients \(\Delta_n(x) = \frac{f(x,y_n) - f(x,y)}{y_n - y}\) are integrable with \(\int \Delta_n\, d\mu = \frac{h(y_n) - h(y)}{y_n - y}\). For a.e. \(x\), \(\Delta_n(x) \to \partial_y f(x,y)\), and by the mean value theorem \(\Delta_n(x) = \partial_y f(x, \theta_{n,x})\) for some \(\theta_{n,x}\) between \(y\) and \(y_n\), so \(|\Delta_n(x)| \le g(x)\). Dominated convergence gives \(\int \Delta_n\, d\mu \to \int \partial_y f(\cdot, y)\, d\mu\), a limit independent of the sequence. \(\square\)
REFRESH:
(none)
EXPLANATION:
We are studying a function defined by an integral, \(h(y) = \int f(x,y)\, \mu(dx)\), and asking whether it inherits continuity and differentiability from the integrand. The answer is yes, provided one dominating integrable function \(g(x)\) controls \(f\) (for continuity) or \(\partial_y f\) (for differentiability) uniformly in the parameter \(y\). Both statements are dominated convergence in disguise: continuity at \(y\) is convergence along sequences \(y_n \to y\), and the derivative is the limit of difference quotients, which the mean value theorem bounds by \(g\). This is how one differentiates \(\mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]\) in \(t\), and how Hoffmann checks that \(u(t,x) = \mathbb{E}[f(x + B_t)]\) solves the heat equation.
DEEP:
We want to pass limits in \(y\) through the integral. Plan: reduce to sequences, then apply dominated convergence with the given \(g\).

**Step 1: continuity.** Fix \(y \in F\) and a sequence \(y_n \to y\); in a metric space, continuity at \(y\) is equivalent to \(h(y_n) \to h(y)\) for every such sequence. Put \(f_n(x) = f(x, y_n)\). Outside the null set where continuity in \(y\) or the domination fails, \(f_n(x) \to f(x,y)\) by continuity of \(y \mapsto f(x,y)\), and \(|f_n(x)| \le g(x)\) for every \(n\); the bound is uniform in \(n\) because \(g\) does not depend on \(y\). Dominated convergence gives \(h(y_n) = \int f_n\, d\mu \to \int f(x,y)\, \mu(dx) = h(y)\). The reduction to sequences is essential: dominated convergence is a statement about sequences, not about a continuous parameter.

**Step 2: differentiation.** Fix \(y \in I\) and a sequence \(y_n \to y\) with \(y_n \neq y\). The difference quotients \(\Delta_n(x) = \frac{f(x,y_n) - f(x,y)}{y_n - y}\) are measurable and integrable, with \(\int \Delta_n\, d\mu = \frac{h(y_n) - h(y)}{y_n - y}\) by linearity. For a.e. \(x\), \(\Delta_n(x) \to \partial_y f(x,y)\) by differentiability; in particular \(x \mapsto \partial_y f(x,y)\) is measurable as an a.e. limit of measurable functions. For the domination, the mean value theorem applied to \(y \mapsto f(x,y)\) on the segment between \(y\) and \(y_n\) gives a point \(\theta_{n,x}\) in between with \(\Delta_n(x) = \partial_y f(x, \theta_{n,x})\), hence \(|\Delta_n(x)| \le g(x)\) because the bound on \(\partial_y f\) holds for all \(y \in I\). Dominated convergence yields \(\int \Delta_n\, d\mu \to \int \partial_y f(x,y)\, \mu(dx)\). The limit does not depend on the sequence, so \(h'(y)\) exists and equals it.

**Where the hypotheses bite.** The domination must be uniform in \(y\). Take \(E = (0,1)\) with Lebesgue measure and \(f(x,y) = \frac{y}{x^2 + y^2}\) for \(y > 0\), \(f(x, 0) = 0\). For each \(x\), \(f(x,y) \to 0 = f(x,0)\) as \(y \to 0\), and each \(x \mapsto f(x,y)\) is bounded and integrable; yet \(h(y) = \int_0^1 \frac{y\, dx}{x^2 + y^2} = \arctan(1/y) \to \pi/2 \neq 0 = h(0)\). What fails is the dominating function: \(\sup_{y > 0} f(x,y) = \frac{1}{2x}\) (attained at \(y = x\)) is not integrable near \(0\).

Used next: derivatives of characteristic functions (Lesson 2), the computation \(\phi_Z'(t) = -t\phi_Z(t)\) for a Gaussian (Lesson 4), and the heat equation \(\partial_t u = \frac12\partial_{xx}u\) for \(u(t,x) = \int f(y)\, p_t(x,y)\, dy\) in Hoffmann's Chapter 2.
EXAMPLE:
Let \(X\) be integrable and \(\phi(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]\), i.e. \(f(\omega, t) = e^{itX(\omega)}\) against \(\mathbb{P}\) (real and imaginary parts separately). Continuity: \(|f| \le 1\), integrable on a probability space, and \(t \mapsto e^{itX(\omega)}\) is continuous, so \(\phi\) is continuous. Differentiation: \(\partial_t f = iXe^{itX}\) is bounded by \(|X|\), integrable by assumption, so \(\phi'(t) = \mathbb{E}[iXe^{itX}]\) and \(\phi'(0) = i\,\mathbb{E}[X]\). With \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] < \infty\) one differentiates once more: \(\phi''(0) = -\mathbb{E}[X^2]\). For \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), \(\phi(t) = e^{-t^2/2}\) indeed has \(\phi'(0) = 0\) and \(\phi''(0) = -1\).

TITLE: Theorem 3 (Product measures and Fubini–Tonelli — Forien Def. 9–10, Thm 3–4)
IMPORTANCE: half imp
HIGHLIGHT: 0
STATEMENT:
Let \((E, \mathcal{E}, \mu)\) and \((F, \mathcal{F}, \nu)\) be \(\sigma\)-finite measured spaces. The **product \(\sigma\)-algebra** is \(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{F} = \sigma(\{A \times B : A \in \mathcal{E},\ B \in \mathcal{F}\})\), and the **product measure** \(\mu \otimes \nu\) is the unique measure on \((E \times F, \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{F})\) with \((\mu \otimes \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A)\nu(B)\) (existence admitted; uniqueness by Proposition 1). On \(\mathbb{R}^d\), \(\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^d) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})^{\otimes d}\) and \(\lambda^{\otimes d}\) is the Lebesgue measure of \(\mathbb{R}^d\).

1. **(Tonelli)** If \(f : E \times F \to [0, +\infty]\) is \(\mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{F}\)-measurable, then \(x \mapsto \int_F f(x,y)\, \nu(dy)\) and \(y \mapsto \int_E f(x,y)\, \mu(dx)\) are measurable and
\[
\int_{E \times F} f\, d(\mu \otimes \nu) \;=\; \int_E \Big(\int_F f(x,y)\, \nu(dy)\Big)\mu(dx) \;=\; \int_F \Big(\int_E f(x,y)\, \mu(dx)\Big)\nu(dy).
\]
2. **(Fubini)** If \(f : E \times F \to \mathbb{R}\) is measurable and one of the three quantities \(\int |f|\, d(\mu \otimes \nu)\), \(\int_E\int_F |f|\, d\nu\, d\mu\), \(\int_F\int_E |f|\, d\mu\, d\nu\) is finite, then all three are, \(f\) is integrable, the partial integrals are defined a.e. and integrable, and the identity of 1 holds for \(f\).
PROOF:
**Sections.** The family of \(C \in \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{F}\) whose sections \(C_x = \{y : (x,y) \in C\}\) all lie in \(\mathcal{F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing the rectangles, hence everything; so \(y \mapsto f(x,y)\) is measurable.

**Indicators, finite measures.** For \(C = A \times B\), \(x \mapsto \nu(C_x) = \nu(B)\mathbf{1}_A(x)\) is measurable with \(\mu\)-integral \(\mu(A)\nu(B) = (\mu \otimes \nu)(C)\). The family \(\mathcal{D}\) of \(C\) for which \(x \mapsto \nu(C_x)\) is measurable with \(\int \nu(C_x)\, \mu(dx) = (\mu \otimes \nu)(C)\) is a monotone class (differences by subtraction of finite quantities, increasing unions by continuity from below and monotone convergence) containing the \(\pi\)-system of rectangles, so \(\mathcal{D} = \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{F}\) by Theorem 1. In the \(\sigma\)-finite case apply this on \(E_n \times F_n\) with \(E_n \uparrow E\), \(F_n \uparrow F\) of finite measure and let \(n \to \infty\) by monotone convergence.

**Simple functions and limits.** Linearity gives 1 for simple \(f \ge 0\); for measurable \(f \ge 0\), take simple \(f_n \uparrow f\) and apply monotone convergence three times (inner integral, outer integral, product integral). Exchanging the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) gives the other order.

**Fubini.** Tonelli for \(|f|\) shows the three quantities coincide. Then Tonelli for \(f^\pm\), both integrable: \(g^\pm(x) = \int_F f^\pm(x,y)\, \nu(dy)\) are \(\mu\)-integrable, hence finite for a.e. \(x\), where \(\int_F f(x,y)\, \nu(dy) = g^+(x) - g^-(x)\); integrating in \(x\) and subtracting finite quantities gives the identity. \(\square\)
REFRESH:
(none)
EXPLANATION:
This block says when a double integral can be computed as two successive single integrals, in either order. For non-negative integrands the answer is always (Tonelli), the common value possibly being \(+\infty\); for signed integrands one first checks with Tonelli that \(\int |f| < \infty\), and then exchanges freely (Fubini). In probability the product measure is the law of a pair of independent variables, and the theorem is how one computes \(\mathbb{E}[g(X,Y)]\) by integrating first in \(X\), then in \(Y\); the freezing property of conditional expectation in Lesson 3 is its conditional form. The proof runs the indicator–simple–monotone machine, the indicator step being a monotone class argument.
DEEP:
The goal is to understand the structure of the proof, which is the template for every "for all measurable \(f\)" statement, and to see where \(\sigma\)-finiteness enters. Plan: sections, indicators, simple functions, limits, then the signed case.

**Step 1: sections are measurable.** For \(C \in \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{F}\) and \(x \in E\), the section \(C_x = \{y : (x,y) \in C\}\) is in \(\mathcal{F}\): the family of \(C\) with this property is a \(\sigma\)-algebra (sections commute with complements and countable unions) containing the rectangles, since \((A \times B)_x\) is \(B\) or \(\varnothing\). Hence \(y \mapsto f(x,y)\) is measurable for measurable \(f\), and the inner integrals make sense.

**Step 2: indicators, \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) finite.** For \(C = A \times B\), \(\int_F \mathbf{1}_C(x,y)\, \nu(dy) = \nu(B)\mathbf{1}_A(x)\), a measurable function of \(x\) whose \(\mu\)-integral is \(\mu(A)\nu(B) = (\mu \otimes \nu)(C)\). The family \(\mathcal{D}\) of sets \(C\) for which \(x \mapsto \nu(C_x)\) is measurable with \(\int \nu(C_x)\, \mu(dx) = (\mu \otimes \nu)(C)\) is a monotone class: nested differences by subtraction, legitimate because everything is finite, which is where finiteness enters; increasing unions by continuity from below and monotone convergence. The rectangles form a \(\pi\)-system, so Theorem 1 gives \(\mathcal{D} = \mathcal{E} \otimes \mathcal{F}\).

**Step 3: the \(\sigma\)-finite case.** Take \(E_n \uparrow E\), \(F_n \uparrow F\) of finite measure, apply Step 2 to the restricted measures on \(E_n \times F_n\), and let \(n \to \infty\) by monotone convergence. Without \(\sigma\)-finiteness the theorem fails: with \(\mu\) the Lebesgue measure and \(\nu\) the counting measure on \([0,1]\), the diagonal \(C = \{x = y\}\) has \(\int \nu(C_x)\, \mu(dx) = 1\) while \(\int \mu(C^y)\, \nu(dy) = 0\).

**Step 4: simple functions and limits.** By linearity the identity holds for simple \(f \ge 0\). For measurable \(f \ge 0\) take simple \(f_n \uparrow f\): for each fixed \(x\), \(\int_F f_n(x,y)\, \nu(dy) \uparrow \int_F f(x,y)\, \nu(dy)\) by monotone convergence, so the inner integral is measurable as a limit, and two more applications of monotone convergence, in \(\mu\) and in \(\mu \otimes \nu\), pass the identity to \(f\). Exchanging the roles of \(x\) and \(y\) gives the other order.

**Step 5: Fubini.** Tonelli applied to \(|f|\) shows the three quantities are equal, so one finite means all finite. Then apply Tonelli to \(f^+\) and \(f^-\), both integrable: \(g^\pm(x) = \int_F f^\pm(x,y)\, \nu(dy)\) are \(\mu\)-integrable, hence finite for \(\mu\)-a.e. \(x\), and for those \(x\) the partial integral of \(f\) is \(g^+(x) - g^-(x)\). Integrating in \(x\) and subtracting finite quantities gives the claim.

Used next: independence gives \(\mathbb{E}[g(X)h(Y)] = \mathbb{E}[g(X)]\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]\) (Lesson 2); Borel–Cantelli exchanges \(\mathbb{E}\) and \(\sum_n\) (Tonelli with the counting measure); Hoffmann's Chapter 2 writes \(P_{t+s} = P_tP_s\) as the exchange of two Gaussian integrals.
EXAMPLE:
Let \(X, Y\) be independent with laws \(\mu, \nu\) on \(\mathbb{R}\), so that \((X,Y)\) has law \(\mu \otimes \nu\) (Lesson 2). For \(g \ge 0\), \(\mathbb{E}[g(X+Y)] = \int\!\!\int g(x+y)\, \mu(dx)\, \nu(dy)\); with \(g = \mathbf{1}_{(-\infty, a]}\), \(\mathbb{P}(X + Y \le a) = \int \mu((-\infty, a - y])\, \nu(dy)\), the convolution formula. For \(X, Y\) uniform on \([0,1]\) and \(a \in [0,1]\): \(\mathbb{P}(X + Y \le a) = \int_0^a (a - y)\, dy = a^2/2\). An exchange that fails: \(f(x,y) = \frac{x^2 - y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}\) on \((0,1]^2\) gives \(\int_0^1\!\int_0^1 f\, dy\, dx = \pi/4\) and \(\int_0^1\!\int_0^1 f\, dx\, dy = -\pi/4\); Fubini does not apply because \(\int |f| = +\infty\).
