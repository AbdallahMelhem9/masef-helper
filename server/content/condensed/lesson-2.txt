===LESSON===
TITLE: Random variables, independence and convergence
DESCRIPTION: Forien §2–3 condensed: laws and the transfer formula, functions of a variable, L^p spaces and the projection theorem, the inequalities, independence, Borel–Cantelli, the four modes of convergence, characteristic functions and Lévy's theorem. 13 statements instead of 34.
INTRO:
Lesson 1 was measure theory; this lesson is the same theory with the vocabulary of probability, plus the two ideas that measure theory does not contain: independence and convergence in law. Nothing here is hard once the dictionary is clear. A random variable is a measurable map, its law is an image measure, an expectation is an integral, \(\sigma(X)\) is the information carried by \(X\), and "almost surely" means "outside a null set".

Thirteen statements out of thirty-four. Here is what each one is for.

- Definition 3 and Proposition 4 (the transfer formula) are what you use to compute anything: \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)] = \int h\, d\mathcal{L}_X\). Proposition 5 says that "\(\sigma(X)\)-measurable" means "a function of \(X\)"; it will make conditional expectations concrete.
- Definition 4, Theorem 4 and Proposition 6 are the analysis: the \(L^p\) spaces, the Hilbert structure of \(L^2\) with the projection theorem (which will give us conditional expectation without Radon–Nikodym, and which Hoffmann uses for Gaussian spaces), and the inequalities of Jensen, Cauchy–Schwarz, Hölder and Minkowski.
- Proposition 7 (Markov, Chebyshev, Chernoff) and Proposition 8 (Borel–Cantelli) form the pair "bound a probability by a moment, then sum": this is how every almost sure statement about Brownian paths is proved, starting with Kolmogorov's continuity criterion.
- Definition 5 is independence, with the criterion on \(\pi\)-systems that makes it checkable and the grouping property that makes it usable.
- Definition 6, Proposition 9, Definition/Proposition 10 and Theorem 5 are the theory of convergence: the four modes and their relations, characteristic functions, and Lévy's theorem, which turns convergence in law into a computation.

Left out: the tightness and Prokhorov compactness theorem, the characterisation of distribution functions and the inverse-transform simulation method, Exercise 2 (Borel–Cantelli under pairwise independence). The full prérentrée keeps them.

In Hoffmann's Chapter 1, the first proposition already uses "\(L^2\) convergence implies convergence in law implies convergence of characteristic functions", the uniform integrability of Lesson 3, and the Hilbert structure of \(L^2\). Everything in that first page is here.

KIND: definition
TITLE: Definition 3 (Probability space, random variables, laws — Forien Def. 11)
IMPORTANCE: not imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
A **probability space** is a measured space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) with \(\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1\). Measure-theoretic objects then get probabilistic names.

- An **event** is a set \(A \in \mathcal{F}\); it holds **almost surely** (a.s.) if \(\mathbb{P}(A) = 1\).
- A **random variable** with values in a measurable space \((E, \mathcal{E})\) is a measurable map \(X : \Omega \to E\) (real-valued when \(E\) is not specified; a **random vector** when \(E = \mathbb{R}^d\)).
- Its **law** (or distribution) is the image probability measure on \(E\), \(\mathcal{L}_X(A) = \mathbb{P}(X \in A) = \mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(A))\), also written \(\mathbb{P}_X\); \(X \sim \mu\) means \(\mathcal{L}_X = \mu\), and \(X \overset{d}{=} Y\) means \(\mathcal{L}_X = \mathcal{L}_Y\).
- For a real variable, the **distribution function** \(F_X(x) = \mathbb{P}(X \le x)\) determines the law (Proposition 1); the law **has density** \(f\) if \(\mathbb{P}(X \in A) = \int_A f(x)\, dx\) for all Borel \(A\).
- The **expectation** of a non-negative or integrable variable is \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_\Omega X\, d\mathbb{P}\); \(X\) is **centred** if \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 0\).
- The **variance** of \(X \in L^2\) is \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}X)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}X)^2\), and the **covariance** of \(X, Y \in L^2\) is \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}X)(Y - \mathbb{E}Y)] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]\).
- The **\(\sigma\)-algebra generated by** \(X\) is \(\sigma(X) = \{X^{-1}(A) : A \in \mathcal{E}\}\), the smallest sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra of \(\mathcal{F}\) making \(X\) measurable; for a family, \(\sigma(X_i, i \in I)\) is the \(\sigma\)-algebra generated by all the sets \(X_i^{-1}(A)\).

The set \(\Omega\) is usually left unspecified: what matters are the random variables and their laws.
PROOF:
**The law is a probability measure.** \(\mathcal{L}_X(E) = \mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1\); for disjoint \(A_n \in \mathcal{E}\), the sets \(X^{-1}(A_n)\) are disjoint and \(X^{-1}(\bigcup_n A_n) = \bigcup_n X^{-1}(A_n)\), so \(\sigma\)-additivity transfers from \(\mathbb{P}\). By Proposition 1, \(\mathcal{L}_X\) is determined by \(F_X\).

**Variance and covariance.** For \(X, Y \in L^2\), \(XY\) and \(X\) are integrable (Proposition 6). With \(m = \mathbb{E}X\), \(m' = \mathbb{E}Y\), expand \((X - m)(Y - m') = XY - m'X - mY + mm'\) and take expectations: \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - mm'\); \(Y = X\) gives the variance.

**\(\sigma(X)\).** \(\{X^{-1}(A) : A \in \mathcal{E}\}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra since preimages commute with complements and countable unions; it is contained in \(\mathcal{F}\) because \(X\) is measurable; any sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra making \(X\) measurable contains it. \(\square\)
REFRESH:
(none)
EXPLANATION:
Nothing new is defined here; the block translates Lesson 1 into the language of probability. The sample space \(\Omega\) is the set of all possible states of the world, the \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\) lists the events, and \(\mathbb{P}\) weighs them. A random variable is a measurable map, and the only two things one ever computes about it are its law, a probability measure on the target space, and expectations, which are integrals. The law forgets \(\Omega\) entirely: two variables on different spaces with the same law are indistinguishable by any probabilistic computation. Finally \(\sigma(X)\) is the information carried by \(X\), the events that can be decided by looking at \(X\) only.
DEEP:
The goal is to check that these objects are well defined and to understand the identities that are used without comment everywhere.

**Step 1: the law is a probability measure.** \(\mathcal{L}_X(E) = \mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1\), and for disjoint \(A_n\) the preimages \(X^{-1}(A_n)\) are disjoint with \(X^{-1}(\bigcup_n A_n) = \bigcup_n X^{-1}(A_n)\), so \(\sigma\)-additivity of \(\mathbb{P}\) transfers. Since \(\mathcal{L}_X\) is a probability measure on \(\mathbb{R}\), Proposition 1 says it is determined by its values on the half-lines, i.e. by \(F_X\). A distribution function is non-decreasing, right-continuous (continuity from above along \((-\infty, x + 1/n] \downarrow (-\infty, x]\)), with limits \(0\) at \(-\infty\) and \(1\) at \(+\infty\); its jump at \(x\) is \(\mathbb{P}(X = x)\).

**Step 2: variance and covariance.** For \(X \in L^2\), \(X\) is integrable (\(|X| \le 1 + X^2\)), and expanding \((X - m)^2 = X^2 - 2mX + m^2\) with \(m = \mathbb{E}X\) gives \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2m^2 + m^2\). The same expansion gives the covariance formula; \(\mathrm{Cov}\) is bilinear and symmetric, \(\mathrm{Cov}(X,X) = \mathrm{Var}(X)\), and \(\mathrm{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\mathrm{Var}(X)\). In particular
\[
\mathrm{Var}(X_1 + \dots + X_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}(X_i) + 2\sum_{i < j}\mathrm{Cov}(X_i, X_j),
\]
which reduces to the sum of the variances when the variables are pairwise uncorrelated. The variance is \(0\) if and only if \(X = \mathbb{E}X\) a.s., since a non-negative variable with zero expectation vanishes a.s. (Definition/Proposition 2).

**Step 3: \(\sigma(X)\).** The family \(\{X^{-1}(A) : A \in \mathcal{E}\}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra because preimages commute with complements and countable unions, and it is contained in \(\mathcal{F}\) precisely because \(X\) is measurable. Any sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{G}\) for which \(X\) is measurable contains all the \(X^{-1}(A)\), so \(\sigma(X)\) is the smallest one. Reading: \(\sigma(X)\) is the set of events of the form "\(X \in A\)", exactly the events whose occurrence is known once \(X\) is observed. Proposition 5 makes this precise: the \(\sigma(X)\)-measurable variables are exactly the functions of \(X\).

**Step 4: why \(\Omega\) does not matter.** Every quantity above, \(\mathbb{P}(X \in A)\), \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)]\) (by Proposition 4), \(F_X\), is a function of \(\mathcal{L}_X\) alone. Hoffmann exploits this constantly: Brownian motion is first built on some abstract \(\Omega\), then transported to the canonical space \(C(\mathbb{R}_+, \mathbb{R})\) equipped with the Wiener measure, its law, without any loss.
EXAMPLE:
Let \(\Omega = [0,1]\) with \(\mathcal{F} = \mathcal{B}([0,1])\) and \(\mathbb{P} = \lambda\), and \(X(\omega) = -\ln(1 - \omega)\) (with \(X(1) = +\infty\), a null set). For \(a \ge 0\), \(\{X \le a\} = [0, 1 - e^{-a}]\), so \(F_X(a) = 1 - e^{-a}\): \(X\) is exponential of parameter \(1\), and \(\sigma(X) = \mathcal{B}([0,1])\) since \(X\) is continuous and injective. The variable \(Y(\omega) = \mathbf{1}_{\omega > 1/2}\) has law \(\frac12\delta_0 + \frac12\delta_1\), no density, \(\mathbb{E}[Y] = \frac12\), \(\mathrm{Var}(Y) = \frac14\), and \(\sigma(Y) = \{\varnothing, [0, \tfrac12], (\tfrac12, 1], \Omega\}\): observing \(Y\) only tells you which half \(\omega\) fell in.

KIND: proposition
TITLE: Proposition 4 (Transfer formula — Forien Prop. 5)
IMPORTANCE: imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
Let \(X\) be a random variable with values in \((E, \mathcal{E})\) and \(h : E \to \mathbb{R}\) measurable. Then \(h(X)\) is integrable if and only if \(\int_E |h|\, d\mathcal{L}_X < \infty\), and in that case, as well as whenever \(h \ge 0\),
\[
\mathbb{E}[h(X)] = \int_E h(x)\, \mathcal{L}_X(dx).
\]
If \(E = \mathbb{R}\) and the law of \(X\) has density \(f\), then \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)] = \int_{\mathbb{R}} h(x)f(x)\, dx\); if \(X\) takes its values in a countable set \(S\), then \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)] = \sum_{x \in S} h(x)\,\mathbb{P}(X = x)\).
PROOF:
**Indicators.** For \(h = \mathbf{1}_A\), \(h(X) = \mathbf{1}_{\{X \in A\}}\) and \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)] = \mathbb{P}(X \in A) = \mathcal{L}_X(A) = \int \mathbf{1}_A\, d\mathcal{L}_X\).

**Simple functions.** Both sides are linear in \(h\).

**Non-negative \(h\).** Take simple \(h_n \uparrow h\); then \(h_n(X) \uparrow h(X)\), and monotone convergence on \((\Omega, \mathbb{P})\) and on \((E, \mathcal{L}_X)\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)] = \lim_n \mathbb{E}[h_n(X)] = \lim_n \int h_n\, d\mathcal{L}_X = \int h\, d\mathcal{L}_X\).

**Integrable \(h\).** The previous step for \(|h|\) gives the integrability equivalence; then apply it to \(h^+\) and \(h^-\) and subtract.

**Density and discrete cases.** If \(\mathcal{L}_X(A) = \int_A f(x)\, dx\), the same four steps give \(\int h\, d\mathcal{L}_X = \int h(x) f(x)\, dx\). If \(X\) takes values in a countable \(S\), \(\mathcal{L}_X = \sum_{x \in S}\mathbb{P}(X = x)\delta_x\) and \(\int h\, d\delta_x = h(x)\). \(\square\)
REFRESH:
(none)
EXPLANATION:
This formula is how one actually computes expectations: instead of integrating over the abstract \(\Omega\), integrate over the values, weighted by the law. It says that \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)]\) depends on \(X\) only through \(\mathcal{L}_X\), which justifies the slogan "only the law matters". The proof is the first instance of the standard machine: the formula is the definition of the law when \(h\) is an indicator, extends to simple functions by linearity, to non-negative functions by monotone convergence, and to integrable functions by splitting \(h = h^+ - h^-\). Memorise the machine as much as the formula; it recurs in Proposition 5, in Fubini, and in the properties of conditional expectation.
DEEP:
We want to prove \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)] = \int h\, d\mathcal{L}_X\) for every measurable \(h \ge 0\), then for integrable \(h\). Plan: the standard machine.

**Step 1: indicators.** For \(h = \mathbf{1}_A\), \(A \in \mathcal{E}\), we have \(h(X) = \mathbf{1}_{\{X \in A\}}\), so \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)] = \mathbb{P}(X \in A) = \mathcal{L}_X(A) = \int \mathbf{1}_A\, d\mathcal{L}_X\). This is the definition of the law, nothing more.

**Step 2: simple functions.** For \(h = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i}\) with \(a_i \ge 0\), both sides are linear in \(h\), so Step 1 gives the identity.

**Step 3: non-negative \(h\).** Choose simple \(h_n \uparrow h\), for instance \(h_n = \min(2^{-n}\lfloor 2^n h\rfloor, n)\). Then \(h_n(X) \uparrow h(X)\) pointwise on \(\Omega\), and monotone convergence applied on both spaces, \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) and \((E, \mathcal{E}, \mathcal{L}_X)\), gives \(\mathbb{E}[h(X)] = \lim_n \mathbb{E}[h_n(X)] = \lim_n \int h_n\, d\mathcal{L}_X = \int h\, d\mathcal{L}_X\), in \([0, +\infty]\).

**Step 4: integrable \(h\).** Step 3 applied to \(|h|\) gives \(\mathbb{E}|h(X)| = \int |h|\, d\mathcal{L}_X\), which is the integrability equivalence. Then apply Step 3 to \(h^+\) and \(h^-\), both with finite integrals, and subtract.

**Step 5: the density and discrete cases.** If \(\mathcal{L}_X(A) = \int_A f(x)\, dx\), the same four steps show \(\int h\, d\mathcal{L}_X = \int h(x)f(x)\, dx\): the identity holds for indicators by definition of a density, then extends. If \(X\) takes values in a countable \(S\), then \(\mathcal{L}_X = \sum_{x \in S}\mathbb{P}(X = x)\,\delta_x\), and integrating against a sum of Dirac masses is summing.

**Where this is used.** Every explicit computation in the course: \(\mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]\) for a Gaussian \(X\) is \(\int e^{itx}\frac{e^{-x^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, dx\); \(\mathbb{E}[f(x + B_t)] = \int f(y)\, p_t(x,y)\, dy\) is Hoffmann's definition of the semigroup \(P_tf(x)\) in Chapter 2. Also the change of variables rule: if \(Y = g(X)\), then \(\mathcal{L}_Y\) is the image of \(\mathcal{L}_X\) by \(g\), and \(\mathbb{E}[h(Y)] = \int h(g(x))\, \mathcal{L}_X(dx)\).
EXAMPLE:
Let \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and compute \(\mathbb{E}[Z^2]\) and \(\mathbb{E}[e^Z]\). By the density case, \(\mathbb{E}[Z^2] = \int x^2\frac{e^{-x^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, dx\); integrating by parts with \(xe^{-x^2/2} = -(e^{-x^2/2})'\) gives \(\int x\cdot xe^{-x^2/2}\, dx = \int e^{-x^2/2}\, dx = \sqrt{2\pi}\), so \(\mathbb{E}[Z^2] = 1\). Next, \(\mathbb{E}[e^Z] = \int e^{x - x^2/2}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\pi}} = e^{1/2}\int \frac{e^{-(x-1)^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, dx = e^{1/2}\), by completing the square. This second computation, \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\sigma Z}] = e^{\sigma^2/2}\), is the one behind the martingale \(e^{\sigma B_t - \sigma^2t/2}\) of stochastic calculus.

KIND: proposition
TITLE: Proposition 5 (σ(X)-measurable variables are functions of X — Forien Prop. 6)
IMPORTANCE: imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
Let \(X\) be a random variable with values in \((E, \mathcal{E})\) and \(Y\) a real random variable. Then \(Y\) is \(\sigma(X)\)-measurable if and only if there is a measurable \(\psi : E \to \mathbb{R}\) such that \(Y = \psi(X)\). The same holds for a finite family: \(Y\) is \(\sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n)\)-measurable if and only if \(Y = \psi(X_1, \dots, X_n)\) with \(\psi\) measurable on the product space.
PROOF:
If \(Y = \psi(X)\), then \(\{Y \in B\} = X^{-1}(\psi^{-1}(B)) \in \sigma(X)\). Conversely, let \(Y\) be \(\sigma(X)\)-measurable.

**Indicators.** If \(Y = \mathbf{1}_G\) with \(G \in \sigma(X)\), then \(G = X^{-1}(A)\) for some \(A \in \mathcal{E}\) and \(Y = \mathbf{1}_A(X)\).

**Simple functions.** \(Y = \sum_i c_i\mathbf{1}_{X^{-1}(A_i)} = \psi(X)\) with \(\psi = \sum_i c_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i}\).

**\(Y \ge 0\).** Take \(\sigma(X)\)-measurable simple \(Y_n \uparrow Y\) and write \(Y_n = \psi_n(X)\). Let \(D = \{\limsup_n \psi_n < \infty\} \in \mathcal{E}\) and \(\psi = (\limsup_n \psi_n)\mathbf{1}_D\), measurable. For every \(\omega\), \(\psi_n(X(\omega)) = Y_n(\omega) \to Y(\omega) < \infty\), so \(X(\omega) \in D\) and \(\psi(X(\omega)) = Y(\omega)\).

**General \(Y\).** Apply the previous step to \(Y^\pm\) and set \(\psi = \psi^+ - \psi^-\).

**Finite families.** \(\sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n) = \sigma(\mathbf{X})\) for \(\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \dots, X_n)\) with values in the product space with its product \(\sigma\)-algebra (both are generated by the sets \(\{X_1 \in A_1, \dots, X_n \in A_n\}\)), so the one-variable case applies to \(\mathbf{X}\). \(\square\)
REFRESH:
(none)
EXPLANATION:
The statement gives the concrete meaning of "\(\sigma(X)\)-measurable": it means "a function of \(X\)". One direction is trivial, a measurable function of a measurable map being measurable; the substance is the converse: if every event about \(Y\) can be decided by looking at \(X\), then \(Y\) is literally computable from \(X\) by some measurable rule \(\psi\). The proof is again the standard machine, starting from indicators, where the statement is just the definition of \(\sigma(X)\). This lemma is what turns the abstract conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[Z \mid \sigma(X)]\) into a function \(h(X)\), the form in which the Markov property is written in Hoffmann's Chapter 2.
DEEP:
We want to show that a \(\sigma(X)\)-measurable \(Y\) is of the form \(\psi(X)\). The converse is immediate: \(\{\psi(X) \in B\} = X^{-1}(\psi^{-1}(B)) \in \sigma(X)\) for Borel \(B\). Plan: the standard machine.

**Step 1: indicators.** Let \(Y = \mathbf{1}_G\) with \(G \in \sigma(X)\). By definition of \(\sigma(X)\), \(G = X^{-1}(A)\) for some \(A \in \mathcal{E}\), so \(\mathbf{1}_G(\omega) = \mathbf{1}_A(X(\omega))\) for every \(\omega\): \(Y = \psi(X)\) with \(\psi = \mathbf{1}_A\), measurable.

**Step 2: simple functions.** If \(Y = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i\mathbf{1}_{G_i}\) with \(G_i = X^{-1}(A_i)\), then \(Y = \psi(X)\) with \(\psi = \sum_i c_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i}\).

**Step 3: non-negative \(Y\).** Take \(\sigma(X)\)-measurable simple \(Y_n \uparrow Y\) (the canonical approximations \(\min(2^{-n}\lfloor 2^nY\rfloor, n)\) are \(\sigma(X)\)-measurable because \(Y\) is), and write \(Y_n = \psi_n(X)\) by Step 2. Set \(\psi = \limsup_n \psi_n\) on the set \(D = \{\limsup_n \psi_n < \infty\} \in \mathcal{E}\) and \(\psi = 0\) outside \(D\); \(\psi\) is measurable as a truncated \(\limsup\) of measurable functions. For every \(\omega\), \(\psi_n(X(\omega)) = Y_n(\omega) \to Y(\omega) < \infty\), so \(X(\omega) \in D\) and \(\psi(X(\omega)) = \lim_n Y_n(\omega) = Y(\omega)\). The truncation modifies \(\psi\) only outside the range of \(X\), which is harmless; it is needed because the \(\psi_n\) may fail to converge at points \(x\) that are not values of \(X\).

**Step 4: general \(Y\).** Apply Step 3 to \(Y^+\) and \(Y^-\) and set \(\psi = \psi^+ - \psi^-\).

**Finite families.** \(\sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n) = \sigma(\mathbf{X})\) where \(\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \dots, X_n)\) is a random variable with values in the product space with the product \(\sigma\)-algebra: the sets \(\{X_1 \in A_1, \dots, X_n \in A_n\}\) generate both. So the statement for one variable applies to \(\mathbf{X}\). For an infinite family the same holds with \(\psi\) defined on the product space \(\prod_i E_i\), and one can show \(\psi\) depends on countably many coordinates only; we will not need it.

Used next: the definition \(\mathbb{E}[Z \mid X] := \mathbb{E}[Z \mid \sigma(X)]\) in Lesson 3 is a function \(h(X)\) by this proposition; in Hoffmann's Chapter 2 the Markov property is the identity \(\mathbb{E}[f(B_{t+s}) \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = P_sf(B_t)\), a function of \(B_t\) alone.
EXAMPLE:
Let \(X\) be uniform on \([0,1]\) and \(Y = \mathbf{1}_{\{X \le 1/3\}} + 2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{X > 2/3\}}\). Then \(Y = \psi(X)\) with \(\psi(x) = \mathbf{1}_{[0, 1/3]}(x) + 2\cdot\mathbf{1}_{(2/3, 1]}(x)\), and indeed \(Y\) is \(\sigma(X)\)-measurable. Conversely \(X\) is not \(\sigma(Y)\)-measurable: \(\sigma(Y)\) is generated by the three events \(\{X \le 1/3\}\), \(\{1/3 < X \le 2/3\}\), \(\{X > 2/3\}\), so every \(\sigma(Y)\)-measurable variable is constant on each of them, which \(X\) is not. No measurable \(\psi\) can give \(X = \psi(Y)\), since \(Y\) takes three values and \(X\) infinitely many.

KIND: definition
TITLE: Definition 4 (L^p spaces; L^2 as a Hilbert space — Forien Def. 12)
IMPORTANCE: half imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\) be a probability space and \(1 \le p < \infty\). For a random variable \(X\), \(\|X\|_{L^p} = \mathbb{E}[|X|^p]^{1/p}\), and
\[
L^p(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P}) = \big\{X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R} \text{ measurable},\ \|X\|_{L^p} < \infty\big\} \big/ \sim,
\]
where \(X \sim Y\) if \(X = Y\) a.s., so that \(\|X\|_{L^p} = 0\) exactly for the zero class. For \(p = \infty\), \(\|X\|_{L^\infty} = \inf\{c : \mathbb{P}(|X| \le c) = 1\}\). Each \(L^p\) is a vector space, \(\|\cdot\|_{L^p}\) is a norm on it (Minkowski's inequality, Proposition 6), and \(L^p\) is **complete** for this norm, i.e. a Banach space (admitted). For \(p \le q\), \(L^q \subset L^p\) and \(\|X\|_{L^p} \le \|X\|_{L^q}\).

The space \(L^2\) is a **Hilbert space** for the scalar product \(\langle X, Y\rangle = \mathbb{E}[XY]\), with \(\|X\|_{L^2}^2 = \langle X, X\rangle\); two variables are **orthogonal** if \(\mathbb{E}[XY] = 0\), and centred variables are orthogonal exactly when they are uncorrelated. If \(X_n \to X\) in \(L^2\), then \(\mathbb{E}[X_n] \to \mathbb{E}[X]\), \(\langle X_n, Y\rangle \to \langle X, Y\rangle\) for every \(Y \in L^2\), and \(\mathrm{Var}(X_n) \to \mathrm{Var}(X)\); in particular an \(L^2\) limit of centred variables is centred.
PROOF:
**Vector space.** \(|X + Y|^p \le 2^{p-1}(|X|^p + |Y|^p)\) by convexity of \(t \mapsto t^p\); the triangle inequality is Minkowski (Proposition 6); \(\|X\|_{L^p} = 0\) iff \(|X|^p = 0\) a.s. (Definition/Proposition 2).

**Nesting.** Jensen with the convex \(t \mapsto |t|^{q/p}\) applied to \(|X|^p\): \((\mathbb{E}|X|^p)^{q/p} \le \mathbb{E}|X|^q\) (Proposition 6).

**Scalar product.** \(\mathbb{E}|XY| \le \|X\|_{L^2}\|Y\|_{L^2}\) (Cauchy–Schwarz) makes \(\langle X, Y\rangle\) finite; bilinearity and symmetry are clear; \(\langle X, X\rangle = \|X\|^2_{L^2}\).

**Limits.** \(|\mathbb{E}X_n - \mathbb{E}X| \le \|X_n - X\|_{L^1} \le \|X_n - X\|_{L^2}\); \(|\langle X_n, Y\rangle - \langle X, Y\rangle| \le \|X_n - X\|_{L^2}\|Y\|_{L^2}\); \(\big|\|X_n\| - \|X\|\big| \le \|X_n - X\|\); hence \(\mathrm{Var}(X_n) = \|X_n\|^2 - (\mathbb{E}X_n)^2 \to \mathrm{Var}(X)\).

**Completeness** (Riesz–Fischer) is admitted: extract a subsequence with \(\|X_{n_{k+1}} - X_{n_k}\|_{L^p} \le 2^{-k}\); the series \(\sum_k |X_{n_{k+1}} - X_{n_k}|\) is in \(L^p\) by monotone convergence and Minkowski, hence finite a.s.; the subsequence converges a.s. to some \(X\), and Fatou gives \(\|X_n - X\|_{L^p} \to 0\). \(\square\)
REFRESH:
(none)
EXPLANATION:
\(L^p\) collects the random variables whose \(p\)-th moment is finite, with variables equal a.s. identified so that \(\|\cdot\|_{L^p}\) becomes a genuine norm. On a probability space the spaces are nested, \(L^\infty \subset L^2 \subset L^1\): more integrability means a smaller space. The important member is \(L^2\): its norm comes from a scalar product, \(\mathbb{E}[XY]\), so it has a geometry with orthogonality, Pythagoras and projections. Completeness, every Cauchy sequence converges, is what allows one to define objects as \(L^2\) limits; this is how Hoffmann builds Brownian motion, \(B_t = X(\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]})\), from an isometry between \(L^2(\mathbb{R}_+, dt)\) and a closed subspace of \(L^2(\Omega)\) made of Gaussian variables.
DEEP:
The goal is to see why \(L^p\) is a normed vector space, why the spaces are nested, and what the Hilbert structure of \(L^2\) gives.

**Step 1: a vector space.** If \(X, Y \in L^p\), then \(|X + Y|^p \le (|X| + |Y|)^p \le 2^{p-1}(|X|^p + |Y|^p)\) by convexity of \(t \mapsto t^p\), so \(X + Y \in L^p\); scalar multiples are obvious. The triangle inequality for the norm is Minkowski's inequality (Proposition 6); homogeneity is clear; and \(\|X\|_{L^p} = 0\) means \(\mathbb{E}|X|^p = 0\), i.e. \(X = 0\) a.s. (Definition/Proposition 2), which is why one must quotient by a.s. equality to get a norm. In practice one keeps writing \(X\) for its class and remembers that identities in \(L^p\) hold almost surely.

**Step 2: nesting.** For \(p \le q\), the function \(t \mapsto |t|^{q/p}\) is convex, so Jensen's inequality (Proposition 6) applied to \(|X|^p\) gives \((\mathbb{E}|X|^p)^{q/p} \le \mathbb{E}|X|^q\), i.e. \(\|X\|_{L^p} \le \|X\|_{L^q}\). This uses \(\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1\): on \(\mathbb{R}\) with Lebesgue measure there is no inclusion between \(L^1\) and \(L^2\) in either direction (\(x^{-1/2}\mathbf{1}_{(0,1)}\) is in \(L^1\) but not \(L^2\); \(x^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{(1,\infty)}\) is in \(L^2\) but not \(L^1\)).

**Step 3: the scalar product.** \(\langle X, Y\rangle = \mathbb{E}[XY]\) is defined for \(X, Y \in L^2\) by Cauchy–Schwarz, \(\mathbb{E}|XY| \le \|X\|_{L^2}\|Y\|_{L^2}\); it is bilinear, symmetric, and \(\langle X, X\rangle = \|X\|_{L^2}^2 > 0\) unless \(X = 0\) a.s. Hence Pythagoras: \(\|X + Y\|_{L^2}^2 = \|X\|_{L^2}^2 + \|Y\|_{L^2}^2\) when \(\mathbb{E}[XY] = 0\). For centred variables, \(\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\), so orthogonality is uncorrelatedness, and \(\mathrm{Var}(X + Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Y)\) for uncorrelated variables is Pythagoras.

**Step 4: what passes to \(L^2\) limits.** If \(X_n \to X\) in \(L^2\), then \(|\mathbb{E}X_n - \mathbb{E}X| \le \mathbb{E}|X_n - X| \le \|X_n - X\|_{L^2} \to 0\) (Step 2 with \(p = 1\), \(q = 2\)), so means converge; and \(|\langle X_n, Y\rangle - \langle X, Y\rangle| \le \|X_n - X\|_{L^2}\|Y\|_{L^2}\), so scalar products, norms and variances converge. This is the input of the lemma "an \(L^2\) limit of Gaussian variables is Gaussian" (Lesson 4, Corollary 2).

**Step 5: completeness, and what it is for.** The statement that every Cauchy sequence in \(L^p\) converges in \(L^p\) (Riesz–Fischer) is admitted here; its proof extracts a subsequence with \(\|X_{n_{k+1}} - X_{n_k}\|_{L^p} \le 2^{-k}\), shows the series of increments converges a.s. by monotone convergence, and identifies the limit. Completeness is used every time an object is defined as a limit: the stochastic integral \(\int_0^T H_s\, dB_s\) is the \(L^2\) limit of integrals of step processes, and Hoffmann's Theorem 10 defines \(X(h)\) for \(h \in L^2(\mathbb{R}_+)\) as the \(L^2\)-convergent series \(\sum_i \langle h, e_i\rangle\,\xi_i\).
EXAMPLE:
Let \(X\) have density \(f(x) = \frac{2}{x^3}\mathbf{1}_{x \ge 1}\) (a Pareto law). Then \(\mathbb{E}|X| = \int_1^\infty \frac{2}{x^2}\, dx = 2 < \infty\), but \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \int_1^\infty \frac{2}{x}\, dx = +\infty\): \(X \in L^1 \setminus L^2\), so the inclusion \(L^2 \subset L^1\) is strict. In \(L^2\), take \(Z_1, Z_2\) independent standard Gaussians and \(X = Z_1\), \(Y = Z_1 + Z_2\). Then \(\langle X, Y\rangle = \mathbb{E}[Z_1^2] + \mathbb{E}[Z_1Z_2] = 1\), \(\|Y\|_{L^2}^2 = 2\), and the orthogonal projection of \(Y\) on the line spanned by \(X\) is \(\frac{\langle Y, X\rangle}{\|X\|^2}X = Z_1\), leaving the orthogonal remainder \(Z_2\).

KIND: theorem
TITLE: Theorem 4 (Orthogonal projection in L^2 — not in the prérentrée; replaces Radon–Nikodym)
IMPORTANCE: imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
Let \(H\) be a closed linear subspace of \(L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})\), for instance \(H = L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, \mathbb{P})\) for a sub-\(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{G} \subset \mathcal{F}\). For every \(X \in L^2\) there is a unique \(\pi_H(X) \in H\) such that
\[
\|X - \pi_H(X)\|_{L^2} = \min_{Y \in H}\|X - Y\|_{L^2},
\]
and it is characterised by: \(\pi_H(X) \in H\) and \(\mathbb{E}[(X - \pi_H(X))\,Y] = 0\) for every \(Y \in H\). The map \(\pi_H\) is linear, \(\|\pi_H(X)\|_{L^2} \le \|X\|_{L^2}\), and \(\|X\|_{L^2}^2 = \|\pi_H(X)\|_{L^2}^2 + \|X - \pi_H(X)\|_{L^2}^2\).
PROOF:
Norms are \(L^2\) norms. Let \(d = \inf_{Y \in H}\|X - Y\|\) and \(Y_n \in H\) with \(\|X - Y_n\| \to d\). The parallelogram identity \(\|U + V\|^2 + \|U - V\|^2 = 2\|U\|^2 + 2\|V\|^2\) with \(U = X - Y_n\), \(V = X - Y_m\) gives \(\|Y_m - Y_n\|^2 = 2\|X - Y_n\|^2 + 2\|X - Y_m\|^2 - 4\|X - \frac{Y_n + Y_m}{2}\|^2 \le 2\|X - Y_n\|^2 + 2\|X - Y_m\|^2 - 4d^2 \to 0\), since \(\frac{Y_n + Y_m}{2} \in H\). So \((Y_n)\) is Cauchy, converges to some \(Y_\ast \in H\) (completeness, \(H\) closed), and \(\|X - Y_\ast\| = d\).

**Orthogonality.** For \(Y \in H\), \(t \in \mathbb{R}\): \(\|X - Y_\ast - tY\|^2 \ge d^2\), i.e. \(-2t\langle X - Y_\ast, Y\rangle + t^2\|Y\|^2 \ge 0\); letting \(t \to 0^\pm\) gives \(\langle X - Y_\ast, Y\rangle = 0\).

**Converse and uniqueness.** If \(Z \in H\) and \(\langle X - Z, Y\rangle = 0\) for all \(Y \in H\), then for \(Y \in H\), Pythagoras gives \(\|X - Y\|^2 = \|X - Z\|^2 + \|Z - Y\|^2 \ge \|X - Z\|^2\), with equality only for \(Y = Z\). With \(Y = 0\): \(\|X\|^2 = \|X - Z\|^2 + \|Z\|^2\).

**Linearity.** \(\pi_H(X) + \pi_H(X') \in H\) and \(X + X' - \pi_H(X) - \pi_H(X') \perp H\), so it is \(\pi_H(X + X')\) by uniqueness; similarly for scalars. \(\square\)
REFRESH:
**Closed subspace of \(L^2\)** (Definition 4): a linear subspace containing its \(L^2\) limits. \(L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, \mathbb{P})\) is closed: an \(L^2\) limit of \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable variables has a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable version (Proposition 9).
EXPLANATION:
This is the geometric fact that makes \(L^2\) so useful: given a closed subspace \(H\), every \(X\) has a unique closest point in \(H\), its orthogonal projection, and the difference \(X - \pi_H(X)\) is orthogonal to \(H\). When \(H\) is the space of square-integrable \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable variables, the projection is the best approximation of \(X\) by a quantity computable from the information \(\mathcal{G}\): this is exactly what conditional expectation will be in Lesson 3, and the orthogonality relation \(\mathbb{E}[(X - \pi_H(X))Y] = 0\) is its defining property. The proof finds the closest point as the limit of a minimising sequence, which is Cauchy by the parallelogram identity; completeness supplies the limit.
DEEP:
We want to find the closest point of \(H\) to \(X\) and to show that "closest" is the same as "orthogonal remainder". Plan: existence by a minimising sequence, uniqueness and characterisation by a variational computation. All norms are \(L^2\) norms.

**Step 1: a minimising sequence is Cauchy.** Let \(d = \inf_{Y \in H}\|X - Y\|\) and pick \(Y_n \in H\) with \(\|X - Y_n\| \to d\). The parallelogram identity \(\|U + V\|^2 + \|U - V\|^2 = 2\|U\|^2 + 2\|V\|^2\), valid in any space whose norm comes from a scalar product (expand both sides), applied to \(U = X - Y_n\), \(V = X - Y_m\), gives
\[
\|Y_m - Y_n\|^2 = 2\|X - Y_n\|^2 + 2\|X - Y_m\|^2 - 4\Big\|X - \tfrac{Y_n + Y_m}{2}\Big\|^2 \le 2\|X - Y_n\|^2 + 2\|X - Y_m\|^2 - 4d^2,
\]
because \(\frac{Y_n + Y_m}{2} \in H\) (a subspace), so its distance to \(X\) is at least \(d\). The right-hand side tends to \(2d^2 + 2d^2 - 4d^2 = 0\): \((Y_n)\) is Cauchy.

**Step 2: existence.** By completeness \(Y_n \to Y_\ast\) in \(L^2\), and \(Y_\ast \in H\) because \(H\) is closed; by continuity of the norm \(\|X - Y_\ast\| = d\).

**Step 3: minimiser implies orthogonality.** Let \(Y \in H\) and \(t \in \mathbb{R}\). Since \(Y_\ast + tY \in H\), \(\|X - Y_\ast - tY\|^2 \ge \|X - Y_\ast\|^2\), i.e. \(-2t\langle X - Y_\ast, Y\rangle + t^2\|Y\|^2 \ge 0\) for all \(t\). Dividing by \(t > 0\) and letting \(t \to 0^+\) gives \(\langle X - Y_\ast, Y\rangle \le 0\); with \(t < 0\), the reverse inequality. So \(\mathbb{E}[(X - Y_\ast)Y] = 0\).

**Step 4: orthogonality implies minimiser, and uniqueness.** If \(Z \in H\) satisfies \(\langle X - Z, Y\rangle = 0\) for all \(Y \in H\), then for any \(Y \in H\), \(X - Y = (X - Z) + (Z - Y)\) with \(Z - Y \in H\), so Pythagoras gives \(\|X - Y\|^2 = \|X - Z\|^2 + \|Z - Y\|^2 \ge \|X - Z\|^2\), with equality only if \(Y = Z\). Hence the minimiser is unique and equals any \(Z\) with the orthogonality property. Taking \(Y = 0 \in H\) in the same identity gives \(\|X\|^2 = \|X - Z\|^2 + \|Z\|^2\), hence \(\|\pi_H(X)\| \le \|X\|\).

**Step 5: linearity.** \(\pi_H(X) + \pi_H(X')\) lies in \(H\) and \(X + X' - \pi_H(X) - \pi_H(X')\) is orthogonal to \(H\); by uniqueness it is \(\pi_H(X + X')\). Similarly for scalars.

**Why \(L^2(\mathcal{G})\) is closed.** If \(Y_n\) are \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable and \(Y_n \to Y\) in \(L^2\), a subsequence converges a.s. (Proposition 9), and \(\limsup_k Y_{n_k}\) (set to \(0\) where infinite) is a \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable version of \(Y\).

Used next: conditional expectation on \(L^2\) is \(\pi_{L^2(\mathcal{G})}\) (Lesson 3, Theorem 6); Hoffmann's Corollary 8 compares the projection onto a Gaussian subspace with the conditional expectation, and his Remark 9 explains why they coincide only in the Gaussian case.
EXAMPLE:
Let \(H = \{c\mathbf{1}_\Omega : c \in \mathbb{R}\}\), the constants, a closed subspace of dimension \(1\). For \(X \in L^2\), the orthogonality condition reads \(\mathbb{E}[(X - c)\cdot 1] = 0\), so \(\pi_H(X) = \mathbb{E}[X]\), and the decomposition \(\|X\|^2 = \|\pi_HX\|^2 + \|X - \pi_HX\|^2\) is \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] = (\mathbb{E}X)^2 + \mathrm{Var}(X)\): the expectation is the constant closest to \(X\) in mean square. Next, let \(H = \mathrm{span}(1, Y)\) with \(Y \in L^2\) non-constant. The projection of \(X\) is \(a + bY\) with \(\mathbb{E}[X - a - bY] = 0\) and \(\mathbb{E}[(X - a - bY)Y] = 0\), which solve to \(b = \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/\mathrm{Var}(Y)\), \(a = \mathbb{E}X - b\,\mathbb{E}Y\): linear regression, and already the Gaussian conditioning formula of Lesson 4.

KIND: proposition
TITLE: Proposition 6 (Jensen, Cauchy–Schwarz, Hölder, Minkowski — Forien Prop. 7)
IMPORTANCE: imp
HIGHLIGHT: 1
STATEMENT:
1. **(Jensen)** If \(\phi : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) is convex and \(X\) is integrable, then \(\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)]\) is well defined in \((-\infty, +\infty]\) and \(\phi(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[\phi(X)]\).
2. **(Hölder)** For \(1 \le p, q \le \infty\) with \(\frac1p + \frac1q = 1\), \(\mathbb{E}|XY| \le \|X\|_{L^p}\|Y\|_{L^q}\). The case \(p = q = 2\) is **Cauchy–Schwarz**: \(\mathbb{E}|XY| \le \|X\|_{L^2}\|Y\|_{L^2}\).
3. **(Minkowski)** For \(1 \le p \le \infty\), \(\|X + Y\|_{L^p} \le \|X\|_{L^p} + \|Y\|_{L^p}\).
4. For \(1 \le p \le q \le \infty\), \(\|X\|_{L^p} \le \|X\|_{L^q}\).
PROOF:
**Jensen.** Let \(m = \mathbb{E}X\). Convexity gives a supporting line: some \(c\) with \(\phi(x) \ge \phi(m) + c(x - m)\) for all \(x\). Then \(\phi(X) \ge \phi(m) + c(X - m)\), whose right side is integrable, so \(\phi(X)^-\) is integrable; taking expectations, \(\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)] \ge \phi(m)\).

**Hölder.** For \(p = 1\), \(q = \infty\): \(|XY| \le \|Y\|_{L^\infty}|X|\) a.s. For \(1 < p < \infty\), set \(a = \|X\|_{L^p}\), \(b = \|Y\|_{L^q}\), assumed in \((0, \infty)\) (the other cases are trivial). Young's inequality \(uv \le \frac{u^p}{p} + \frac{v^q}{q}\) (convexity of \(\exp\) applied to \(uv = \exp(\frac1p\ln u^p + \frac1q\ln v^q)\)) with \(u = |X|/a\), \(v = |Y|/b\), integrated, gives \(\frac{\mathbb{E}|XY|}{ab} \le \frac1p + \frac1q = 1\).

**Minkowski.** For \(1 < p < \infty\) (the cases \(1, \infty\) are pointwise): \(|X + Y|^p \le |X||X + Y|^{p-1} + |Y||X + Y|^{p-1}\), and Hölder with exponents \(p\), \(q = \frac{p}{p-1}\) gives \(\mathbb{E}|X + Y|^p \le (\|X\|_{L^p} + \|Y\|_{L^p})(\mathbb{E}|X + Y|^p)^{1/q}\); divide by the last factor (finite by \(|X + Y|^p \le 2^{p-1}(|X|^p + |Y|^p)\), and non-zero unless there is nothing to prove).

**Nesting.** Jensen for \(t \mapsto |t|^{q/p}\) applied to \(|X|^p\); for \(q = \infty\), \(|X| \le \|X\|_{L^\infty}\) a.s. \(\square\)
REFRESH:
(none)
EXPLANATION:
These four inequalities are the ones used to bound expectations. Jensen says that a convex function of an average is below the average of the function: \((\mathbb{E}X)^2 \le \mathbb{E}[X^2]\), \(e^{\mathbb{E}X} \le \mathbb{E}[e^X]\), \(|\mathbb{E}X| \le \mathbb{E}|X|\). Hölder and its special case Cauchy–Schwarz control the expectation of a product by norms of the factors, which is how one shows that products of \(L^2\) variables are integrable and that covariances are defined. Minkowski is the triangle inequality of the \(L^p\) norm, and the last point is the nesting of the \(L^p\) spaces on a probability space. All the proofs rest on one idea: a convex function lies above each of its tangent lines.
DEEP:
We want to prove the four inequalities; everything follows from convexity. Plan: Jensen from a supporting line; Hölder from Young's inequality, itself a case of the convexity of the exponential; the rest as consequences.

**Step 1: Jensen.** Let \(m = \mathbb{E}[X]\). A convex \(\phi\) has a supporting line at \(m\): there is \(c \in \mathbb{R}\), any value between the left and right derivatives at \(m\), which exist because the slopes \(\frac{\phi(m + t) - \phi(m)}{t}\) are non-decreasing in \(t\), with \(\phi(x) \ge \phi(m) + c(x - m)\) for all \(x\). At \(x = X(\omega)\), \(\phi(X) \ge \phi(m) + c(X - m)\); the right-hand side is integrable, so the negative part of \(\phi(X)\) is integrable and \(\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)]\) makes sense in \((-\infty, +\infty]\). Taking expectations, \(\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)] \ge \phi(m) + c(\mathbb{E}X - m) = \phi(\mathbb{E}X)\).

**Step 2: Young's inequality.** For \(1 < p < \infty\) and \(u, v > 0\), \(uv = \exp\big(\frac1p\ln u^p + \frac1q\ln v^q\big) \le \frac1pu^p + \frac1qv^q\) by convexity of \(\exp\) with the weights \(\frac1p + \frac1q = 1\).

**Step 3: Hölder.** If \(p = 1\), \(q = \infty\): \(|XY| \le \|Y\|_{L^\infty}|X|\) a.s., take expectations. For \(1 < p < \infty\), set \(a = \|X\|_{L^p}\), \(b = \|Y\|_{L^q}\); if one is \(0\) the corresponding variable vanishes a.s. and both sides are \(0\); if one is infinite there is nothing to prove. Otherwise apply Young to \(u = |X|/a\), \(v = |Y|/b\): \(\frac{|XY|}{ab} \le \frac{|X|^p}{pa^p} + \frac{|Y|^q}{qb^q}\), and taking expectations gives \(\frac{\mathbb{E}|XY|}{ab} \le \frac1p + \frac1q = 1\).

**Step 4: Minkowski.** The cases \(p = 1, \infty\) are the pointwise triangle inequality. For \(1 < p < \infty\), write \(|X + Y|^p \le |X||X + Y|^{p-1} + |Y||X + Y|^{p-1}\) and apply Hölder to each term with exponents \(p\) and \(q = p/(p-1)\), noting \((p-1)q = p\): \(\mathbb{E}|X + Y|^p \le (\|X\|_{L^p} + \|Y\|_{L^p})\,\mathbb{E}[|X + Y|^p]^{1/q}\). If \(\mathbb{E}|X + Y|^p\) is finite (it is, by Step 1 of Definition 4) and non-zero, divide by \(\mathbb{E}[|X + Y|^p]^{1/q}\) and use \(1 - \frac1q = \frac1p\).

**Step 5: nesting.** For \(p \le q < \infty\), apply Jensen to the convex function \(t \mapsto |t|^{q/p}\) and the variable \(|X|^p\): \((\mathbb{E}|X|^p)^{q/p} \le \mathbb{E}|X|^q\). For \(q = \infty\), \(|X| \le \|X\|_{L^\infty}\) a.s.

**Where they are used.** Jensen with \(\phi(x) = x^2\) is \(\mathrm{Var}(X) \ge 0\); with \(\phi = |\cdot|^{q/p}\) it gives the nesting; its conditional version (Lesson 3) gives \(|\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]| \le \mathbb{E}[|X| \mid \mathcal{G}]\) and shows that closed martingales are uniformly integrable. Cauchy–Schwarz defines the scalar product of \(L^2\) and gives \(|\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)| \le \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}X\,\mathrm{Var}Y}\). In Hoffmann's Chapter 1, the nesting is what allows one to pass from the \(L^p\) bounds of Kolmogorov's criterion to conclusions in \(L^1\) and in probability.
EXAMPLE:
Jensen with \(\phi(x) = e^x\) and \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\): \(e^{\mathbb{E}X} = 1 \le \mathbb{E}[e^X] = e^{\sigma^2/2}\), the gap being the correction that appears in \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\sigma B_t}] = e^{\sigma^2t/2}\). Cauchy–Schwarz for the correlation: for \(X, Y \in L^2\) centred, \(|\mathbb{E}[XY]| \le \sqrt{\mathbb{E}X^2\,\mathbb{E}Y^2}\), so \(\rho = \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y) \in [-1, 1]\), and \(\rho = \pm 1\) forces \(Y = \pm\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}X\) a.s. Nesting for \(Z\) standard Gaussian: \(\mathbb{E}|Z| = \sqrt{2/\pi}\), \((\mathbb{E}Z^2)^{1/2} = 1\), \((\mathbb{E}Z^4)^{1/4} = 3^{1/4}\), and indeed \(0.80 \le 1 \le 1.32\), as \(\|Z\|_{L^1} \le \|Z\|_{L^2} \le \|Z\|_{L^4}\) requires.
